Mental Load

What is the mental load?

Shown the same room, people rated the mess identically — and judged Jennifer more harshly than John for it. He sees it. He is not the one who gets blamed for it.

The Dayly Editors Published 4 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 9 min read Ages all
The short answer

Mothers report doing 72.6% of the thinking behind a household — the noticing, planning and remembering — against partners' 27.4%. The physical jobs split 63.6 to 36.4. The thinking is divided more unevenly than the work, and it is the thinking that predicts depression, stress and burnout. Housework, on its own, does not.

There is the reading folder that needs to go back on Wednesday. The shoes that have suddenly become too small. The birthday party on Saturday, for which nobody has bought a present. The dentist who texted three weeks ago and is still somewhere in the back of your mind.

None of these things is particularly difficult. That is partly why they are so easy to underestimate.

The problem is that somebody has to notice them. Somebody has to remember them. Somebody has to know what needs to happen next, and when, and whether anybody else has thought about it yet.

In most families, that somebody is the mother.

Not because anyone decided it. She answered the first school email, so the school has her number. She joined the class group, so she is the one who sees at 22:40 that own-clothes day has moved. She booked the dentist once, and now the dentist texts her.

And once you are the person who knows, being asked is faster than explaining.

So she gets asked.

Where is my kit?

Do I need anything tomorrow?

Did you see the email?

When is the dentist?

She becomes the place the answers live. It is a promotion nobody applied for and there is no title.

He does see the mess

There is a standard explanation for this, and it is worth taking seriously because almost everybody believes some version of it. He does not notice. She is more sensitive to clutter. Their eyes are simply calibrated differently.

In 2019 three sociologists tested it. Sarah Thébaud, Leah Ruppanner and Sabino Kornrich showed people a photograph of a room and asked them to rate how clean it was. Some were told the room belonged to John. Others were told the identical room belonged to Jennifer.

Men and women rated the mess the same.

There is no calibration difference. That theory is simply not true.

What changed was the judgement of the person. The same tidy room was rated as less clean when it was Jennifer’s, and as more likely to make visitors think badly of her (Thébaud, Ruppanner & Kornrich, 2019).

He sees it. He is simply not the one who will be blamed for it.

Which explains a great deal that “he doesn’t notice” never could — including why a father who takes his own children to the park is described as babysitting, and why a mother who does the same thing is described as having gone to the park.

Four stages, and she does the first and the last

Allison Daminger, then at Harvard, spent a long time trying to work out what this labour actually consists of. It is hard to study, because it happens silently inside somebody’s head, and there is no obvious point at which it starts or stops.

In a study of 35 couples she found it breaks into four stages: anticipating what will be needed, working out the options, choosing between them, and then checking that it happened.

Mothers did more at all four. But the pattern within them is the interesting part. Couples usually chose together — the school, the holiday, the club. The anticipating beforehand and the checking afterwards were not shared (Daminger, American Sociological Review, 2019).

So the visible moment looks equal. Two people, a decision, a conversation. Everything around it is one person.

Daminger noticed something else, which is why this so rarely turns into an argument. In the interviews, husbands described their wives as the one “keeping track of more”. Several used the phrase project manager, approvingly, as a compliment. And the imbalance had an explanation ready on both sides: he works longer hours, she is better at planning. Neither explanation gets examined. So neither arrangement gets revisited.

Zach Watson, who now coaches men on exactly this, spent the first decade of his marriage doing more housework than his father ever had. His wife was still drowning. What he eventually worked out, in his own words, was that “he’d been doing the tasks while she carried the load”.

Those are two different jobs, and only one of them has an end.

It is not the housework that is making her ill

This is the part that turns a grievance into a problem.

In 2024 a team led by Elizabeth Aviv and Darby Saxbe surveyed 322 mothers of young children about how their household labour was split. The mothers reported doing 72.6% of the cognitive work — the noticing, planning and remembering — against their partners’ 27.4%.

The physical work split 63.6 to 36.4.

Read those two lines again. The thinking is divided more unevenly than the work. Every household that has carefully shared out the jobs, and cannot understand why one person is still exhausted, is described by the gap between those numbers.

And carrying more of the thinking predicted higher depression, higher stress, more burnout, worse overall mental health and lower satisfaction with the relationship.

Physical housework predicted almost none of that. It was associated with how the relationship was going, and nothing else (Aviv et al., Archives of Women’s Mental Health, 2024).

The washing up is not what is wearing her out. It is knowing about the washing up, at 23:15, along with fourteen other things.

The proof that it is not about being a woman

If this were really about temperament — if some people were simply built to notice and others were not — it would show up wherever two people share a house.

It does not. Research on same-gender couples finds this work divided far more evenly, and divided deliberately: by preference, by who minds least, by what is actually going on that month, and by talking about it rather than letting it settle (*PLOS One*, 2023).

Same amount of household. Same number of dentists. Different result.

Which means this is not a fact about women. It is a fact about what is expected of mothers, and expectations are the one thing in this article that can actually be changed.

What moves it

Not a rota, and not “just tell me what to do” — which is offered generously and takes the doing while handing back the noticing, with checking-up-on-somebody added to it.

What moves is a whole area, permanently, with the anticipating included. Not “can you do the shopping this week” but food is yours: the meals, the list, the order, and the fact that we are out of coffee. It will be done differently, and probably worse for a month. That is what it costs, and it costs less than the arrangement you have.

There is a longer version of this — how to share the mental load, which is a method rather than a principle, and the checklist if you want to see the size of it first.

And a word about what a shared system does, since this is a company that sells one. Putting the family’s information somewhere everyone can see it takes away the second problem in this article: it stops being faster to ask her. It does not touch the first one. No app decides who anticipates. That is a conversation between two adults, and the honest thing to say is that software is only useful after it.

Because the finding that ought to change something is the one in the middle. Two households can share the chores identically, down to the last bin, and only one of them has a person lying awake.

The difference is not the work. It never was.

What to try this week
  1. Write down every area — not task — that keeps a household running. Aim for twenty lines. Do it separately from your partner, then compare.
  2. Pick the one area you would most like to stop holding. Hand over the whole thing, including the noticing.
  3. Agree out loud that it will be done differently, and that you will not correct it for a month.
  4. Give each child one area that is genuinely theirs, with the consequence attached.
  5. Put the areas somewhere everyone can see them, so nobody has to be asked.

Common questions

Is the mental load the same as multitasking?

No. Multitasking is doing several things at once. The mental load is holding responsibility for things that are not happening yet — remembering that they exist, tracking when they will matter, and deciding what to do about them. You can be sitting still and carrying an enormous mental load.

Is this only about mothers?

No, and framing it that way makes it harder to fix. The pattern is structural: whoever becomes the default point of contact accumulates the load. Research does find it lands disproportionately on mothers in different-sex couples, but single parents carry all of it, and in plenty of households it is the father. The useful question is not who is naturally better at it — it is which structure put it all in one place.

How do I explain it to a partner who does not see the problem?

Lists work better than arguments. Write down the areas separately, then compare. Most of the disagreement disappears when both people can see that one column contains twenty items and the other contains six — and the conversation moves on to who takes what.

Can an app fix this?

Not on its own. Software makes responsibility visible and stops it living in one head, which is the necessary condition — but the handover is a decision two adults make, and the standard-lowering is the hard part. Dayly is a place to keep it. It cannot want it for you.
