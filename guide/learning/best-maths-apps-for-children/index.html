The best maths apps for children

Most maths apps are drilling software with animation on top. Some of that is genuinely useful.

The Dayly Editors Published 30 June 2026 Updated 15 August 2026 4 min read Ages 4–16
The short answer

The best maths app for a child depends on what they need: fluency practice, conceptual understanding, or catching up on a specific gap. Short, daily, adaptive practice has better support than long sessions, and no app substitutes for a child being taught something they have not understood.

Before the list, the thing that matters more than which app you pick.

What maths apps are good at, and what they are not

Good at: fluency. Times tables, number bonds, mental arithmetic — the things that need to become automatic so that working memory is free for the actual problem. Short, frequent, adaptive practice is well suited to software, and this is the strongest case for an app.

Not good at: teaching a concept a child has not understood. If a child does not grasp what a fraction is, more fraction questions will not supply it. That is a conversation with a person, and usually with their teacher.

Worth knowing: the practice principles with the best evidence — spacing over days, retrieval rather than review, immediate feedback — are the same on a screen as on paper (Dunlosky et al., 2013). An app is a delivery mechanism for those, not a replacement for them.

What to look for

Adaptive difficulty. It should get harder when they are succeeding and easier when they are not. Fixed-difficulty apps waste the time of both fast and struggling children.

Short sessions. Ten to fifteen minutes daily beats an hour on Sunday, for the same reason spacing beats cramming.

Immediate, specific feedback. Wrong-and-move-on teaches nothing. Wrong-and-here-is-why is the whole value.

A parent view worth having. What they did, what they struggled with — not just a streak count.

Alignment with what school is teaching. Methods differ, and an app teaching a different method from the classroom creates confusion rather than reinforcement.

What to avoid

Anything where the game is the point. If a child can progress through the game without doing much maths, they will, and that is rational.

Streak mechanics that punish a missed day. A child who loses a 60-day streak on holiday frequently stops entirely.

Heavy monetisation aimed at children. Anything with in-app purchases, loot mechanics or advertising is not a learning tool.

Anything you cannot cancel easily. Check the cancellation route before subscribing, not after.

The categories, and what each is for

Fluency drillers — times tables, number facts, mental arithmetic. Best used for ten minutes a day over months. This is where apps genuinely earn their place.

Curriculum-aligned programmes — follow a school syllabus with lessons and practice. Useful for catching up or for a child who wants to work ahead; check which curriculum before subscribing.

Games with maths in them — good for younger children and for keeping a reluctant child engaged. Weaker for actual progress, and worth being clear-eyed about.

Tutoring platforms with software attached — the most expensive category, and the only one that addresses the “does not understand the concept” problem, because there is a person in it.

Try this

Before subscribing to anything, ask your child’s teacher what would actually help. The answer is frequently “more reading” or “times tables, five minutes a day” rather than a subscription — and it is free.

Our honest position

We do not think most families need a maths app. What most children need is short daily practice of the things that should be automatic, and someone to explain the things that are not.

If those two are covered and you want a tool for the first, the criteria above matter far more than the brand. If the second is the problem, no app on the market solves it well.

What to try this week
  1. Ask the teacher what would actually help before subscribing to anything.
  2. If you use an app, cap it at fifteen minutes and use it daily rather than in long sessions.
  3. Check whether the app teaches the same method the school does.
  4. Check the cancellation route before you subscribe.

Common questions

Are maths apps better than a tutor?

They serve different purposes. Apps are good and cheap at fluency practice. A tutor addresses understanding, which is what most struggling children actually need. If money is limited and the problem is comprehension, a few hours with a person beats a year of subscription.
