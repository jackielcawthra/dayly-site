How to teach children about consequences

The most useful consequences are the ones you did not have to invent.

The Dayly Editors Published 29 April 2026 Updated 10 August 2026 4 min read
The short answer

Let natural consequences happen wherever they are safe and proportionate — the forgotten kit, the cold hands, the late homework — and say as little as possible while they do. Reserve imposed consequences for situations with no natural one, make them known in advance, and keep them small.

There are two kinds, and conflating them is why this advice so often fails.

A natural consequence is what would have happened anyway. No coat, cold. No kit, no swimming. No homework, a conversation with the teacher.

An imposed consequence is something you add. No tablet. Lost pocket money. An earlier bedtime.

Natural consequences teach about the world. Imposed consequences teach about you — which is sometimes necessary and always more expensive, because it puts you in the frame as the source of the unpleasantness.

Why natural ones work better

  • They are impersonal. There is nothing to argue with. The pool did not have an opinion.
  • They are immediate and legible. The link between action and result is obvious.
  • They cost you no authority. You are not the punisher; you are the person who is sympathetic
  • about it.
  • They generalise. A child who learns that forgetting has costs applies it to new situations. A
  • child who learns that you take the tablet away learns to manage you.

The three-question filter

Before letting one land, check:

  1. Is it safe? No consequence involving danger, health, or anything genuinely irreversible.
  2. Is it proportionate? A missed swimming session, yes. A missed exam, no. A ruined school trip
  3. the whole class has looked forward to, no.
  4. Does it fall on them? If your child forgetting means a teacher is inconvenienced or a sibling
  5. misses out, that is not a natural consequence for them, it is a cost passed to someone else.

If all three pass, do nothing. Doing nothing is the technique.

Worth knowing

Doing nothing is harder than it sounds. The urge to add commentary — “well, if you’d listened” — is strong and it converts a natural consequence into an imposed one, because now the unpleasant part is you. The whole method rests on keeping your mouth shut and being kind about it.

When you do need to impose one

Some things have no useful natural consequence, or the natural one arrives far too late: being unkind to a sibling, treating a room badly, screen time that eats an evening.

Three rules that keep imposed consequences effective:

Known in advance. A consequence invented in anger is a punishment. One agreed at a calm moment is a rule, and rules are easier to accept.

Small. Effectiveness comes from certainty, not severity. A consequence that reliably happens beats a dramatic one that happens once.

Connected. As close to the behaviour as you can get. Screen time lost for screen-time overruns. Cleaning up what was made worse. Arbitrary consequences produce compliance without learning.

What about very young children?

Under about five, the link between action and later result is unreliable, so natural consequences mostly do not teach — they just produce an upset child and a confused one. At that age, structure does the work: the same order every day, choices between two acceptable options, and immediate, concrete responses.

The long view

The purpose is not obedience. It is that a child develops an internal model — if I do this, that tends to happen — and starts running it before acting rather than after.

That model is built from hundreds of small, survivable outcomes. Which means every time you prevent one, you have removed a data point. Occasionally that is the right call. Routinely, and there is nothing to learn from.

What to try this week
  1. Notice one consequence you would normally prevent, and let it happen.
  2. Say nothing afterwards except that it was annoying.
  3. Write down the two or three imposed consequences you actually use, and check they are known in advance.
  4. Make any of them that are dramatic smaller and more certain instead.

Common questions

Is this the same as "logical consequences"?

Related. "Logical consequences" usually means an imposed consequence designed to connect to the behaviour — cleaning up a mess you made. That is the middle ground between natural and arbitrary, and it is a reasonable tool when nothing natural is available.
