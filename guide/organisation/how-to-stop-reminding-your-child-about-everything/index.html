How to stop reminding your child about everything

Every reminder you give is a small promise that you will give the next one.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 4 min read Ages 5–17
The short answer

Replace verbal reminders with something external the child can check — a list, a routine, a place by the door — then move from reminding to asking ("what does your list say?") and finally to silence. Expect a fortnight of things going wrong; that fortnight is the transfer.

Count them, one morning.

Most parents of primary-age children are somewhere between fifteen and forty verbal prompts before nine o’clock.

Get up. Get dressed. Teeth. Breakfast. No, not that. Bag. Shoes. Coat. Have you got. Did you remember. We’re going. Now. Now.

Every one of them is reasonable.

Together, they are a system. And the system is you.

Why reminders keep needing to happen

A reminder does two things.

It gets the thing done, which is why you keep doing it.

And it removes any need for the child to have noticed — which is why you keep needing to.

There is nothing wrong with their memory. There is simply a more reliable memory in the room, running for free.

No organism uses an expensive process when a cheap one is available.

The tell: your child does not know what they have to do today. But they know that you know.

What to put in their place

You cannot just subtract the reminders. Something has to hold the information.

Step one — get it out of your head.

Everything you currently remind about goes somewhere visible. A list by the door. A routine on the wall. A calendar they can read. A shared app with their own view of it.

Do this first, for at least a week, while still reminding exactly as normal. Nothing else changes yet.

Step two — turn reminders into questions.

For two weeks, do not say the thing. Point at the system instead.

Instead ofSay
“Have you got your reading folder?”“Have you checked your list?”
“It’s swimming today”“What day is it? What does that mean?”
“Put your shoes on”“What’s next?”
“Your homework is due Thursday”“When’s that due? What’s your plan?”

This feels artificial for about four days. Then it becomes normal.

Children stop resenting it quickly, because a question is not a criticism.

Step three — go quiet.

Stop asking, too.

Say once, at the start of the week, that you are not going to be mentioning things any more. Then do not.

Worth knowing

Step three is where the forgetting happens. That is what it is for.

And if nothing gets forgotten in the first fortnight, you are almost certainly still prompting in some smaller way. A glance at the bag. A “got everything?” A sigh.

What to do about the fallout

Some things are going to be forgotten.

Sort them into two piles in advance, so that you are not deciding at 07:48.

Let it land: kit, snacks, library books, a coat on a mild day, homework once.

Step in: anything unsafe. Anything with a consequence out of all proportion — an exam, a school trip, a performance the whole class is depending on, medication.

Say the sympathy. Skip the lecture.

“That’s annoying” is a complete sentence.

The teenage version

The same, with one addition.

Teenagers need to be told what you are doing and why. Otherwise the reminders stopping reads as your interest stopping.

“You’re old enough to run your own week now. I’m going to stop reminding you about things. If you want help setting something up, ask — but I’m not going to be the alarm any more.”

Then hold it.

The dip is real. It usually lasts a few weeks.

And the alternative is a seventeen-year-old who cannot get themselves to a dentist appointment.

The part nobody mentions

Reminding is a way of managing your own anxiety.

The prompt is not really for them. It is so that you can stop thinking about it.

Which means going quiet involves carrying that discomfort yourself for a while.

It gets easier at roughly the same rate they get better. That is not a coincidence.

What to try this week
  1. Count your prompts on one ordinary morning. Just count — do not change anything.
  2. Put everything you prompted about onto one visible list.
  3. Spend two weeks converting reminders into questions.
  4. Then go quiet, and decide in advance which forgotten things you will let land.

Common questions

Will my child just fail at everything for a while?

Some things, briefly. Most families see a rough fortnight followed by a noticeably better month, because the child starts building their own prompts — a bag by the door, a note, a check after dinner. If it is still chaotic after a month, the external system probably is not visible or specific enough.

What about children who genuinely struggle with memory?

They need more external structure, not more reminders from you — because a person-prompt cannot be checked at will and a list can. For children with ADHD in particular, visible, consistent, in-the-environment cues generally work better than verbal ones, and it is worth getting specific advice for your child rather than relying on general guidance.

Is not reminding them the same as not caring?

Say the opposite out loud, because it is not obvious from the outside. You are still available, still interested, still willing to help build a system. What you are withdrawing is the running commentary, not the attention.
