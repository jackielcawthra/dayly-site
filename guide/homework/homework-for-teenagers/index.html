Homework and teenagers: what changes

At secondary the evidence is stronger, the stakes are higher, and your role gets smaller. All three at once.

The Dayly Editors Published 2 July 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read Ages 13–18
The short answer

For teenagers, homework has a better evidence base and a real effect on attainment — but parental control becomes counterproductive. Shift to protecting conditions: a phone-free place, food, sleep, help with planning, and interest in the subject rather than surveillance of the task.

Three things change at once, which is why what worked at nine stops working at fourteen.

The evidence gets stronger. At secondary level, homework is associated with meaningfully better attainment — around five months’ additional progress on the EEF’s summary. It matters more than it did.

Your leverage gets weaker. Control is counterproductive at every age and especially now.

Sleep becomes a real variable. Adolescent body clocks shift later, school start times do not, and teenagers are chronically short of sleep. An hour of revision at 23:30 usually costs more than it buys.

What to keep doing

Protect the conditions. A place to work that is not their bed. Food. A phone that is in another room — the effect of a phone on the desk is large enough to be worth a fight, and it is the one worth having.

Help with planning. Most teenagers will accept planning help long after they have stopped accepting content help. Deadlines, steps, working backwards.

Be interested in the subject. “What are you doing in history?” lands very differently from “have you done your history?” One is interest, the other is surveillance, and they can tell.

Talk about how they study, not just whether. Most teenagers reread and highlight, which are the two lowest-value techniques available. Retrieval and spacing are worth explaining properly — the mechanism is genuinely interesting and it is one of the few things you can offer that their friends will not have.

What to stop doing

  • Checking their work for correctness
  • Setting the schedule — offer to help build one, then let it be theirs
  • Adding pressure to a system that is usually applying plenty already
  • Comparing them to a sibling or a friend, ever
  • Trading sleep for revision

Exam periods

Three things carry disproportionate weight:

A realistic revision plan, made by them, ideally weeks out, with topics rather than subjects on it.

Retrieval, not rereading. Past papers, closed-book recall, teach-it-to-me. Highlighting a textbook is the study equivalent of tidying your desk.

Sleep, food and some exercise. Dull advice, well supported, and consistently the first thing abandoned.

Worth knowing

If a teenager is genuinely not working at all, the useful question is what is actually going on — anxiety, feeling hopeless about a subject, something social, or simply not knowing how to start. The answer is rarely more pressure, and it is often a conversation with the school.

The honest bit

At sixteen or seventeen, you cannot make someone work. What you can do is keep the conditions good, keep the relationship intact, and stay the person they will tell when it is going badly.

That last one is worth more, over the whole of a school career, than any amount of enforcement.

What to try this week
  1. Move the phone out of the room during study. Hold that one.
  2. Ask about the subject, not the task, three times this week.
  3. Explain retrieval practice and spacing, properly, once.
  4. Protect sleep in exam weeks, even at the cost of revision hours.

Common questions

How much revision should a teenager do?

Frequency and method matter far more than hours. Short, spaced, retrieval-based sessions across several weeks beat long blocks close to the exam, and the research on this is unusually consistent.

Sources & further reading

  2. Dunlosky, J. et al. (2013). Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques
