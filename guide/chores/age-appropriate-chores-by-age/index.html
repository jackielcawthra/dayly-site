Chores

Age-appropriate chores, from 3 to 17

Most lists are either too ambitious or too cautious. This one assumes your child is normal and your week is busy.

The Dayly Editors Published 29 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 3 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

Three-year-olds can carry, sort and put away; six-year-olds can lay a table, feed a pet and manage their own kit; nine-year-olds can own a room and cook something simple; twelve-year-olds can run their own washing and cook a family meal; sixteen-year-olds can run a household area unsupervised. Readiness is about whether the last job is automatic, not about age.

Two warnings before the list.

Most published chore charts are too ambitious for the young ages — a four-year-old is not “cleaning the bathroom” — and far too cautious about the teenagers. A fifteen-year-old can absolutely cook dinner for five.

And the number of jobs matters much less than whether the last one has become automatic.

3–5

By age
DailyToys into a named box. Plate to the counter. Shoes on the shelf. Coat on a low hook.
WeeklyPutting socks together. Watering a plant. Wiping a low surface with a damp cloth.
With an adultLaying part of the table. Feeding a pet. Carrying laundry to the machine.

Expect it to be slower with them than without them.

That is not the arrangement failing. That is the arrangement.

You are buying the identity, not the labour.

6–8

By age
DailyLaying and clearing the table. Their own bag packed the night before. Bed pulled straight. Clothes in the basket.
WeeklyEmptying a dishwasher rack. Watering the plants. Tidying their room to an agreed standard. Taking the recycling out.
New this stageOne job that is genuinely theirs and does not rotate. Feeding a pet alone. Making their own breakfast.

This is the age when a visible list starts doing the work instead of your voice.

9–11

By age
DailyTheir own kit sorted after training. Emptying the dishwasher. Their own room to a known standard.
WeeklyOne area of the house — the hallway, the downstairs loo, the shoe rack. Hoovering. Changing their own bed.
New this stageCooking one simple meal with supervision. Loading the washing machine. Putting the bins out on the right day, without being told the day.

This is the window that matters most.

Most of the daily reminding can stop here — but only if the jobs are owned rather than assigned.

12–14

By age
DailyTheir own washing basket to the machine. Kitchen reset after they have eaten. Their own room.
WeeklyA cooked meal for the family. A proper share of the cleaning. Food shop with a list.
New this stageWashing start to finish, including putting it away. Cleaning a bathroom properly. Looking after a younger sibling for an hour.

Expect competence and inconsistency in the same fortnight.

Both are completely normal at this age.

15–17

By age
DailyEverything to do with themselves, without supervision and without reminding.
WeeklyA household area run to a standard, unsupervised. Meals planned and cooked. Being useful in a busy week without being asked.
New this stageNoticing what needs doing, and doing it.

That last line is the whole point.

And it arrives late in most households, usually because nobody ever actually handed over the noticing.

How to know when to add the next one

Three signals.

You have not mentioned the current job for three weeks.

They have adapted it to suit themselves.

Or they complain that the whole system is unnecessary, because they already know.

All three are promotion requests.

If you are starting late

Do not start at their age.

Start two bands below, get it automatic, then move up quickly. Most children catch up inside a term.

Starting a fourteen-year-old on the fourteen-year-old list usually buys you a fortnight of conflict and a return to exactly how things were.

What to try this week
  1. Find the band your child is actually at, not the one their age suggests.
  2. Add exactly one job, owned and named.
  3. Check the previous job is genuinely automatic before adding anything.
  4. Let them choose from a shortlist rather than assigning.

Common questions

How long should chores take?

Ten to fifteen minutes a day for primary-age children, twenty to thirty for teenagers, plus something larger at the weekend. Beyond that you are into territory where school, sleep and being a child start to suffer.
