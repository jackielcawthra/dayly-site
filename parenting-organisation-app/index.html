What you are actually carrying

Not the tasks. The knowing: which child needs what on which day, that the shoes are too small, that the passport expires in June, that the dentist never got rebooked, that Thursday is going to be difficult.

None of it has a deadline until the morning it does. All of it lives in one head. It is invisible until it fails, and then it is your fault.

Three things change in the first month

It stops living in your head. The week, the jobs, the kit days, the recurring things — somewhere both adults and every child can see without asking you.

Responsibilities get owners. Not tasks handed out each day. Areas, held by people, including the noticing. That is the version that can actually be given away.

The children take some of it. Their kit, their week, their area, their deadlines — not as a favour to you, as practice they need anyway.

The screen that starts the conversation

The Everyone view shows who owns what across the household, including the areas nobody has claimed — which are usually the ones causing the arguments.

It is hard to argue about a division of labour in the abstract. It is much easier to look at a list together and move four things.

Doing it alone

Single parents carry all of it and no division fixes that. What still helps: getting it out of your head, because the failure mode of a solo load is that one forgotten thing takes the week with it. And moving responsibilities to the children earlier than typical, deliberately rather than in a crisis.

Common questions

How long does setup take?

About an hour, and you do not have to finish in one sitting. Most families put in the week and the jobs first, and add school, travel and money later.

What if my partner will not use it?

Start with what benefits them — their own areas, and not having to ask you things. An app nobody else opens has not fixed anything, so this is worth being honest about early. The mental load calculator, done separately, is usually a better opening than a new app.
