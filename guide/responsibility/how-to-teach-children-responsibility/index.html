Responsibility

How to teach children responsibility

A child who packs a swimming bag because you reminded them has completed a task. The difference is not in the bag. It is in who is carrying the thinking.

The Dayly Editors Published 12 February 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 10 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

Responsibility is not only being able to do something. It is seeing that it needs doing, working out what to do, and taking care of it without somebody else carrying the thought first. Children get there by having areas that genuinely belong to them, by being given information rather than instructions, and by being allowed to experience manageable consequences.

Responsibility is one of those things parents say they want their children to develop, but it is surprisingly easy to teach the opposite.

We remind them to pack their bags, tell them when it is time to get dressed, ask whether they have done their homework, check that they have taken their sports kit and eventually become frustrated because we seem to be doing all the remembering ourselves.

The child may be perfectly capable of doing every one of these things. They simply have no reason to notice that they need doing, because someone else already does.

That is the part of responsibility that is easiest to miss. It is not only the ability to perform a task. It is the ability to see that something needs doing, work out what to do, and take care of it without somebody else carrying the thought first.

A child who packs a swimming bag because you reminded them has completed a task. A child who knows that swimming is tomorrow, checks what they need and packs the bag before bed has taken responsibility for something. The difference is not really in the bag. It is in who is carrying the thinking.

Children become responsible by being part of things

Jesper Juul) wrote about children as equal members of the family, while also making an important distinction between equality and treating children as though they were adults. Children need guidance and boundaries, but they also need genuine participation. They need to experience that their actions matter to other people and that they have something to contribute.

That begins very early.

A three-year-old can carry their plate to the kitchen. A five-year-old can help put away the shopping. A seven-year-old can take increasing responsibility for their school things. None of these jobs is necessary because the family could not manage without the child’s contribution. An adult could obviously do them faster.

The point is that the child is learning that this is their home too, and that being part of a family involves contributing to it. That is different from helping Mum because Mum has asked.

Give information instead of carrying the whole thought

A lot of everyday parenting sounds like this:

“Put your shoes away.” “Have you packed your bag?” “Don’t forget your water bottle.” “Your room needs tidying.”

The words work in the moment, but they also keep the parent responsible for noticing what needs to happen and telling the child.

One of the practical ideas in Adele Faber and Elaine Mazlish’s How to Talk So Kids Will Listen & Listen So Kids Will Talk is to describe what you see or give information rather than immediately issuing an order. Their approach is intended to encourage cooperation while giving children more room to think and act for themselves.

So instead of “Put your shoes away”, you might say, “There are shoes by the door.” Instead of “You need to pack your swimming bag”, you might say, “Swimming is tomorrow.”

Sometimes that is enough. Sometimes it is not, and a child still needs a clear instruction. The point is not to find a clever phrase that makes children obey. It is to avoid doing all of the thinking for them when they are capable of doing some of it themselves.

As children get older, that distinction becomes increasingly important. If every problem arrives already translated into an instruction, the parent remains responsible for noticing the problem, remembering it and deciding what the child should do about it. The child can become very good at following instructions without becoming particularly good at managing their own life.

Responsibility grows gradually

Responsibility does not appear all at once. A child first helps with something, then learns to do it, then learns to remember it, and eventually begins to notice it without being prompted. The stages overlap, and they vary enormously between children, but the direction matters.

A child who clears the table when asked has learned to respond to a request. A child who notices that everyone has finished eating and starts clearing the table has begun to understand the responsibility itself.

This is why simply adding more chores does not necessarily make children more responsible. A longer list can just give them more things to wait to be told to do. The more useful question is whether the thinking is gradually moving with the task.

Let children experience manageable consequences

This is one of the harder parts of parenting because adults know what will happen when a child forgets something. They forget their PE kit and we know they will be embarrassed. They leave their homework at home and we know they may be in trouble. They forget their coat and we know they will be cold.

So we rescue them.

Sometimes that is exactly what a parent should do. Children are still learning, and there are situations where the consequences are too serious or simply unnecessary. But when the consequence is manageable, allowing it to happen can teach something that another reminder cannot.

A forgotten swimming bag can be information. The child learns that swimming requires a bag, that the bag needs to be prepared and that tomorrow’s requirements are easier to deal with the night before. They may need help thinking through what happened, but the parent does not need to turn the experience into a lecture.

Faber and Mazlish similarly emphasise alternatives to punishment that help children take responsibility for their actions, including allowing appropriate consequences and helping them make amends.

The consequence belongs to the responsibility.

Don’t take responsibility back because it isn’t done your way

There is another reason this process takes time.

When responsibility is handed over, the child will almost certainly do some things differently from you. Their room may not look the way you would arrange it. Their school bag may be packed in an order that makes no sense to you. They may fold clothes badly, load the dishwasher strangely or make a meal that you would have prepared differently.

If the standard is exactly how you would do it, ownership becomes difficult.

The child needs enough freedom to develop their own way of doing things, provided the important part is being taken care of. This is true between adults too. A family cannot really share responsibility if one person remains the final inspector of everybody else’s work.

There will be things that genuinely matter. There will also be things that only matter because they are different from the way you would have done them. Knowing the difference is part of letting responsibility move.

Give children things that are genuinely theirs

The most useful responsibilities are not random jobs handed out because it is Saturday morning. They are areas of life that belong to the child.

Their school things. Their sports equipment. Their room. Their laundry. Later, parts of the household.

A child who is responsible for their football equipment needs to know when training is, what they need, where the kit is, whether it is clean and what to do when something is missing. That is a much richer responsibility than being told to put the football bag by the door.

It is also why 100:100 makes more sense than 50:50. The aim is not to divide family life into equal numbers of chores. It is for each person to have areas they genuinely own, with the thinking included. For a young child, that ownership will be small. As they grow, it should become larger.

Help them see themselves differently

There is another useful idea in Faber and Mazlish’s work: children can become trapped by the descriptions other people give them. The messy one. The forgetful one. The irresponsible one. Once a child is repeatedly treated as that person, it becomes surprisingly easy for everyone to behave as though the description is a fact.

Responsibility gives parents an opportunity to show children another picture of themselves.

Instead of saying, “You’re always forgetting your things,” you can notice the specific thing they did manage to remember. Instead of “You’re so responsible,” which turns responsibility into a judgement about who they are, you can describe what actually happened: “You checked tomorrow’s timetable and realised you needed your trainers.” Faber and Mazlish recommend this kind of descriptive acknowledgement rather than broad evaluation.

The child begins to see the connection between their actions and the person they are becoming.

The real test is whether you still have to remember

There is a useful way to tell whether responsibility has actually moved. Ask yourself whether you are still carrying it.

If you are responsible for remembering that your child needs their swimming kit, reminding them to pack it, checking whether they packed it and rescuing them when they forgot, then the responsibility is still yours, even if the child is technically the one carrying the bag.

This is why responsibility can take years to develop. A child needs repeated opportunities to notice, decide, act and sometimes get things wrong. The parent’s role gradually changes from doing and reminding to supporting and stepping back.

The aim is not to remove every difficulty from a child’s path, nor to make them manage everything before they are ready. It is to give them increasingly real opportunities to contribute, make decisions, solve problems and take care of the things that belong to them.

Eventually, there is less for you to remember.

More importantly, there is more that they know how to carry for themselves.

What to try this week
  1. Pick one thing that is currently yours and should be theirs. Small — one object, one moment.
  2. Have the handover conversation out loud, including the sentence about not reminding them.
  3. Put it somewhere they can see it without asking you.
  4. When it fails — and it will, in about a fortnight — let the consequence land and ask what they would change.
  5. Wait three weeks before adding the next one.

Common questions

What age should children start having responsibilities?

From about three, at the scale of a single object — a plate to the counter, shoes on a shelf. The point at that age is not the help, it is that participating in the household is normal. The scale grows; the principle does not change.

What if my child simply refuses?

Check three things first: is the responsibility too big, is it visible to them without you, and is the consequence real? Refusal is often a design problem wearing a defiance costume. If all three check out, hold the line calmly and let the consequence happen — but do not turn it into a battle you have to win in public.

Is it fair to let a child fail on purpose?

You are not engineering a failure; you are declining to prevent one. There is a limit — you do not let a child miss an exam or arrive somewhere unsafe. Between "no consequence at all" and "consequences that genuinely damage something", there is a wide band of forgotten kit and cold hands, and that band is where children learn.

How is this different from chores?

A chore is a task you are told to do. A responsibility includes noticing that it needs doing. Both are useful; only the second one builds independence. The distinction, in detail.
