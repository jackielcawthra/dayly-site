How Dayly works

Stop being the only one who knows what’s happening.

A planned day, not a list

A to-do list with eleven things on it and a day with four hours in it are different objects, and family software usually pretends otherwise.

In Dayly, jobs carry a duration and either their own time or a window. The day is laid out around the fixed points — waking, the leave time, training, dinner, bedtime — and when the pieces do not fit, it says so.

That single behaviour changes the conversation. Instead of arriving at nine in the evening having failed at a list, you find out at eight in the morning that Wednesday has forty minutes too much in it, and you can decide what goes.

The week

Every person’s commitments in one view, with the two points where it genuinely does not work made obvious. Most weeks have exactly two, and both usually have an easy answer once they are named — a lift, a different meal, a thing moved.

Planning with the children in the room

Children who can see the week understand why Wednesday is rushed, and they can take parts of it: their own kit, their own deadlines, their own clash. From about eleven, this is where they start doing their own version.

The weekly reset

Thirty minutes, one fixed slot: look at the week, sort the kit, plan the food, reset the house, tell everyone what is coming. It is the routine that stops a week compounding, and Dayly holds it as a list you work through rather than something to remember.

Common questions

Is this a to-do app?

Not really. To-do apps assume tasks that can be done at any point. Family life is mostly recurring, owned and time-bound, and it involves people who are not always in the house.

Does it work if our week is different every week?

Yes — presence rules handle who is where, including by half-day, and nothing falls due on a day its owner is away.
