Independence

How to give children more independence without losing control

Independence is granted in specific, practised increments — not as a general policy.

The Dayly Editors Published 24 April 2026 Updated 14 August 2026 3 min read Ages 6–17
The short answer

Grant independence as specific, practised steps rather than as a general permission: rehearse the situation, agree what to do if it goes wrong, start with a short version, and widen it when it goes well. Separate genuine risks from your own discomfort, and plan around the first.

“More independence” as a general policy produces anxiety in parents and confusion in children. What works is specific: this journey, this shop, this hour, this decision.

The four-step grant

1. Rehearse it. Do it together first. Walk the route, make the phone call together, go into the shop with them. Talk about what could go wrong while you are there.

2. Agree the failure plan. What do you do if the bus does not come, if you cannot find it, if someone talks to you, if you are running late? A child with a plan is a child who does not freeze. This conversation does more for safety than any restriction.

3. Do the short version. One stop, not four. Ten minutes, not an hour. The corner shop, not the town centre.

4. Widen it when it goes well. And it usually goes well, which is worth noticing out loud.

Risk versus discomfort

Worth separating deliberately, because they feel identical from the inside.

A risk is something with a real probability of real harm: traffic, water, an unfamiliar road at night. Plan around these specifically and do not talk yourself out of them.

A discomfort is your own unease at not knowing where they are or what they are doing. Real, and not the same thing. Most restrictions on children’s independence are responses to the second while being described as responses to the first.

The test: if I knew nothing would go wrong, would I still say no? If yes, it is a rule about something else. If no, it is discomfort, and it is worth sitting with rather than acting on.

Staying in touch without surveillance

There is a middle ground between no contact and live location tracking.

Agreed check-ins. “Text when you get there and when you leave.” Simple, respectful, and it teaches the habit of letting people know.

Location sharing, agreed rather than imposed. Many families use it; the difference between a reasonable arrangement and a corrosive one is whether the young person agreed to it and whether it is used to check up on them constantly.

Not: tracking without their knowledge. If it is discovered — and it is usually discovered — the cost to trust is much larger than whatever was gained.

Worth knowing

The purpose of contact is to know they are safe, not to know what they are doing. Independence that comes with constant monitoring is not independence; it is a longer lead.

When it goes wrong

It will, in small ways. The child gets the wrong bus, forgets to text, comes home late.

What matters is the response. A large reaction to a small failure teaches a child to hide things, which is the outcome that actually creates danger. A calm response — what happened, what will you do next time — keeps them telling you things, which is the single most protective factor you have.

The teenage negotiation

From about thirteen, independence becomes something negotiated rather than granted. What helps:

  • Say what the boundary is for. “Home by ten because I need to sleep and I cannot sleep until you
  • are in” is a reason. “Because I said so” is a fight.
  • Give ground somewhere real so that the things you hold have credibility.
  • Widen it on a schedule, out loud. “When you are fifteen we will look at this again.” Predictable
  • expansion removes most of the pressure.
  • Distinguish safety from taste. Hair, clothes, music and bedroom state are not safety.
What to try this week
  1. Pick one specific thing to grant, not a general policy.
  2. Rehearse it together and agree the failure plan out loud.
  3. Do the short version first.
  4. Ask yourself, for one restriction, whether it is a risk or a discomfort.
