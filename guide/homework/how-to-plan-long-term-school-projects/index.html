How to help children plan long-term school projects

Set on the 1st, remembered on the 27th. The fix happens on the 1st.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 June 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read Ages 9–18
The short answer

On the day a project is set, break it into three to six concrete steps, estimate each one, and put each on a specific day working backwards from the deadline with slack at the end. The failure is almost always decomposition, not motivation.

The pattern is universal: set on the 1st, due on the 28th, started on the 27th, finished by a parent at eleven o’clock at night.

It is not a motivation problem. A four-week project contains no natural trigger — nothing happens on the 2nd to make you start — and “do a project on the rainforest” contains no first move.

The method

Do this on the day it is set, in ten minutes, at the kitchen table.

1. Read the brief together. What is actually being asked for? What form does it take? How is it marked? Most children have not read past the title.

2. List the steps. Three to six. Concrete, each with a visible end.

3. Estimate each one. They will be wrong; do it anyway. Estimating improves with practice and it makes the total visible.

4. Work backwards from the deadline. Put each step on a specific day, and stop two days before the due date. Those two days are for the thing that always goes wrong.

5. Write it where it will be seen. A calendar, a list, a shared app. A plan that lives in somebody’s memory is not a plan.

Try this

For a rainforest project due on the 28th:

  • Choose the country — 15 min — Mon 4th
  • Find three sources — 30 min — Wed 6th
  • Notes on climate and animals — 20 min — Mon 11th
  • Write it up — 40 min — Thu 14th
  • Read it aloud to someone and fix it — 10 min — Sun 17th
  • Spare days: 26th and 27th

Total: under two hours, spread over a fortnight, finished eleven days early.

Why the slack matters

Something always goes wrong: the printer, the library book, an illness, a step that turns out to be three steps. A plan that finishes on the deadline is a plan that misses the deadline.

Finishing early is also the single most persuasive experience for a child who does not believe in planning. One project finished on the 17th, calmly, does more to change their behaviour than a year of advice.

Handing it over

By age
9–11You run the session. They contribute the steps. You write it down.
12–14They run it, you ask the questions: what are the steps, how long, which day.
15+They do it. You ask once whether they have a plan, and then leave it alone.

When they will not plan

Some children resist the process itself, and the honest answer is that one late, panicked project is a decent teacher — provided the consequence lands on them and not on you at eleven at night.

Which means the hard part is yours: not rescuing it. Offering help at 21:00 the night before is what makes the strategy of not planning viable.

What to try this week
  1. On the next project set, do the ten-minute planning session the same day.
  2. Estimate every step, however badly.
  3. Work backwards and leave two spare days.
  4. Put the steps somewhere visible, not in a conversation.

Common questions

What if the school does not tell us about projects until late?

Ask for the deadlines at the start of term — most secondary schools publish an assessment calendar and many primaries will tell you if asked. Then put the deadline in your own calendar with a planning session a day or two after it is set.
