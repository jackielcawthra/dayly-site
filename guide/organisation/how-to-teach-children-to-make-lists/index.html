How to teach children to make and use lists

Most children’s lists fail for the same three reasons, and all of them are fixable.

The Dayly Editors Published 3 June 2026 Updated 8 August 2026 3 min read Ages 5–15
The short answer

A usable child’s list is short (five or six lines), written in their words, kept where the task happens, and triggered by something that already occurs in the day. Long, tidy, adult-written lists fail almost universally.

A list is the first piece of external memory a child can own outright, and it is worth teaching properly rather than assuming it is obvious. It is not obvious. Most adults are bad at it.

Why children’s lists fail

Too long. Fifteen lines is a wall. Nobody reads a wall. Five or six lines is a list.

Written by an adult. In adult words, in adult order, with adult categories. It reads as instructions, which is a different thing entirely, and it gets treated accordingly.

Not where the task is. A list in a drawer, on a fridge in a different room, in an app they have to unlock. A list has to be visible at the moment it is needed.

No trigger. Nothing in the day says “look at the list now”. This is the biggest one.

How to make one that works

  1. They write it. Even if it is misspelled, out of order and includes the dog. Their handwriting
  2. turns it into their list.
  3. Five or six lines. If the task genuinely has more steps, split it into two lists at different
  4. moments in the day.
  5. Verbs and objects. “Water bottle”, not “hydration”. “Feed rabbit”, not “pet care”.
  6. Put it where it happens. Bag list by the bag. Morning list by the bathroom door. Job list in
  7. the room.
  8. Attach a trigger. After dinner. After teeth. When you come down. The trigger is what turns a
  9. list into a habit.
Try this

Ask them to write the list from memory first, without help. What they miss is exactly what the list is for — and it makes the case for having one far better than you can.

Ticking things off

Underrated. Finishing a visible list is genuinely satisfying, and for children the tick is a large part of the appeal. Give them something to tick: a box, a whiteboard pen, a laminated card, an app.

Avoid the temptation to convert this into a reward scheme with points and prizes. The tick is doing the work. Adding a prize changes what the activity is about and tends not to last.

Progression

By age
5–7Pictures they drew. Three steps. An adult points at it.
8–10Written, five lines, theirs, in one place, with a trigger.
11–13Two or three lists for different parts of the day. They rewrite them when they stop fitting.
14+A notes app, a diary, or something they invented and will not explain. Leave it alone.

The last stage is the goal, and the sign of it is that they defend their system from your improvements.

What to try this week
  1. Replace one list you wrote with one they write.
  2. Cut it to six lines.
  3. Move it to where the task actually happens.
  4. Give it a trigger — something that already happens every day.

Common questions

Paper or app?

Under about eleven, paper on a wall usually wins, because it is always visible and needs no decision to open. Older children with their own phone and a complicated week often do better with something shared, so that clubs, deadlines and family plans are all in the same place.
