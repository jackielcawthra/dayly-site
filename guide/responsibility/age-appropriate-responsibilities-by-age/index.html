Age-appropriate responsibilities, from 3 to 17

A list by age, plus the question that matters more than age: is the last one automatic yet?

The Dayly Editors Published 19 February 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 5 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

Children can own a single object and a single job from about three, their own school bag and one household area by seven, their own washing and week by twelve, and their own appointments, revision and packing by fifteen. Readiness is better judged by whether the previous responsibility has become automatic than by the number of years.

Age lists are useful and slightly misleading. Useful, because most parents underestimate what children can do. Misleading, because readiness is not really about age.

The better test: has the previous responsibility become automatic — three weeks without you mentioning it? If yes, add. If no, wait, or make the current one smaller.

Three columns run through every age: what they look after for themselves, what they do for the household, and what they organise — the part most lists leave out and the part that actually transfers.

3–5 years

By age
ThemselvesShoes and coat on their hook. Plate to the counter. Choosing between two outfits. Washing hands without being walked there.
HouseholdLaying one thing on the table. Feeding a pet with an adult. Putting toys in a named box. Carrying their own bag from the car.
OrganisingFollowing a three-picture morning routine. Knowing where their things live.

At this age the point is not the labour — it is slower with them than without them. The point is that participation is normal, and that they experience themselves as someone who does things in this house.

6–8 years

By age
ThemselvesGetting dressed unprompted. Their book bag, packed the night before against a list. Their reading. Brushing teeth without supervision.
HouseholdLaying and clearing the table. Emptying a dishwasher rack. Feeding a pet alone. Watering plants. Their own washing into the basket.
OrganisingA morning routine they can follow without prompts. Knowing which day is PE. Ticking things off a list themselves.

This is the age when a visible system starts paying off. Six-year-olds cannot hold a five-step sequence reliably, but they can follow one on a wall.

9–11 years

By age
ThemselvesKit for their own clubs. Their homework, started without being told. Their washing in and out of the basket. Showering properly, on a schedule they know.
HouseholdOne area they own — the hallway, the downstairs loo, the shoe rack — with a standard they know. Cooking one simple meal with supervision. Taking bins out. Hoovering their own room.
OrganisingReading a family calendar. Packing for a sleepover from a list. Noticing a deadline before it arrives. Telling you when they need something bought — before the morning of.

Nine to eleven is the pivotal window. Handled well, most of the daily reminding stops here.

12–14 years

By age
ThemselvesTheir whole week: kit, deadlines, plans. Their washing, start to finish. Their own alarm. Managing their kit bag after training without it fermenting.
HouseholdCooking a meal for the family once a week. A proper share of the cleaning. Doing the food shop with a list. Looking after a younger sibling briefly.
OrganisingPlanning around clashes. Breaking a school project into steps. Telling you about a parents’ evening. Their own money, and the ability to spend it badly.

Expect competence and inconsistency in the same fortnight. That combination is normal at this age and is not evidence the responsibility should be withdrawn.

15–17 years

By age
ThemselvesAppointments they book and attend. A revision plan they made. Packing for a trip, unchecked. Their own budget, with real trade-offs.
HouseholdWhole areas run without supervision. Meals planned and cooked. Being genuinely useful in a busy week without being asked.
OrganisingTheir own calendar. Travel arranged. Deadlines managed weeks out. Applications, forms, sign-ups.

The aim by seventeen is uncomfortable and correct: you should be largely unnecessary to the running of their life, and available for the parts that matter.

The three signals they are ready for more

  1. It has gone quiet. You have not mentioned it for three weeks.
  2. They have adapted it. They have invented their own version — a different order, a note, a
  3. place by the door. That is ownership.
  4. They complain about the system, not the job. “This list is stupid, I already know” is a
  5. promotion request.

And when they are not ready

Shrink it rather than removing it. A twelve-year-old who cannot manage their whole week can manage Wednesdays. Withdrawing a responsibility entirely teaches something you did not intend; narrowing it teaches that responsibilities are sized to fit.

What to try this week
  1. Find the one thing your child is genuinely ready for and does not yet own.
  2. Check the previous responsibility is actually automatic before adding it.
  3. Add one column they are missing — usually the organising one.
  4. Ask them which of your jobs they would like to take over. The answers are frequently surprising.

Common questions

My child is 11 and does almost nothing. Where do I start?

Not with the age-11 list. Start with two things from the 6–8 column, get them automatic, and move up quickly — most children catch up in a term because the capability was never the constraint. Starting at their age level in this situation usually produces a fortnight of conflict and a return to the status quo.

Do children with ADHD or autism need a different list?

Usually the same list with more visible structure and smaller steps, but this is exactly where a generic guide stops being useful. Talk to whoever knows your child clinically. The general principles — external prompts rather than verbal ones, one step at a time, consistent order — tend to matter more, not less.
