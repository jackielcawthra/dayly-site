How to teach children time management

Most children cannot tell you how long anything takes. That is a teachable skill, and it is the one that matters.

The Dayly Editors Published 27 February 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 4 min read Ages 6–18
The short answer

Time management for children is three skills learned in order: feeling how long things take, estimating before doing, and planning backwards from a deadline. Teach them with concrete exercises — guess and time it, then compare — rather than with lectures about being organised.

Ask a ten-year-old how long it takes to get ready in the morning and you will get a number that is wrong by a factor of two, in either direction. Ask how long their homework will take and the answer will be five minutes, regardless of the homework.

This is not carelessness. Time is abstract, children have less experience of it, and nobody has ever made them check.

Skill 1 — Feeling duration

Before a child can plan time, they need a physical sense of how long a length of time is.

Exercises that work:

  • Guess and time. “How long do you think it takes to get dressed?” Then time it. Then compare.
  • Do it six or seven times over a fortnight with different activities.
  • Timer as a game. Five minutes on a visible timer for a tidy-up. Under-tens find this genuinely
  • engaging, and it teaches duration incidentally.
  • Countdowns rather than instructions. “Five minutes until we leave” beats “hurry up”, and it
  • builds the sense of what five minutes contains.

Visual timers — the kind showing a shrinking coloured disc — are worth the small cost for children under about ten, because time becomes a shape rather than a number.

Skill 2 — Estimating before doing

Once they have some sense of duration, make estimating explicit and routine:

“How long do you think this will take?” — then do it — “How long did it actually take?”

Write both numbers down. Say nothing else. Children correct their own estimates faster than any amount of advice does, and the exercise removes the main cause of procrastination: a job that feels like it will take an hour and actually takes eleven minutes.

Try this

For one week, ask the estimate question before every piece of homework and every job. Keep a running list of guesses and actuals on the fridge. Most children’s estimates improve markedly within a fortnight, and they find it genuinely interesting.

Skill 3 — Planning backwards

The highest-value skill, and the one that separates a child who copes at secondary school from one who does not.

The move: start at the deadline and work back.

“It’s due Friday. It has four parts, twenty minutes each. What are you doing on Tuesday?”

Do it with them on paper the first several times. Then ask the questions rather than supplying the plan. Then stop.

The two rules that make plans survive contact with reality:

Leave slack. A plan finishing on the deadline misses the deadline.

Put it somewhere visible. A plan held in a conversation is not a plan.

By age

By age
6–8Duration only. Timers, countdowns, guess-and-check. No planning yet.
9–11Estimating routinely. A weekly view of what is on. Starting to notice when two things clash.
12–14Planning backwards from a deadline. Their own calendar. Breaking projects into dated steps.
15+Managing several deadlines at once, prioritising between them, and protecting time for the important rather than the urgent.

What does not work

Telling them to manage their time better. No content, no method, and it reads as criticism.

Managing it for them. A parent-run schedule teaches compliance with a schedule, not the making of one.

Punishing lateness. Being late is usually an estimation failure, not a moral one, and punishment does not improve estimation.

Elaborate systems. Colour-coded planners are appealing to adults and abandoned within a month by almost every child. Simple, short, and theirs.

The teenage version

Two things matter more than technique at fifteen or sixteen:

Sleep. A tired teenager has poor executive function, and executive function is what time management runs on. Protecting sleep does more for their time management than any planner.

Their own system. Whatever they have invented — a notes app, a wall of sticky notes, a whiteboard — is worth more than yours, because they will use it. Resist improving it.

What to try this week
  1. Do guess-and-time on three ordinary activities and write both numbers down.
  2. Ask the estimate question before every homework session for a week.
  3. Plan one deadline backwards together, on paper.
  4. Leave their system alone, however much better yours would be.

Common questions

What age can children start managing their own time?

Duration awareness from about six, estimating from nine, planning backwards from twelve, and multiple competing deadlines from about fifteen. Expect the consistency to lag the capability by two or three years.
