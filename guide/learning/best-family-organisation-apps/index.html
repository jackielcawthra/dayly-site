The best family organisation apps

Written by people who make one of these. Here is the honest version, including where ours is the wrong choice.

The Dayly Editors Published 15 August 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 4 min read
The short answer

Shared calendars are best for families whose main problem is scheduling; task apps suit adults who already run a system; chore apps suit families focused on children’s jobs; and combined family systems suit households where school, jobs, routines and children’s independence all need to live together. Dayly is in the last category, and it is not the right answer for everyone.

Where we stand

We make Dayly, which is one of the products in this category. That is a conflict of interest and the honest thing is to name it rather than write a comparison that pretends to be neutral. What follows describes the categories fairly, and says plainly where Dayly is the wrong choice.

First, the question that decides it

What is actually going wrong in your house? The categories below solve different problems, and most families buy the wrong one because they have not named the problem.

  • ”We double-book things” → a shared calendar
  • ”I remember everything and nobody else does” → a shared system with ownership
  • ”The children do nothing” → a chore or responsibility app
  • ”Everything about school lives in my head” → a family system with school in it
  • ”I want the children to run their own lives” → something with a genuine child view

Category 1 — Shared calendars

Good at: avoiding clashes, showing everyone the week, syncing between adults’ phones. Usually free, usually already on your devices.

Weak at: anything that is not an event. A calendar cannot hold “the bins are Leo’s” or “Emma’s kit day is Thursday”, and it has no useful concept of a child.

Best for: families whose only real problem is scheduling.

Category 2 — Shared task and list apps

Good at: shopping lists, flexible projects, adults who already have a system.

Weak at: children. Most are designed for work, they assume a user who checks an app voluntarily, and there is no meaningful difference between a parent’s view and a nine-year-old’s.

Best for: two adults who both like lists.

Category 3 — Chore and reward apps

Good at: children’s jobs, visible progress, and the rewards side if you want it.

Weak at: everything else. School, calendar, travel, meals and the adults’ own load sit outside them, so they become a fifth app rather than a consolidation. Also worth watching: many are built around points and prizes, and reward systems fade.

Best for: families whose specific problem is getting children to do household jobs.

Category 4 — Family systems

Good at: holding the whole thing — calendar, responsibilities, household jobs, school, routines, travel — with a separate view for each person, including children.

Weak at: being quick to set up. There is more to configure, and a family that only needs a shared calendar will find it excessive.

Best for: households where the load is spread across school, jobs, activities and children who should be doing more themselves.

Where Dayly sits, honestly

Dayly is in the fourth category, and it is built around one specific idea: that responsibility should move from parent to child over time, so each person — including a seven-year-old — has their own view of what is theirs.

Dayly is a good fit if: one person is carrying most of the household’s mental load; you have more than one child; school, clubs and household jobs all need to be in the same place; and you want children to be able to see their own responsibilities without asking you.

Dayly is the wrong choice if: you only need a shared calendar between two adults; you want detailed project management; you want a rewards-and-prizes system as the centre of it; or you do not want to spend an hour setting something up.

What to check before choosing anything

  • Can a child use it? Not “can a child log in” — can a seven-year-old find out what they are
  • supposed to do, unaided?
  • Can you get your data out? Export before you commit.
  • What happens to children’s data? Advertising, profiling, third-party trackers.
  • Ours.
  • Does it work on the phones you actually have?
  • What does it cost per family, not per person, and what happens when you stop paying?
  • Would it survive a bad fortnight? Anything requiring daily maintenance by one adult has
  • recreated the problem.
What to try this week
  1. Name the specific problem before looking at any product.
  2. Check whether a child can use the thing unaided — that is the test most tools fail.
  3. Check the data export and the children’s privacy terms before subscribing.
  4. Try one tool properly for a month rather than three for a week each.

Common questions

Is a shared calendar enough?

For many families, genuinely yes — if the main problem is double-booking. It stops being enough when the load is made of things that are not events: kit days, whose job is whose, what school sent, what needs buying before Thursday.
