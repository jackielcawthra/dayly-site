Family travel organisation: the complete guide

Family holidays are a logistics exercise that one person usually runs alone. They do not have to be.

The Dayly Editors Published 14 May 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 4 min read Ages all
The short answer

Work to a timeline — passports and bookings eight weeks out, documents and kit two weeks out, packing three days out — and give every person their own list, including the children. The parent’s job is the shared items and the timings; everything personal belongs to its owner.

A family holiday is a project with a hard deadline, multiple stakeholders and a single point of failure who is usually also the person driving to the airport. Most of the stress is avoidable, and almost all of it is front-loaded.

The timeline

Eight weeks out

  • Check every passport. Expiry dates, and the rules for your destination — many countries require
  • months of validity remaining, and children’s passports are short and expire at inconvenient times.
  • Book the things that sell out: transfers, car hire, the airport parking, the one restaurant.
  • Check what school needs to know if any of it falls in term time.
  • Arrange the house: pets, plants, keys, post.

Four weeks out

  • Check the kit. Swimming costumes, wetsuits, ski gear, walking boots — children grow between
  • holidays, reliably, and always in the wrong direction.
  • Order anything missing. Now, not in the last week.
  • Insurance, and check what it actually covers.
  • Any medication that needs a repeat prescription.

Two weeks out

  • Documents in one place: passports, tickets, insurance, booking confirmations, the address, any
  • visa or entry requirement. Digital and printed.
  • Currency and cards. Tell the bank if you need to.
  • Adapters, chargers, entertainment for the journey.
  • Give every child their list and tell them the packing date.

Three days out

  • Everyone packs. Children pack their own against their list.
  • Laundry finished, so the things people want are available.
  • Check the bags — for younger children, with them; for older ones, not at all.

The day before

  • Charge everything.
  • Work out the leave time backwards from check-in, including travel, parking and the walk.
  • Set out what leaves the house: passports, tickets, the bag that must not be forgotten.
  • Empty the bins, sort the fridge, set the heating.

The morning

  • Nothing new. If it was not done yesterday, it is not happening.
Try this

Work out the leave time by counting backwards from the actual check-in deadline, then subtract fifteen minutes for the thing that always happens. Write it on the fridge. Every argument on the morning of departure is really about a leave time nobody agreed.

Packing: one list per person

The single change that most reduces the load. Not one master list held by one adult — a list per person, and each person packs their own.

The shared list (an adult): documents, medicines, chargers, first aid, sun cream, the beach bag, the things nobody owns individually.

Each person’s list: clothes by the day, shoes, toiletries, their own entertainment, their own kit.

Children from about seven can pack their own bag against a written list, and from about eleven without a check. How to hand it over.

The journey

  • One small bag each, packed by them, for the journey itself: book, headphones, snack, water,
  • something to do. It stops the “where’s my—“ from the row behind.
  • Snacks that are not sweets, in quantities that seem excessive.
  • A change of clothes accessible for anyone under about seven.
  • Screens: decide in advance and say so, rather than negotiating at 30,000 feet.

Arriving

Twenty minutes at the start saves the whole trip: everyone unpacks into somewhere, the dirty-clothes bag is designated, the documents go in one place that is not a pocket, and everyone knows where their things live. Children who unpack are children who can find their own swimming costume.

Coming home

The unglamorous half, and it determines what the following week feels like:

  • Washing started the day you get back, not the following weekend
  • Bags emptied entirely, including the small pockets with the boarding passes and the sand
  • Kit checked back into its place so the next trip starts from a known position
  • Anything broken or outgrown noted while you remember
What to try this week
  1. Check every passport expiry date today. It is the failure with the highest cost.
  2. Make a list per person rather than one master list.
  3. Give the children their lists a week before packing day.
  4. Work the leave time backwards and write it where everyone can see.

Common questions

How far in advance should we start?

Passports and anything that sells out: eight weeks. Everything else fits comfortably in two. The item that ruins holidays is almost always a passport or a visa requirement, and both need lead time you cannot buy back.

How do I stop packing everything for everyone?

Give each person a written list and a packing date, and do not check bags for anyone over about eleven. The first trip where someone has no socks is the last trip where they do not read the list.
