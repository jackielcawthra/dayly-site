How to get kids to help around the house

The problem is rarely willingness. It is that the job is vague, invisible, and quietly gets done by somebody else.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 5 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

Children help when the job is specific, theirs alone, visible without being told, and does not get done by an adult when they are slow. Start younger than feels efficient, use named ownership rather than a rota, and expect the first three weeks to be worse than doing it yourself.

Ask a hundred parents what they actually want and you get roughly the same sentence.

I don’t want to have to ask.

Not more help, exactly. Less asking.

Which means the thing to aim at is not obedience. It is a house where jobs have owners, and the owner is the one who notices.

Why it usually does not work

The job is vague. “Help tidy up” has no beginning, no end and no standard. “Clear the table and wipe it” has all three.

It belongs to nobody. A rota that changes every week means nobody builds the habit, and somebody has to keep track of whose turn it is. That somebody is you.

It is invisible. If the only way to find out is being told, you have not handed over a job. You have handed over a task and kept the noticing.

Somebody rescues it. This is the big one. A job that an adult does when the child is slow is not the child’s job, and children work that out remarkably fast.

It started too late. A twelve-year-old asked to start contributing after twelve years of not being asked will experience it as a new regime. And they will be right.

What works instead

Name it. Do not rotate it.

Ownership beats rotas.

“The dishwasher is Noah’s” builds a habit. “We take turns” builds a negotiation, every single day.

Rotate at the end of a term if you want fairness. Not weekly.

Attach it to something that already happens

Not a time. A trigger.

After dinner. When you come down. Before screens.

Times need clocks, and clocks need someone to do the reminding. Triggers happen on their own.

Put it somewhere other than your mouth

A list on the fridge. A whiteboard. A shared app.

Here is the test. Could your child find out what they are meant to do today without asking a person?

Say what “done” means, once

Wiped, not just cleared. Cupboards shut. Bin bag replaced.

Agree it at the start. Then do not keep re-specifying it, because weekly re-specification is management — and management is the thing you are trying to get out of.

Start absurdly young

A three-year-old carrying two forks is not helping. It is slower than doing it yourself.

It is also when the identity forms.

Children who have always done things do not experience jobs as an imposition. They experience them as what happens in this house.

What the research says

Cross-cultural work on children’s spontaneous helping is worth knowing about here. In communities where young children are routinely included in real household work — however inefficiently — they volunteer help more readily as they get older. Children whose participation is delayed until they are “old enough to be useful” tend to help less (Coppens & Rogoff, 2021).

This is observational, and cultures differ in a great many ways at once.

But it matches what most families find: it is far easier to include a four-year-old than to recruit a fourteen-year-old.

The conversation to have

Once. At a calm moment. With everybody there.

Not a lecture. A division.

“There’s a certain amount of work in running this house. Some of it is ours. Some of it is going to be yours. Here’s the list. What would you rather have?”

Letting children choose from a list works dramatically better than assigning, and it costs you nothing.

They will choose badly sometimes. It does not matter. A child who picked the bins is far more likely to do the bins than a child who was given the bins.

When they do not do it

Two rules, and they head off most of the conflict.

It still needs doing. Not as a punishment. It is simply still their job — later, and before the next enjoyable thing.

Do not do it yourself to make a point. Doing it yourself while sighing is the least effective option available. The job gets done, the lesson does not get learned, and everybody feels worse.

If it keeps happening, treat it as a design problem rather than a character one. There are six usual causes, and most of them are yours to fix.

What to actually expect

The first three weeks are worse than doing it yourself.

That is the investment, and it genuinely is one. A fortnight of slower evenings, against years of not asking.

Six months in, the sign that it worked is not a spotless house.

It is that nobody had to be asked.

What to try this week
  1. Convert one rotating job into a named, owned one.
  2. Attach it to a trigger rather than a time.
  3. Write what “done” means, once, where they can see it.
  4. Let each child pick one new job from a list.
  5. Decide in advance that you will not do it for them when they are slow.

Common questions

How many jobs should a child have?

Fewer than most charts suggest, and consistently. One or two daily jobs plus one weekly is plenty for a primary-age child. A long list without a system produces nothing; two jobs that always happen produce a habit.

Should I pay them?

Not for the basics of being a household member. Payment for genuinely extra work is reasonable. The evidence and a workable split.

What about teenagers who have never done anything?

Start with two jobs, negotiated rather than imposed, and be honest that it is a change: "we got this wrong, and it needs to be different now." Teenagers respond far better to a straight admission than to a new chart appearing on the fridge.

Sources & further reading

  2. Grusec, J., Goodnow, J. & Cohen, L. (1996). Household work and the development of concern for others. Developmental Psychology
