Do reward charts work?

They work for about three weeks. Understanding why tells you what to do next.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 4 min read
The short answer

Reward charts reliably change behaviour in the short term and reliably stop working as novelty fades. They are most useful for establishing a specific new habit over a few weeks, and least useful as a permanent system. The evidence suggests rewards can reduce interest in activities a child already enjoyed, though the effect is narrower than often claimed.

Almost every family tries one. Almost every family finds the same thing: three good weeks, then a slow fade, then a chart on a wall that nobody has updated since March.

That pattern is not a failure of consistency. It is what reward systems do.

What the evidence shows

Short-term: they work. Behaviour reliably changes when a reward is attached, and this is well-established. For establishing a specific new habit over a defined period, a chart is a reasonable tool.

Long-term: the effect fades. Novelty is doing a lot of the work, and novelty is not renewable.

The overjustification effect. A large meta-analysis found that tangible rewards, offered in advance and contingent on doing a task, reduced later intrinsic motivation for that task — particularly for activities the child already found interesting (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).

What the research says

The qualifications matter. The effect is strongest for tasks that were already intrinsically interesting; emptying a bin does not qualify. Unexpected rewards, given after the fact, do not show the same effect. Rewards for quality rather than mere participation behave differently. And other researchers argue the effect is smaller and more conditional than the headline implies (Cameron & Pierce, 1994).

The honest summary: rewarding something a child already wanted to do can reduce their appetite for it. Rewarding something nobody wants to do is mostly just paying, and its main weakness is that it stops working.

When a chart is genuinely useful

  • Establishing one specific new habit over three or four weeks — a new bedtime routine, a
  • transition, getting used to something difficult
  • Making progress visible for a child who cannot see their own improvement
  • Getting through a hard patch — a new school, a new sibling, a period of upheaval
  • As a visibility tool with no prize attached, which is really a
  • list rather than a reward system

When it is the wrong tool

  • As a permanent household system. It will fade, and its fading teaches something unhelpful:
  • that the job was only worth doing for the sticker.
  • For things you want a child to value. Reading is the classic. Paying for pages read is a
  • well-intentioned way to make reading into work.
  • When it becomes negotiation. “How many stickers?” as the opening question is a sign it has gone
  • wrong.
  • For siblings, comparatively. A chart where children can see each other’s totals is a
  • competition, and one child is losing it every day.

Making a chart work while it lasts

If you are going to use one:

One behaviour, not eight. Charts fail on breadth more than anything else.

Achievable daily. A chart a child cannot succeed at today is a chart they have stopped reading.

No resets. Losing everything for one bad day is a reliable way to lose them entirely.

Short. Three or four weeks, then stop deliberately rather than letting it decay. Stopping on purpose — “you don’t need this any more” — is a much better ending.

Praise the specific thing, not the sticker count. “You got yourself ready without me saying anything” is what you actually want, and it is what should be named.

What to use instead

For most household jobs, the four conditions do more than any reward system: ownership, visibility, consequence and time.

And for motivation specifically, the three things with the best evidence behind them are autonomy (a real choice), competence (it is achievable and they can see progress) and relatedness (it matters to people they care about). More on that.

What to try this week
  1. If you have a chart, cut it to one behaviour.
  2. Remove any reset rule.
  3. Set an end date and stop deliberately rather than letting it fade.
  4. Replace one sticker with a specific, named observation.

Common questions

Do reward charts work for toddlers?

They can, for a short period and for one specific thing, provided the reward is immediate — a sticker now, not a prize on Friday. Delayed rewards do not connect for children under about four.

What about points systems in apps?

Same mechanism, same fade. They work best when reserved for genuinely extra effort rather than for the basics of being a household member — that narrower use avoids most of the problem.

Sources & further reading

  1. Deci, E., Koestner, R. & Ryan, R. (1999). Extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin
  2. Cameron, J. & Pierce, W. (1994). Reinforcement, Reward, and Intrinsic Motivation. Review of Educational Research
  3. Ryan, R. & Deci, E. (2000). Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation. American Psychologist
