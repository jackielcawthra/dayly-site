Do reward charts work?

They can work. The more interesting question is what, exactly, they teach a child — and what happens when the stickers stop.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 6 min read
The short answer

Reward charts can help a child start a new routine, and they work less well as a permanent arrangement, because the behaviour stays attached to the reward. The more useful version of gamification makes the repetition enjoyable and the progress visible without turning ordinary family life into a series of transactions.

Reward charts are everywhere in parenting. Stars for brushing your teeth, stickers for staying in bed, points for helping around the house, prizes for getting through the week without an argument.

They can work. The more interesting question is what, exactly, they teach a child.

A reward can make a behaviour happen more often, particularly when the behaviour is new or difficult. But there is a difference between helping a child get started and helping them develop a reason of their own to keep going.

That distinction matters.

Why rewards are so tempting

Parents use rewards because they solve an immediate problem.

The child does not want to tidy their room. You offer a sticker. They tidy it. Everyone is happier, and it feels as though you have found the missing ingredient.

Sometimes you have.

Rewards are particularly useful when a child is learning a new routine or when the behaviour is genuinely effortful. A visible reminder of progress can make something abstract feel concrete, and a small reward can provide the extra motivation needed to get started.

The problem comes when the reward becomes the reason for doing the thing.

If brushing your teeth always earns a sticker, what happens when the stickers stop?

The answer is not necessarily that the child has learned nothing. But the behaviour may have remained attached to the reward rather than becoming part of their own routine.

Motivation matters more than the sticker

There is a large body of research on intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. One of the recurring findings is that external rewards can sometimes undermine intrinsic motivation, particularly when a person was already interested in the activity.

That does not mean rewards are harmful or that parents should never use them. It means they are not a universal solution to motivation.

A child is unlikely to develop a love of reading because you pay them to read. They may, however, read more because the payment gives them a reason to pick up the book in the first place.

Those are different outcomes.

The useful question is therefore not “Does this reward work?” It is “What do I want my child to learn from this?”

If the answer is simply “I want the room tidied tonight”, a reward may do the job.

If the answer is “I want my child to understand that everyone who lives here contributes to the home”, a reward chart may be pointing in the wrong direction.

Be careful what you are rewarding

There is another problem with reward charts: they can turn ordinary family life into a series of transactions.

A child washes their plate because they get a point. They put their shoes away because they get a sticker. They help their sibling because there is something in it for them.

Eventually, the obvious question arrives: What do I get for doing it?

That is not necessarily the lesson you intended to teach.

Children are part of a family. They have rights, needs and responsibilities. Some things are simply part of living together.

We do not need to pay adults every time they put something away, because contribution is part of belonging to a household. Children can gradually learn the same thing.

That does not mean expecting them to do everything cheerfully. Responsibility and enthusiasm are not the same thing.

There is a better use for gamification

Games are powerful because they make repetition enjoyable and progress visible. Children often do things in a game that they would resist if you simply told them to do them.

That is worth using.

The trick is to make the experience rewarding rather than making every responsibility a transaction.

A child might collect points for completing a personal challenge, move through levels, race the clock, work towards a family goal or see their progress over time. The reward can be the feeling of getting somewhere, rather than a parent handing over a prize for every completed task.

This is particularly useful for routines that otherwise feel boring: getting ready, packing a school bag, putting things away or completing a series of small jobs.

The game provides momentum. The responsibility remains theirs.

Rewards can also become a way of noticing

There is a difference between “You did that, so you get something” and “I noticed that you remembered to do that.”

The second one can be surprisingly powerful.

Children want to be seen. Specific recognition tells them that their effort mattered: You remembered your swimming things without me asking. You noticed your brother needed help. You kept going when that was difficult.

That kind of feedback connects the behaviour to the child rather than to the prize.

It also gives them information about themselves. They begin to notice what they are capable of doing.

What about money?

Money can be useful when the thing being taught is actually about money.

Paying children for extra work can help them learn that money is earned, that work takes effort and that there are choices about what to do with what they earn.

It becomes less useful when money is attached to every ordinary responsibility.

There is a difference between paying a child to wash the car and paying them to put their own clothes away. One is an extra contribution. The other is part of looking after themselves and the home they live in. There is more on where that line sits in should children be paid for chores.

Keeping that distinction clear makes the system easier to understand.

So should you use a reward chart?

Sometimes.

If a child is struggling to establish a new routine, a chart can make progress visible and provide some immediate encouragement. If the child enjoys collecting stickers or points, there is nothing inherently wrong with that.

But do not confuse a chart with the underlying lesson.

The longer-term goal is not a child who behaves because somebody is keeping score. It is a child who gradually understands what needs doing, takes responsibility for it and experiences the satisfaction of being capable.

Use rewards when they help. Use games when they make something more enjoyable. And when the behaviour becomes familiar, let the reward become less important.

The best outcome is not a full chart.

It is a child who eventually does the thing without needing one.

What to try this week
  1. If you have a chart, cut it to one behaviour.
  2. Remove any reset rule.
  3. Set an end date and stop deliberately rather than letting it fade.
  4. Replace one sticker with a specific, named observation.

Common questions

Do reward charts work for toddlers?

They can, for a short period and for one specific thing, provided the reward is immediate — a sticker now, not a prize on Friday. Delayed rewards do not connect for children under about four.

What about points systems in apps?

Same mechanism, same fade. They work best when reserved for genuinely extra effort rather than for the basics of being a household member — that narrower use avoids most of the problem.

Sources & further reading

  3. Ryan, R. & Deci, E. (2000). Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation. American Psychologist
