Mental Load

What is the mental load?

The reading folder. The shoes that have suddenly become too small. The party on Saturday with no present bought. Somebody has to hold all of it.

The Dayly Editors Published 4 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 9 min read Ages all
The short answer

The mental load is the noticing, remembering, planning and deciding that sits underneath family life. It is not the jobs themselves. It is knowing what needs doing, and when, and whether anybody else has thought about it. In most families it ends up with one person, and it only moves when whole areas are handed over rather than tasks handed out.

There is the reading folder that needs to go back on Wednesday. The shoes that have suddenly become too small. The birthday party on Saturday, for which nobody has bought a present. The dentist who texted three weeks ago and is still somewhere in the back of your mind.

None of these things is particularly difficult. That is partly why they are so easy to underestimate.

The problem is that somebody has to notice them. Somebody has to remember them. Somebody has to know what needs to happen next, and when it needs to happen, and whether anyone else has thought about it yet.

Usually, that somebody is a person.

And if you asked a hundred couples with children which of them it is, most would give you the same answer.

What researchers found when they went looking

For a long time this was hard to study, because it happens silently and inside somebody’s head. There is no way to observe it, and no obvious point at which it starts or stops.

In 2019 Allison Daminger, then a sociology doctoral candidate at Harvard, published a study of 35 couples. She found that the thinking behind household work breaks into four stages: anticipating what will be needed, working out the options, deciding between them, and then checking that it actually happened.

Mothers did more at all four stages. Decisions were often made together — but the anticipating, the researching and the following-up were not shared (Daminger, American Sociological Review, 2019).

The visible moment — the conversation where two people choose a school, or a holiday — looks equal. Everything around it is not.

Leah Ruppanner, a sociologist at the University of Melbourne, has made a related point: this is the work that follows you to your desk. It is the low-level, more or less permanent question of whether you are doing enough.

Three kinds of work, and only one of them shows

What it looks likeHow visible is it?
Physical workCooking, driving, washing, tidyingVisible. It happens in front of people.
Cognitive workNoticing, remembering, planning, decidingInvisible until it fails.
Emotional workAnticipating how people will feel, smoothing, keeping the peaceAlmost entirely invisible.

A household can be genuinely even on the first row and completely uneven on the other two, which is how two people can look at the same kitchen and both be telling the truth. One of them is counting the dishwasher. The other is counting knowing about the dishwasher.

What the research says

What the evidence supports. Cognitive household labour can be measured, it is unevenly distributed, and the person carrying more of it reports lower wellbeing (Dean, Churchill & Ruppanner, 2022).

What it does not support. That anybody is naturally better at it. Daminger’s follow-up work found couples explaining the imbalance to themselves in terms of temperament — one partner was simply “interested in being organised” — while other research points the other way. People are not born better at remembering appointments. They become better at it by being the one who does it.

How it settles on one person

Almost never by decision. Ask a couple when it happened and neither of them can point to a moment.

It happens through availability. One parent takes the longer leave, or has the job with more give in it, or simply has the quieter year — and whoever is present when the systems get set up ends up inside them. They register with the nursery. They meet the other parents. Their number goes on the form. And after that, the school rings them first, the dentist emails them, and the class group has them in it and not the other one.

Then something smaller starts happening, and it does more damage. The other parent genuinely does not know where the swimming kit is, so they ask. Answering takes four seconds. Explaining the whole system takes ten minutes, and they will probably ask again next week anyway.

So you answer.

Multiply that by a thousand exchanges over two years. Now the knowledge is not just in one person. The house runs on it being there.

The tell is the word help. Nobody helps with their own responsibilities.

Daminger noted something else that is worth knowing, because it explains why this rarely turns into an argument: in her study, the imbalance mostly did not. Both partners had an explanation ready. He works longer hours. She is better at planning. Neither explanation gets examined, and so neither arrangement gets revisited.

Why “just tell me what to do” does not help

It is offered kindly, by people who are willing, and it lands badly — which surprises the person offering it.

The reason is in Daminger’s four stages. Telling somebody what to do requires you to have anticipated the need, weighed the options, decided, and then chosen a moment to say it; and it commits you to checking afterwards that it happened. The offer takes the doing. It hands the other three back, and adds checking up on someone to the list.

What actually moves is a whole area, with the anticipating included. Not “can you do the shopping this week”, but food is yours — the meals, the list, the order, the fact that we are out of coffee.

It will be done differently. That is what it costs, and it costs less than the arrangement you have.

What it consists of

Most people underestimate it, because it never appears anywhere as a list. It is held in fragments, across weeks, some of it triggered by a date in a newsletter and some of it by a glance at a pair of shoes. The only place all of it exists at once is inside one person’s head.

Written down, for one household with school-age children, it looks something like this.

  • Term dates, inset days, holiday clubs, and the childcare arranged around them
  • Which child needs kit, on which day, in which bag
  • Newsletters, permission slips, parents’ evenings, reading records
  • Clothes: sizes, seasons, what no longer fits, what was lost
  • Appointments: dentist, optician, vaccinations, the follow-up nobody booked
  • Birthdays: theirs, their friends’, presents, cards, the invitation on the fridge
  • Food: what everyone eats, what is in the house, what expires on Thursday
  • Clubs and sport: fees, times, lifts, the kit still in the boot of the car
  • Gifts and cards for grandparents, teachers, whoever
  • The state of things: batteries, bulbs, the boiler service, the passport that expires in June

Nobody is bad at organising. There is simply a great deal of it, and almost none of it arrives with a deadline attached until the morning it is due.

What actually shifts it

Make it visible

You cannot divide something nobody can see. Write down the areas — not the tasks, the areas — and put one name against each.

Most couples find this uncomfortable and useful in roughly equal measure. The mental load checklist is one way in; the mental load calculator is quicker.

Hand over areas, not tasks

An area comes with the noticing. “Sport is yours” means kit, times, fees, lifts, and knowing that the shin pads no longer fit.

Expect a wobble in the first month. That is what a handover looks like, and it is not a sign that it is failing.

Let the standard move

The other person will do it differently, and for a while probably slightly worse by your standard. This is where most handovers die.

If a correction follows every one of them, the responsibility comes straight back, and everybody has learned that it was lent rather than given. Save the correction for the things that genuinely matter.

Give the children their share

A child old enough to lose a water bottle is old enough to be responsible for a water bottle. Not as a chore — as something that is theirs, with the consequence attached.

Every area a child takes on is an area nobody else is holding for them, and the skills they practise doing it are the point rather than a side-effect.

What to aim for

The load does not go to zero. A family that generates no logistics is not a goal worth having, and no arrangement survives a term without something going wrong.

What is achievable is narrower. It stops being one person’s. It lives somewhere other than inside a single head. And the people it concerns can see it for themselves, without being told.

Which is a much lighter way to run a family.

What to try this week
  1. Write down every area — not task — that keeps a household running. Aim for twenty lines. Do it separately from your partner, then compare.
  2. Pick the one area you would most like to stop holding. Hand over the whole thing, including the noticing.
  3. Agree out loud that it will be done differently, and that you will not correct it for a month.
  4. Give each child one area that is genuinely theirs, with the consequence attached.
  5. Put the areas somewhere everyone can see them, so nobody has to be asked.

Common questions

Is the mental load the same as multitasking?

No. Multitasking is doing several things at once. The mental load is holding responsibility for things that are not happening yet — remembering that they exist, tracking when they will matter, and deciding what to do about them. You can be sitting still and carrying an enormous mental load.

Is this only about mothers?

No, and framing it that way makes it harder to fix. The pattern is structural: whoever becomes the default point of contact accumulates the load. Research does find it lands disproportionately on mothers in different-sex couples, but single parents carry all of it, and in plenty of households it is the father. The useful question is not who is naturally better at it — it is which structure put it all in one place.

How do I explain it to a partner who does not see the problem?

Lists work better than arguments. Write down the areas separately, then compare. Most of the disagreement disappears when both people can see that one column contains twenty items and the other contains six — and the conversation moves on to who takes what.

Can an app fix this?

Not on its own. Software makes responsibility visible and stops it living in one head, which is the necessary condition — but the handover is a decision two adults make, and the standard-lowering is the hard part. Dayly is a place to keep it. It cannot want it for you.
