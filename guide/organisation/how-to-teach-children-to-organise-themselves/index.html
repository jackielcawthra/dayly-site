Organisation

How to teach children to organise themselves

They are not disorganised. They are being asked to organise more of their life than their ability to remember and anticipate can support.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 February 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 9 min read Ages 4–17
The short answer

Before a child can organise themselves, there has to be something outside their head to organise with. Executive function develops slowly through childhood, so the system does the remembering first and the skill arrives through using it. The work is building something they will actually use, then pointing them at it rather than answering.

A child who is always losing things, forgetting what they need and discovering at 7:45 that they were supposed to bring something to school can look disorganised.

Often, they are not. They are being asked to organise more of their life than their current ability to remember, sequence and anticipate can support.

There is usually an organised person somewhere nearby making up the difference.

That person knows that it is swimming tomorrow. They know where the swimming bag is. They know that the permission slip still needs signing, that the library book goes back on Thursday and that leaving the house at 7:52 is already pushing it.

The child does not need to know any of this because, so far, somebody else has been keeping track.

This is why teaching organisation is not simply a matter of telling children to “get organised”. Before a child can organise themselves, there has to be something outside their head that they can organise with.

Organisation starts outside the child

Adults who seem organised are rarely relying on memory alone. We use calendars, lists, alarms and places where things live. We put tomorrow’s clothes by the door and check what we need before leaving the house.

Children are often expected to do the same work internally.

That is a much bigger ask than it sounds. Executive function — the group of skills involved in working memory, self-control, planning and switching between tasks — develops gradually throughout childhood and adolescence.

So it makes sense to give children external structures long before we expect them to carry the whole system themselves.

A list is not a sign that a child is failing to remember. It is how they learn to remember. A routine is not there because they are incapable of making decisions. It removes some decisions while they are still learning how to make them.

The external system comes first. The internal skill develops through using it.

Make the system easy to use

A child does not need a sophisticated organisational system. They need one they will actually use.

One place to look is better than several. The same order each morning is easier than having to work out what comes next. A reminder attached to something that already happens — checking the list after breakfast, for example — is easier to act on than a reminder that exists only on a clock.

And there is one habit that tends to make an outsized difference: looking at tomorrow before today has finished.

An evening check finds the missing football shirt while there is still time to wash it. It finds the permission slip before the school run. It gives a child a chance to discover that something is missing while there is still something they can do about it.

A morning check tends to find the same problems when everybody is already trying to leave.

Build it with them

If you want a child to use a system, it helps if they have had some part in making it.

Start with one part of the day. The evening is often easier than the morning because there is less pressure. Work out together what actually needs to happen and write it down in the child’s language.

“Bag by the door” is more useful than “prepare belongings”. For a younger child, pictures may work better than words.

Then give it some time.

Parents are naturally inclined to keep improving systems, but a slightly imperfect routine that a child understands and feels belongs to them is often more useful than a beautifully designed routine that an adult has imposed.

The child has to be able to see themselves using it.

The difficult part is handing it over

There is a gradual shift from doing something for a child, to doing it together, to watching them do it, and eventually to no longer being involved.

The middle part can be frustrating.

You are standing there while your child takes eight minutes to do something that would take you two. You can see exactly what they have forgotten. You know what needs to happen next.

It is very tempting to tell them.

Sometimes that is appropriate. But when they are capable of finding the answer themselves, it can be more useful to point them back towards the system.

“What comes next?”

“What do you need tomorrow?”

“Have you checked your list?”

The purpose is not to make the child work everything out alone. It is to make the list, calendar or routine the place they go for the answer, rather than you.

Over time, that distinction matters. If every organisational problem comes back to a parent, the parent remains the system.

Organisation is several skills

A child can be good at one part of organisation and struggle badly with another.

There is remembering what needs to be done, but also sequencing it: knowing that you need to find the kit before you can pack the bag.

There is estimating time. Many children can know exactly what they need to do and still believe they can shower, get dressed, eat breakfast, pack their bag and leave in twelve minutes.

There is anticipation: looking at tomorrow today and noticing what will be needed.

And there is recovery. What happens when the plan fails?

That last one is easy to overlook. An organised person is not someone who never forgets anything. They are someone who notices when something has gone wrong and knows what to do next.

Children need to practise that too.

Let them experience some of the consequences

If you always rescue a child from the consequences of being disorganised, they have very little opportunity to learn what happens when their system fails.

This does not mean deliberately letting children fail. It means noticing when the consequence is small enough to be useful.

The forgotten water bottle can sometimes mean being thirsty. The forgotten homework can sometimes mean explaining to the teacher. The missing football kit can sometimes mean missing training.

A child may be upset. That does not mean the experience was unhelpful.

The useful response is often to help them work out what happened and what they could do differently next time, rather than turning the mistake into evidence that they are careless or irresponsible.

Being organised includes knowing what to do when you have not been organised.

Give them something to own

Organisation becomes much easier to learn when it is attached to something that genuinely belongs to the child.

Their school things. Their sports equipment. Their room. Their laundry. Later, parts of the household.

A child who is responsible for their football equipment needs to know when training is, what they need, where the kit is, whether it is clean and what to do when something is missing.

That is a much richer responsibility than being told to put the football bag by the door.

It is also why 100:100 makes more sense than 50:50. The aim is not to divide family life into equal numbers of chores. It is for each person to have areas they genuinely own, with the thinking included.

For a young child, that ownership will be small. As they grow, it should become larger.

Give the skill time to catch up

There is a temptation to judge an organisational system by whether a child follows it consistently.

That can be misleading.

A twelve-year-old may know exactly how to prepare for tomorrow and still forget three times in a fortnight. A teenager may understand how to plan backwards from a deadline and still leave something until the night before.

The important thing is what happens around the mistake.

Did they notice?

Did they understand what had gone wrong?

Did they fix it?

Did they change anything the next time?

Those are signs that the skill is developing, even when the behaviour is still inconsistent.

Children do not become organised because somebody finally finds the right reminder. They become organised through years of having things to remember, systems to use, mistakes to recover from and responsibilities that gradually become their own.

At some point, the parent who used to know everything stops being the place where all the information lives.

The child has somewhere to look. They know what belongs to them. They begin to look ahead without being told.

That is the part worth working towards: not a child who never forgets, but one who increasingly knows how to find out what needs to happen next.

What to try this week
  1. Pick one part of the day and write a five-step list with your child, in their words.
  2. Move one morning check to the night before.
  3. Answer every organisational question with a question for one week.
  4. Do the time-estimate exercise once, and say nothing about the result.

Common questions

How do I get my child to actually use the list?

Attach checking it to something that already happens — after teeth, after dinner, when they come down. Lists fail because nothing triggers them, not because children dislike lists. And keep it to five or six lines; a long list is a wall of text and gets ignored.

My child is very disorganised compared to their sibling. Is something wrong?

The variation between siblings at the same age is genuinely large and mostly normal. If disorganisation is severe, persistent across every setting, and causing real difficulty at school, it is worth raising with the school or your GP — but the first move is always to check whether they have external structure or are being asked to do it from memory.

Should young children use apps or paper?

Whatever they will actually look at. Paper on a wall has a real advantage for younger children: it is always visible and requires no unlocking, no login and no decision to open it. Shared apps come into their own once children have their own phone and a week complicated enough to need one.
