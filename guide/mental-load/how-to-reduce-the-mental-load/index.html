Mental Load

How to reduce the mental load

You can be making dinner and remembering that the dentist needs booking. The physical task may take five minutes. The thought can stay with you for days.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 August 2026 7 min read Ages all
The short answer

Reducing the mental load is not about doing fewer tasks or dividing them more evenly. It is about reducing how many things require one person to keep track of them: giving responsibilities a clear owner, deciding which standards actually matter, and putting family information somewhere everyone can see it.

When people talk about reducing the mental load, the advice often begins with a list. Write everything down. Divide the chores. Make a rota. Delegate.

All of those things can help, but they miss something important about the nature of the problem. Mental load is not simply the number of tasks a person completes. It is the amount of family life they are continually keeping track of: what needs to happen, what might happen, what someone else might need, what has been forgotten and what will need attention next.

That thinking continues even when nothing is being done.

You can be making dinner and remembering that the dentist needs booking. You can be driving to football and wondering whether the birthday present has been bought. You can sit down at the end of the day and find yourself mentally working through tomorrow. The physical task may take five minutes. The thought can stay with you for days.

Research on mental load has increasingly recognised that it extends beyond household organisation. It includes anticipating and planning, but also emotional support, maintaining relationships, creating special moments for other people, helping family members pursue their goals, looking after health and safety, and the longer-term work of raising children who are able to thrive. It is a much larger category of work than what appears on a household chore list.

That is one reason it can be difficult to explain why you are tired. You may have finished everything you needed to do and still feel as though you have been working all day.

Mental load has a habit of multiplying

One small responsibility can generate a surprisingly long chain of thought.

You notice that you are running out of something at home. You need to remember to buy it, decide when you can get it, work out whether you need anything else while you are there and perhaps remember that you bought the wrong quantity last time. The original task is small. The thinking around it is not.

This is particularly true when a responsibility has no clear owner. If nobody knows whose job it is to notice, remember and act, more than one person may keep it somewhere in the back of their mind. The family ends up with duplicated thinking: two people vaguely remembering the same appointment, both wondering whether the other has dealt with it, neither quite willing to let it go.

Clear ownership removes some of that uncertainty.

If one person is responsible for school, they should not have to be reminded about the school trip by someone who is not responsible for school. If another person owns football, the first person should be able to stop carrying the football schedule in their head.

This is one reason 100:100 is more useful than 50:50. The aim is not for two people to perform an identical number of tasks. It is for both people to be fully responsible for the areas they own.

Not everything needs to be shared

There is another important distinction.

The goal is not to eliminate mental effort. Some of the things that take up mental space are things we genuinely value. You may enjoy organising your child’s birthday, planning holidays or cooking for the family. There is no particular virtue in handing those things away simply because they count as work.

The more useful question is whether the effort is connected to something you care about.

Some things need to be done. Some things we enjoy doing. Some things help create the kind of family life we actually want. And then there are things that have accumulated around the edges: habits, expectations and standards that nobody consciously chose but which somebody has nevertheless taken responsibility for maintaining.

Those are worth examining.

Families can spend an extraordinary amount of energy trying to maintain standards that are not especially important to anyone. The birthday has to be elaborate. The house has to be tidy before visitors arrive. Dinner has to be cooked from scratch. Every weekend needs to be planned.

Perhaps those things matter to you. If they do, they are worth the effort.

But sometimes reducing the mental load means deciding that something can simply be less important.

There is a difference between caring and carrying

One of the difficulties with mental load is that it can become tangled up with love.

You remember the birthday because you care about the person. You notice when your child is struggling because you are paying attention. You plan the holiday because you want everyone to have a good time.

Those things should not necessarily disappear.

What can change is the assumption that caring means one person has to carry every detail.

A partner can know the birthday is coming. A child can learn to remember their own equipment. Another adult can take responsibility for making the appointment. Family members can become participants in the organisation of family life rather than simply people being organised.

That shift takes time, particularly with children. A young child will need reminders before they can reliably remember something themselves. They will forget things. They will sometimes discover the consequence of not being prepared.

That is not necessarily a failure of the system. It is part of learning how to take responsibility.

Rest needs to be more than an empty calendar

Another reason mental load is so exhausting is that rest does not always mean that the thinking stops.

A person can have no practical tasks left for the evening and still be mentally running through tomorrow. They can watch television while remembering the school form. They can lie in bed thinking about the thing they forgot to arrange.

The body is resting. The mind is still working.

Mental energy is not unlimited, and this matters because the cost of carrying too much is not simply that the household feels slightly less organised. The research links greater cognitive household labour with poorer wellbeing, including higher stress and burnout.

The answer is therefore not to become more efficient at holding everything.

It is to reduce the number of things that require you to hold them.

Put the information somewhere it can be shared

This is where practical systems become useful.

A family receives an extraordinary amount of information. School newsletters, club messages, appointments, invitations, forms, deadlines and changes to plans arrive continuously. If all of that information has to pass through one person’s head before anyone else can act on it, that person remains the family’s central processor even if the physical jobs have been divided fairly.

The information needs somewhere else to live.

It needs to be visible to the people responsible for it, and it needs to remain available after the original message has disappeared into an inbox or a group chat.

That is one of the problems Dayly is designed to address. It gives family information a shared place, so that the things happening in family life can become visible to the people they actually concern.

But the technology is only part of the answer. The more important change is deciding who owns what, allowing people to do things differently, and giving children the opportunity to take responsibility for the parts of family life that belong to them.

Reducing the mental load does not mean creating a family in which nobody has to think.

It means creating one in which thinking, remembering and responsibility are not quietly concentrated in one person’s head.

And once that happens, the point is not simply that there is less to remember.

There is more room for everything else.

Common questions

Is reducing the mental load the same as doing fewer jobs?

No. A household can divide the physical jobs evenly and leave one person still tracking all of it. What reduces the load is reducing the number of things that require you to keep track of them at all — which usually means giving whole areas a clear owner rather than handing out individual tasks.

What if I actually enjoy organising some of it?

Then it is worth the effort. The point is not to hand away everything that counts as work. It is to notice which of it is connected to something you care about, and which has accumulated around the edges as a standard nobody consciously chose.

How long does it take for a child to take on a responsibility?

Longer than most people expect, and the forgetting is part of it. A young child needs reminders before they can reliably remember something themselves. A forgotten bag is not evidence that it is not working.
