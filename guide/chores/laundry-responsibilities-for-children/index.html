Laundry responsibilities for children

The question is not who physically puts the clothes in the machine. It is who is responsible for noticing, preparing and dealing with the laundry.

The Dayly Editors Published 18 June 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 9 min read Ages 5–17
The short answer

Design the system before handing out jobs. A basket each, enough clothes to last the week, a regular laundry day, loads sorted by how they wash rather than by who owns them, and the job finished only when clean clothes are away. A parent can keep doing most of the washing and still remove most of the mental load.

Laundry sounds like an obvious responsibility for children to take on. In practice, though, “everyone does their own laundry” can create more work than it removes.

Do you really want five tiny loads running throughout the week? What happens with towels? Can two children share a load? And does a child actually have enough clothes to wait a whole week?

The answer is to design the laundry system first, then decide what each child is responsible for.

The short version

A workable family laundry system usually looks something like this:

  1. Each child has somewhere to put their dirty clothes.
  2. Everyone has enough everyday clothes to get through most of the week.
  3. Each child, or pair of children, has a regular laundry day.
  4. Clothes are sorted by washing requirements, not necessarily by child.
  5. Children can share a load, but remain responsible for their own clothes.
  6. The job is not finished until clean clothes are put away.
  7. The system can be adjusted when someone runs out of something.

But there is another perfectly sensible system: the parent can continue doing most of the laundry while the children take responsibility for getting their clothes into the system.

You don’t have to hand over all the laundry

A parent can keep doing most of the washing.

What changes is that the children have to bring their dirty clothes to the laundry area. Nobody goes around their rooms collecting it, and nobody else is responsible for noticing whose clothes need washing.

If they want something washed at a particular time, they can wash it themselves.

That might be because they need a particular sports kit the next morning, want a favourite outfit clean or have simply run out of something.

This works well because the parent does not have to take on every part of the responsibility for the system to improve.

You can keep doing the part that works for your family while gradually handing over the parts that create the most mental load.

You need enough clothes for the system to work

If you want a child to do their laundry once a week, they need enough everyday clothes to last roughly that long.

That does not mean they need a huge wardrobe. A reasonable supply of underwear, socks, school clothes and everyday outfits is usually enough.

The important thing is that laundry day is a planned routine rather than an emergency response to the fact that someone has run out of clean socks.

If a child does run out, they can do an extra wash. That is part of learning to manage their own laundry.

Give each child a basket

The simplest starting point is a laundry basket or hamper where dirty clothes go.

The rule is straightforward:

If it isn’t in the basket, it doesn’t get washed.

This removes one of the most invisible laundry jobs: an adult walking around bedrooms collecting clothes.

A child who leaves their sports kit on the floor may discover that it is not magically clean the next morning.

That is not a punishment. It is simply how the system works.

Choose a laundry day

Once a child has enough clothes to last several days, there is no reason to run a machine every time the basket contains something dirty.

Give each child a regular laundry day.

For example:

  • Monday — Child 1
  • Tuesday — Child 2
  • Wednesday — Child 3
  • Thursday — Child 4

Or, if you have several children, two children can share a day.

The exact day does not matter. What matters is that everyone knows when their laundry normally happens.

A teenager can eventually be responsible for noticing that their basket is full and deciding when it needs doing. For younger children, a family schedule makes the routine easier.

Children do not need separate washing machines

You do not necessarily need to wash each child’s clothes separately.

If two children have laundry on the same day, they can share a load. Their clothes can go into the machine together and be separated afterwards.

And laundry is normally sorted by how it needs to be washed, rather than by who owns it.

You might have:

  • Everyday dark clothes
  • Light clothes
  • Towels
  • Bed linen
  • Delicates or clothes needing special treatment

Two children’s dark everyday clothes can easily become one load.

The children can work together to load and unload the machine, then each takes responsibility for sorting, folding and putting away their own clothes.

What about colours?

You do not need a separate wardrobe of every colour for every child.

The useful distinction is usually between washing requirements, not ownership.

If something is new and likely to run, it may need to be washed separately the first few times. Some clothes need a cooler wash, some should not go in the dryer and some need to be treated differently.

Children can learn these rules gradually.

They do not need to know everything immediately. They need to know enough to recognise when something is straightforward and when they should ask.

Two children sharing a laundry day can work really well

In a larger family, individual laundry days can become unnecessarily complicated.

Two children can have the same laundry day and work together.

They can:

  1. Bring their baskets to the laundry area.
  2. Sort the clothes.
  3. Decide what can go into the same load.
  4. Load and run the machine.
  5. Move the washing to the dryer or drying rack.
  6. Separate their clothes.
  7. Fold and put away their own things.

This is also a useful way to teach younger children. One child may be able to manage more of the process while the other is still learning.

The responsibility does not have to mean doing every step alone.

What about towels and bed linen?

It is usually easier to keep some household laundry separate.

Towels, sheets and kitchen towels do not necessarily need to belong to an individual child. They can have their own place in the family laundry routine.

Children can still take responsibility for their own bed linen: stripping the bed, putting the dirty sheets in the laundry area and eventually putting clean sheets back on.

The important thing is to distinguish personal laundry from shared household laundry.

When should the parent step in?

Children need to learn the basics before they can manage everything independently.

At first, an adult may need to show them:

  • Which clothes can be washed together
  • How much detergent to use
  • Which programme to choose
  • What can go in the dryer
  • What should be hung up
  • How to deal with a stain

Then gradually step back.

You do not need to inspect every load forever. If a child is old enough to use the machine safely, they can learn by doing.

And yes, occasionally something will shrink or turn the wrong colour.

That is part of learning how clothes work.

The job is not finished when the machine stops

This is probably the most important rule.

Laundry is finished when the clean clothes are back where they belong.

A clean basket sitting on the bedroom floor for three days is not a completed job.

Children can learn to fold, hang or put away their own clothes. The standard does not need to be perfect. It needs to be good enough that they can find what they need and the clothes are actually put away.

If an adult always finishes this part, the child is still relying on the adult for the final stage.

What if someone needs something urgently?

The weekly system is a default, not a prison.

If a child needs a particular sports kit tomorrow and it is dirty, they can wash it.

If they run out of underwear, they need to deal with the laundry.

If someone has been ill, gone swimming every day or had an unusually busy week, the normal schedule can change.

The important difference is who notices and solves the problem.

If the parent notices the empty underwear drawer and quietly puts on a load, the responsibility remains with the parent.

If the child notices and says, “I need to do a wash tonight,” the system is working.

There is more than one right system

There is no reason every family has to divide laundry in exactly the same way.

One family might have each child doing their own laundry once a week. Another might have two children sharing a laundry day. Another might have a parent doing most of the washing while the children are responsible for bringing their clothes to the laundry area and dealing with anything they need urgently.

All three can work.

The important question is not ”Who physically puts the clothes in the washing machine?”

It is ”Who is responsible for noticing, preparing and dealing with the laundry?”

You can keep doing the washing yourself and still remove a surprising amount of the mental load.

The system should get easier over time

You do not need to start with a child doing everything.

A younger child might begin by putting dirty clothes in the basket and bringing them to the laundry area.

Later, they can sort their clothes and help load the machine.

Eventually, they may be ready to manage their own laundry from basket to wardrobe.

Or they may simply continue with a family system where the parent does most of the washing but they are responsible for getting their clothes to the right place and dealing with their own last-minute needs.

The goal is not perfectly independent laundry.

It is to make sure one person is not automatically responsible for noticing, collecting, washing, folding and putting away everyone’s clothes.

That is enough to turn laundry from an invisible job into a shared part of family life.

What to try this week
  1. Give every person a named washing day, and say it out loud.
  2. Put a basket in each bedroom.
  3. State the basket rule once, then hold it.
  4. Redefine “done” as in the drawer, not clean.

Common questions

At what age can a child use the washing machine alone?

Most children can run a machine with supervision from about ten and alone from twelve, depending on the machine and the child. Do the first few together, write the programme on a card stuck to the machine, and accept that something pink will happen eventually.
