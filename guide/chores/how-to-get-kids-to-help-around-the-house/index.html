How to get kids to help around the house

It is faster to do it yourself. That is not impatience, it is simply true — and it is what makes the whole thing so easy to postpone.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 7 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

Children help when jobs have owners rather than rotas, a trigger that is not a parent’s voice, and a standard agreed once. The hard part is allowing it to be done badly: a job an adult finishes when the child is slow was never really the child’s. The window is early — including a four-year-old is far easier than recruiting a fourteen-year-old.

It is faster to do it yourself. That is not a failure of patience or a lack of principle; it is simply true. A four-year-old laying a table takes four times as long as an adult and gets the forks wrong. A seven-year-old wiping a surface leaves most of it wet and some of it dirty. A twelve-year-old loading a dishwasher will arrange it in a way that makes you want to say something.

So most parents, most evenings, do it themselves. It is quicker, the result is better, and there is no argument. The cost is invisible, which is exactly the problem: what you are actually buying with those saved minutes is a household in which the children do not know how any of it works.

The window is earlier than it feels

The instinct is to wait until they are capable. It is a reasonable instinct and it appears to be backwards.

Marty Rossmann, at the University of Minnesota, looked at data following 84 children from preschool through to their mid-twenties. Young adults who had started doing household work at three or four were doing better on several measures — relationships with family and friends, early academic and career progress, self-sufficiency — than those who had started later or not at all.

The part worth knowing is what happened at the other end. Children who did not begin until they were fifteen or sixteen did not simply gain less. The results went the other way.

That fits what most families find. A teenager asked to start contributing after fourteen years of not being asked experiences it as a new regime, and responds accordingly. They are not wrong to. The rules changed, and nobody consulted them.

Eighty-four children is a small study, it follows correlations rather than proving causes, and a household that involves small children in its work probably differs in other ways too. But it points the same direction as cross-cultural research on children’s helping, which finds that where young children are routinely included in real household work — however inefficiently — they volunteer help more readily as they get older, and where their participation is delayed until they are old enough to be useful, they help less.

Both suggest the same practical thing: it is far easier to include a four-year-old than to recruit a fourteen-year-old.

What you are buying is not the labour

A three-year-old carrying two forks to the table is not helping. It is slower than doing it yourself and the forks may not arrive. Nothing about that transaction makes sense as labour.

What is being built is an identity. Children who have always done things do not experience household work as an imposition, because they have no memory of a time when it was not simply what happens here. Children who are introduced to it at twelve experience it as something being done to them, and they are right.

This is why the early years are worth more than they look. The work a small child does is worthless. The habit is not.

Letting it be done badly is the whole skill

The most common way this fails has nothing to do with the child.

An area is handed over. It is done differently, and worse, and slowly. An adult steps in — sometimes to correct it, sometimes just to finish it, often with a sigh that says more than the correcting would have. And the child learns, accurately, that the job was never really theirs.

After that no amount of asking helps, because the arrangement everybody can see is that an adult will do it if it is not done well enough.

Accepting a worse outcome is therefore not a compromise you make around the edges. It is the thing that does the work. The table will be laid wrong. The floor will still have crumbs on it. The clothes will go in the drawer in a way you would not have chosen. All of that has to be allowed to stand, visibly, or nothing transfers.

There is a useful rule for the first month of any handover: correct nothing unless it is a safety issue or a deadline somebody outside the family is holding. After a month, raise one or two things, and raise them as preferences rather than errors.

What makes it stick

Beyond that, a few things separate the households where this works from the ones where it collapses by half-term.

Jobs need owners rather than rotas. “The dishwasher is Noah’s” builds a habit; “we take turns” builds a negotiation, every day, and somebody has to run it — which means a parent has acquired a job.

They need a trigger that is not a person. After dinner. When you come in. Before screens. Times require a clock and a reminder; events happen on their own.

The standard needs saying once, at the start, and then judging against. Wiped, not just cleared. Bin bag replaced. Agreeing it in advance turns a disagreement about character into a question about line four.

And it needs to be visible somewhere other than a parent’s voice. If the only way a child can find out what they are meant to be doing is to ask somebody, the job has not really moved — the doing has moved and the noticing has not.

The first three weeks are worse

It is worth saying plainly, because the first fortnight is when most families conclude it is not working.

Evenings will take longer. Things will be done badly. There will be at least one argument that is not really about the bins. Somebody will forget, and you will watch it not happen and have to decide whether to say something.

That period is the investment, and it is a real one. What it buys is not a tidier house this month. It is a fourteen-year-old who packs their own bag, a sixteen-year-old who can cook, and a household in which nobody has to be asked.

Six months in, the sign that it has worked is not a spotless kitchen. It is that nobody had to be asked.

What to try this week
  1. Convert one rotating job into a named, owned one.
  2. Attach it to a trigger rather than a time.
  3. Write what “done” means, once, where they can see it.
  4. Let each child pick one new job from a list.
  5. Decide in advance that you will not do it for them when they are slow.

Common questions

How many jobs should a child have?

Fewer than most charts suggest, and consistently. One or two daily jobs plus one weekly is plenty for a primary-age child. A long list without a system produces nothing; two jobs that always happen produce a habit.

Should I pay them?

Not for the basics of being a household member. Payment for genuinely extra work is reasonable. The evidence and a workable split.

What about teenagers who have never done anything?

Start with two jobs, negotiated rather than imposed, and be honest that it is a change: "we got this wrong, and it needs to be different now." Teenagers respond far better to a straight admission than to a new chart appearing on the fridge.
