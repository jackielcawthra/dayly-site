How to raise considerate children

Not politeness — noticing. They are different skills and only one of them generalises.

The Dayly Editors Published 11 August 2026 Updated 14 August 2026 3 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

Children become considerate through real responsibility for others, seeing consideration modelled, and having the effects of their actions made visible. Contributing to a household — where the work visibly benefits other people — appears to matter more than being asked to be kind.

Politeness is a set of forms. Consideration is noticing that other people have an inner life and adjusting for it. The second is harder, more valuable, and less directly teachable.

What seems to help

Work that visibly benefits others. There is a suggestive finding in the developmental literature here: household tasks that help the family — laying the table, making someone’s lunch — are more associated with children’s concern for others than self-care tasks are, which help only themselves (Grusec, Goodnow & Cohen, 1996).

The evidence is correlational and the mechanism is not certain. But it points at something practical: give jobs that obviously help someone specific, and say who they help.

Making the effect visible. Not “be kind” but “your brother couldn’t find his shoes and you helped — that’s why he wasn’t late.” Children are frequently unaware of the effects of their actions, and naming them is the teaching.

Modelling, including the parts they are not meant to see. How you talk about people who are not in the room is a stronger lesson than anything said to a child directly.

Being needed. A child who is genuinely relied upon behaves differently from one who is looked after. This is one of the underrated arguments for real household responsibility.

Perspective-taking, out loud. “How do you think she felt when that happened?” Not as an interrogation — as a normal way of talking about the world.

Worth knowing

Be careful with the guilt version. “Think how sad you’ve made me” produces compliance driven by anxiety rather than genuine concern, and it tends to make children less honest about what they have done rather than more considerate.

What consideration looks like at each age

By age
3–5Noticing someone is sad. Sharing sometimes. Helping when asked. Do not expect it reliably; the capacity is arriving.
6–9Helping without being asked, occasionally. Noticing when someone is left out. Being careful with other people’s things.
10–13Adjusting behaviour for other people — quieter when someone is working, patient with a younger sibling. Doing something unpleasant because someone needs it.
14+Acting on what they notice without being prompted. Standing up for someone. Being genuinely useful in a difficult week without being asked.

Siblings

The hardest and most useful arena, and worth being realistic about: siblings will fight, and that is normal rather than a failure of your teaching.

What helps: not casting them in fixed roles (the responsible one, the difficult one), refusing to adjudicate everything, and giving them things they have to do together and that only work if they cooperate.

The teenage dip

Consideration often appears to go backwards at thirteen or fourteen. Some of this is genuine — the social world reorganises and family drops down the list — and some is that it is now directed at friends rather than at you.

The evidence suggests it comes back. What matters in the interval is that consideration continues to be expected and modelled, without it becoming the subject of a running argument.

What to try this week
  1. Give one job that visibly helps a specific person, and say who it helps.
  2. Name one effect of your child’s behaviour on someone else, factually.
  3. Notice how you talk about people who are not in the room.
  4. Give siblings one task that only works if they cooperate.

Sources & further reading

  1. Grusec, J., Goodnow, J. & Cohen, L. (1996). Household work and the development of concern for others. Developmental Psychology
