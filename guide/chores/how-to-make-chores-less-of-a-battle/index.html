How to make chores less of a battle

Most chore arguments are not about chores. But some of them genuinely are, and those are fixable.

The Dayly Editors Published 10 June 2026 Updated 6 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

Reduce chore conflict by removing the negotiation (owned jobs, event triggers, agreed standards), giving children a real say in what they take on, and never doing the job yourself to make a point. When conflict persists it is usually about fairness or autonomy rather than the task.

Seven changes, roughly in the order of how much conflict each one removes.

1. Take away the daily negotiation

If whether it happens gets decided each day, you will have that discussion each day.

Owned jobs. Fixed triggers. Agreed standards.

Nothing to discuss means nothing to argue about.

2. Give them a real choice

Choosing from a list of five beats being handed one.

The work is identical. The ownership is not.

And they are more likely to do the thing they picked, even when they picked badly.

3. Stop asking in the middle of something

“Can you empty the dishwasher”, said while a child is deep in something else, produces resistance far out of proportion to the request.

That is not defiance. It is the cost of switching.

Use the transitions instead. When they come in. After dinner. Before screens.

4. Sometimes, do it alongside them

Especially the bigger jobs. Especially with the under-tens.

A parent working in the same room turns a punishment into a shared activity. It costs fifteen minutes.

5. Never do it yourself to make a point

The sighing, martyred version is the worst option available.

The job gets done. The lesson does not land. Everybody feels bad.

Leave it undone. It is still theirs, later.

6. Separate the job from the mood

A child who is tired, hungry or upset is not going to do a good job.

And insisting in that moment turns a small task into a large scene.

Delay it by twenty minutes. That is not a concession. It is timing.

7. Let things go in the hard weeks

Name the minimum that survives an exam week or a bad patch.

Then let the rest lapse deliberately, rather than by collapse.

Announcing the reduction is what keeps the system alive.

Try this

For one week, do not mention any job at the moment you notice it needs doing.

Wait for the next natural transition.

Count the arguments before and after.

When it is not about the chores

If the conflict survives all seven, the subject is probably something else.

Fairness between siblings. Almost always the first candidate. Children keep a detailed ledger.

Make the division visible — actually visible, written down — and be ready to find that they were right.

Autonomy. Particularly from about thirteen. A young person with very little control over their day will fight over the one thing they can control.

Give ground somewhere real, and the bins stop being symbolic.

Something bigger. Chores are a convenient proxy for an argument about school, friends, a separation, or how they are getting on with you.

If the intensity does not match the task, the task is not the subject.

In which case: have the other conversation. Hold a small non-negotiable minimum. And let the rest go for a few weeks.

It will come back more easily than it will be won.

What to try this week
  1. Stop asking mid-activity. Use transitions only.
  2. Offer a choice of jobs rather than an assignment.
  3. Leave one undone job undone, and let it wait for them.
  4. If the fight is bigger than the job, have the bigger conversation instead.
