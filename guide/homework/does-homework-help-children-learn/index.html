Homework: what actually works?

The evidence is weaker and more interesting than either side of the argument admits.

The Dayly Editors Published 15 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 6 min read Ages 5–18
The short answer

The evidence for homework is much stronger for secondary-age students than for primary, where the average effect on attainment is close to zero. What matters more than quantity is the type: short, spaced, retrieval-based practice helps; long, unsupervised, open-ended tasks mostly do not. For younger children, reading with an adult has better evidence than homework of any other kind.

This is a subject where confident claims outrun the evidence in both directions. What follows tries to separate four things: what is reasonably well supported, what is contested, what is opinion, and what to actually do.

What the evidence reasonably supports

Age matters, a lot. The relationship between homework and attainment is consistently stronger for secondary students than primary. The Education Endowment Foundation’s summary of the UK evidence puts the average impact at around five months’ additional progress for secondary pupils, and “low or no impact” on average at primary (EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit).

Type matters more than quantity. Short, focused tasks that require pupils to retrieve what they know outperform long, open-ended ones. Homework that extends practice of something already taught does better than homework that introduces something new.

Quality of the task beats time spent. Studies that separate “time on homework” from “homework completion and quality” generally find the second is the better predictor.

For primary-age children, reading is the strongest bet. Regular reading with an adult has a more robust evidence base than most other forms of primary homework.

What the research says

Where researchers disagree. Cooper’s influential syntheses report positive correlations between homework and achievement that grow with age (Cooper, Robinson & Patall, 2006). Others argue the primary-age correlations are close to zero, that the studies are mostly correlational, and that reverse causation is plausible — more able and better-supported students do more homework rather than homework making them more able.

What nobody has shown: that a specific quantity of homework causes a specific gain for a specific child. Beware anyone quoting a precise figure.

What is opinion

That homework builds character, discipline or resilience. It might; the evidence is thin and mostly anecdotal. It is a reasonable belief and it should be labelled as one.

That homework is inherently harmful. Also not supported. What is documented is that excessive homework is associated with stress and reduced sleep in older students — a dose problem rather than a category problem.

What actually helps, in practice

Short and spaced beats long and blocked

Twenty minutes on three evenings beats an hour on Sunday. Spacing practice over time is one of the better-supported findings in the whole of learning science (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

Retrieval beats rereading

Closing the book and trying to remember is more effective than reading it again, and it feels worse, which is why children avoid it. How to do it at the kitchen table.

A fixed time and place beats willpower

The strongest predictor of homework actually happening in a household is not motivation. It is whether there is a routine. Building one.

Independence beats supervision, once the routine exists

Parental involvement helps — interest, structure, being available. Parental control — sitting over it, correcting it, doing parts of it — is associated with worse outcomes, particularly as children get older (Patall, Cooper & Robinson, 2008).

What often does not work

  • Doing it with them every night, past about age nine
  • Correcting everything. The teacher needs to see what the child can do, not what you can
  • Long unsupervised projects with no interim steps, which reliably become a parent’s project
  • on the last night
  • Rewarding completion. It shifts the point of the activity, and it stops working
  • Fighting about it every evening. The cost to the relationship exceeds the value of the worksheet

What to do by age

By age
5–7Reading together, most days, plus whatever short thing school sets. Ten to fifteen minutes total. Do not let it become a battle at this age — the evidence does not justify the cost.
8–11A fixed slot, twenty to thirty minutes, at a table, with you nearby but not on top of it. Reading continues.
12–14Their own slot and their own plan. You are available, not present. Long projects broken into steps on the day they are set.
15–18Entirely theirs. Your job is the conditions — a quiet place, food, sleep, and not adding pressure to an already-pressured system.

If homework is destroying your evenings

Two things worth saying plainly.

First, tell the school. Teachers are usually surprised to learn that a twenty-minute task took two hours, and often the task can be adjusted. This is a normal conversation, not a complaint.

Second, for primary-age children in particular, the evidence does not support paying a heavy price in family relationships for homework completion. A short note to the teacher saying you stopped at twenty minutes is a legitimate response, and most schools would rather have that than a nightly row.

What to try this week
  1. Pick a fixed time and place, and use it every school night, even when there is nothing set.
  2. Swap one rereading session for retrieval — book closed, tell me what you remember.
  3. On the next long project, write the steps the day it is set.
  4. Move from sitting with them to being available in the next room.
  5. If it is taking far longer than school intends, tell the teacher this week.

Common questions

How much homework should a child have?

The commonly quoted "10 minutes per year group" rule is a convention rather than a research finding — see where it came from. It is a reasonable sanity check, not a target.

Should I check my child’s homework?

Look at whether it is done rather than whether it is right, especially past age nine. A teacher needs to see the child's actual understanding, including the errors — corrected homework hides exactly the information the teacher needs.

My child says they have no homework, every night. What do I do?

Check with the school once so you know what is actually being set, then use the fixed slot regardless — reading, or reviewing something from the week. A slot that only exists when there is homework never becomes a routine.

Is it worth arguing about homework at primary age?

Rarely. The average effect on attainment at primary is small, and the cost of a nightly conflict to a child's attitude to school is real. Build the routine, keep it short, and tell the teacher when it is not working.
