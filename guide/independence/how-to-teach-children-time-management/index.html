How to teach children time management

Ask a ten-year-old how long it takes to get ready and you may get five minutes — whether the answer is five minutes or twenty-five.

The Dayly Editors Published 27 February 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 7 min read Ages 6–18
The short answer

Children judge duration badly because time is abstract and nobody teaches it. The work is building a sense of it: guess how long something will take, then time it and compare. Then estimating, then planning backwards from a deadline with space left in it. Lateness is usually a misjudged estimate rather than a discipline problem.

Most children have a surprisingly poor sense of how long things take. Ask a ten-year-old how long it takes to get ready in the morning and you may get five minutes, whether the answer is five minutes or twenty-five.

That is not necessarily laziness or carelessness. Time is abstract, children have less experience of it, and most of us are never actually taught how to judge it. We simply become better at it by living through it.

Time management is therefore less about teaching children to be efficient and more about helping them build a sense of time they can eventually use for themselves.

First, help them feel time

Before a child can plan an hour, they need some idea of what an hour feels like.

One of the simplest ways to teach this is to ask them to guess how long something will take, then time it. How long does getting dressed take? How long does tidying the room take? How long do you think your homework will take?

Do not turn the answer into a lesson. Just compare the estimate with what actually happened.

Doing this regularly is surprisingly effective because children begin to build their own reference points. Five minutes stops being an abstract number and starts to mean something.

Timers can help too, particularly for younger children. A visible timer makes time concrete: they can see five minutes disappearing rather than being told, again, that they have five minutes.

This is also why “five minutes until we leave” is more useful than “hurry up”. The child is being given something to understand rather than another instruction to obey.

Then teach them to estimate

Once a child has begun to develop a sense of duration, make estimation part of ordinary life.

Before homework, ask how long they think it will take. Before getting ready, ask when they think they will be finished. Then, afterwards, compare.

The interesting part is not whether they were right. It is whether their estimates gradually become better.

Children often avoid something because they imagine it will take much longer than it actually does. A piece of homework that feels like an hour can turn out to take fifteen minutes. Once they have experienced that several times, the task becomes easier to start.

They are learning something more useful than “don’t procrastinate”. They are learning how to judge the size of a job before they begin it.

The next step is planning backwards

This becomes particularly important as schoolwork gets longer and deadlines become less forgiving.

A child who is told on Monday that a project is due on Friday may hear only “Friday”. An organised adult automatically thinks about the steps in between. A child has to learn to do that.

Start with the deadline and work backwards.

If the project has four parts and each might take twenty minutes, ask when those parts need to happen. If something needs to be handed in Friday morning, there should be time to finish it before Friday morning. There is more on this in planning long-term school projects.

Do this together at first. Write it down. Ask the child what they think needs to happen and when. Over time, move from making the plan with them to asking the questions and letting them make it.

Eventually, the plan should belong to them.

There are two things that make a difference here. Leave some space: a plan that finishes exactly at the deadline has no room for a bad evening, a forgotten book or a piece of work that takes twice as long as expected. And put the plan somewhere visible, because something that exists only in a conversation is very easy to forget.

The calendar should become theirs

There is a point at which parents can accidentally make children less organised by being too organised for them.

If you keep the calendar, remember the deadlines, tell them what is happening tomorrow and remind them when they need to start, the child may appear to be managing perfectly well. But the system is still running through you.

As children get older, their calendar should increasingly become somewhere they look themselves.

That does not mean handing a six-year-old a calendar and expecting them to run their week. At six or seven, the useful work is mostly about understanding duration and sequences. Around nine or ten, children can begin estimating more reliably and looking ahead at what is coming. By the early teenage years, they can increasingly manage their own deadlines and work backwards from them.

The ages are not rules. Some children will be ready earlier, some later. What matters is that the responsibility gradually moves in their direction.

Being late is not a character flaw

It is tempting to treat lateness as a discipline problem. Sometimes a child simply has not got ready when they were asked to. But often the underlying problem is much less dramatic: they did not understand how long getting ready would take.

Punishment does not teach estimation.

If a child is repeatedly late, help them look backwards. What time did they need to leave? What had to happen before that? How long did each part actually take?

The next morning, they have something concrete to work with.

This is also why elaborate systems tend not to help for long. A beautiful planner with twelve colours and several categories may be satisfying to an adult and completely irrelevant to a child. The best system is usually the simplest one they will actually use.

Let them have some ownership of it. If their system is a notebook, a phone calendar, a whiteboard or a collection of sticky notes, it does not need improving simply because you would organise it differently.

Teenagers need something slightly different

By the teenage years, time management is no longer just about getting out of the house on time. There may be several deadlines competing with one another, social plans, activities, exams and increasingly independent decisions about how to spend their time.

At this stage, prioritising matters as much as planning. A teenager needs to learn that something being urgent does not automatically make it the most important thing to do.

Sleep matters here too. A tired teenager is not going to manage time particularly well, however sophisticated their planner is. Protecting enough sleep is a more useful intervention than finding a better productivity system.

And the system needs to be theirs. The goal is not to get them to use the calendar you would choose. It is to help them develop a way of organising their time that they can carry with them when you are no longer there to remind them.

The parent eventually has to step back

This is the part that can be hardest.

A child learning time management will get it wrong. They will underestimate the homework, forget the deadline, spend too long getting ready and occasionally discover at 9pm that something is due tomorrow.

If every mistake is prevented, they never get the information that would have taught them.

That does not mean leaving children to struggle when they need help. It means distinguishing between a problem that needs an adult and one that a child can reasonably learn from.

The goal is not a child who never needs reminding. It is a child who gradually becomes the person doing the noticing, estimating and planning.

At first, you are beside them. Then you ask the questions. Eventually, you stop asking.

And one day they look at the week and work out what needs to happen without you.

What to try this week
  1. Do guess-and-time on three ordinary activities and write both numbers down.
  2. Ask the estimate question before every homework session for a week.
  3. Plan one deadline backwards together, on paper.
  4. Leave their system alone, however much better yours would be.

Common questions

What age can children start managing their own time?

Duration awareness from about six, estimating from nine, planning backwards from twelve, and multiple competing deadlines from about fifteen. Expect the consistency to lag the capability by two or three years.
