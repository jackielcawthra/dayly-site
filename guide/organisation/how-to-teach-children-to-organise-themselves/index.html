How to teach children to organise themselves

A disorganised child usually has an organised parent doing the organising. Here is how to swap that round.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 February 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 6 min read Ages 4–17
The short answer

Children organise themselves when the information is outside their heads and inside their reach: a visible list, a fixed order, and a check the night before rather than the morning of. Build the external structure first, hand over one step at a time, and expect the skill to arrive years before the consistency does.

Almost every child described as disorganised lives with somebody who is organising them.

That is not a criticism. The alternative is missed swimming lessons and forgotten cakes.

But it is worth saying out loud, because it explains why the disorganisation carries on. The child has never actually needed the skill. There has always been a working system in the room.

And the system was a person.

Organisation is external before it is internal

Adults who look organised are not remembering more than anybody else.

They are writing more down. Calendars, lists, alarms, a place by the door for the things that leave the house with them.

Children are expected to do it from memory. And then judged when they cannot.

So it is worth saying plainly: the working memory of an eight-year-old cannot hold a five-step morning. It is not supposed to.

The order is not “learn to remember, then use a list”. It is “use a list, and let the remembering arrive later, if it ever does”.

What the research says

Executive function — working memory, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility — develops gradually into early adulthood, with the biggest gains in early childhood and again in adolescence (Diamond, 2013).

Which means that for most of childhood, the system should be doing the remembering. Not the child.

The four things every system needs

One place. Not a wall chart plus a note plus a reminder from you. One place a child checks. Two places is zero places.

A fixed order. The same sequence every day. Order removes decisions, and decisions are exactly what children run out of at 07:20.

A trigger in the world. “After you brush your teeth” beats “at 7:30”. A clock is an abstraction to a seven-year-old. The toothbrush is real.

A check the night before. This is the highest-value habit in the whole article.

An evening check finds problems while there is still time to solve them. A morning check finds problems while you are putting your coat on.

Building it, one step at a time

Start with one part of the day.

Mornings are the obvious candidate and also the hardest. If things are tense, start with the evening instead — lower stakes, and it makes the morning easier anyway.

Write the sequence with your child, not for them. Five or six steps at most. Use their words: “bag by the door”, not “prepare belongings”. If they are young, use pictures, and let them draw them.

Then run it for two weeks without changing anything.

Resist improving it. A mediocre routine the child helped write beats an excellent one imposed on them, because the first one is theirs.

Handing it over

Work through the four stages. Do it together. Then watch and prompt. Then check afterwards. Then stop.

And the prompt that actually transfers is always a question.

“What’s next on your list?” — not “put your shoes on”.

“What day is tomorrow? What does that mean?” — not “you’ve got swimming”.

“Have you checked your list?” — not “have you got your reading folder?”

Answers make you the system. Questions point at the system.

The five skills underneath it

Organisation is not one skill. It is five, and children pick them up at different rates.

Lists. How to write one, how to check it off, how to keep it short enough to be usable. More here.

Time. How long things actually take. Most children are wildly wrong about this, in both directions.

Sequencing. What has to happen before what. Packing a bag before finding the kit is the classic failure, and it is a sequencing problem rather than a memory one.

Anticipation. Looking at tomorrow, today. The hardest of the five, and the most valuable.

Recovery. What to do when it has already gone wrong. That is a skill too, and it is the one you teach by not rescuing.

Try this

Do the estimating exercise this week.

Ask your child how long it takes them to get ready in the morning. Then time it. Then show them both numbers.

Do not make a point of it. The number does the work.

By age, roughly

By age
4–6A picture routine on the wall. Two or three steps. An adult narrates it out loud each time.
7–9A written list they tick. A packed bag the night before. A named day for kit. They still need the prompt to look at the list.
10–12A weekly view: what is on which day, and what is needed for it. Their own reminders. They spot a clash before it happens.
13–15Their own calendar and their own deadlines, including school ones weeks out. They plan backwards from a date.
16+A system they built, which you are not invited to improve.

What to expect

The skill arrives long before the consistency does.

A twelve-year-old can know exactly how to organise themselves and still forget three times in a fortnight. That is not evidence the system has failed.

Adolescence is particularly rough on the parts of the brain that do this, and a dip at thirteen or fourteen is common enough to plan for.

The measure that matters is not “did they forget”.

It is: when they forgot, did they notice — and did they fix it themselves?

What to try this week
  1. Pick one part of the day and write a five-step list with your child, in their words.
  2. Move one morning check to the night before.
  3. Answer every organisational question with a question for one week.
  4. Do the time-estimate exercise once, and say nothing about the result.

Common questions

How do I get my child to actually use the list?

Attach checking it to something that already happens — after teeth, after dinner, when they come down. Lists fail because nothing triggers them, not because children dislike lists. And keep it to five or six lines; a long list is a wall of text and gets ignored.

My child is very disorganised compared to their sibling. Is something wrong?

The variation between siblings at the same age is genuinely large and mostly normal. If disorganisation is severe, persistent across every setting, and causing real difficulty at school, it is worth raising with the school or your GP — but the first move is always to check whether they have external structure or are being asked to do it from memory.

Should young children use apps or paper?

Whatever they will actually look at. Paper on a wall has a real advantage for younger children: it is always visible and requires no unlocking, no login and no decision to open it. Shared apps come into their own once children have their own phone and a week complicated enough to need one.
