The actual problem

It is not that you are disorganised.

It is that a family generates hundreds of small pieces of information a week, almost none of them arrive with a deadline attached, and they all end up in the same head.

Knowing

Which child needs what, on which day, in which bag. Nobody wrote it down because you already knew.

Noticing

That the shoes are too small, the passport expires in June, the dentist never got rebooked.

Deciding

What is for dinner, whether Thursday works, which club to drop, who takes which car.

Remembering

The form, the money, the costume, the library book, the thing somebody said at the gate.

Chasing

Homework, kit, jobs, and the same four sentences every morning.

Coordinating

Two adults, several children, four calendars and one car.

What changes

Three things, in the first month.

  • It stops living in your head.Everything — the week, the jobs, the kit days, the recurring things — is somewhere both adults and every child can see without asking you.
  • Responsibilities get owners.Not tasks handed out each day. Areas, held by people, including the noticing. That is the version that can actually be given away.
  • The children take some of it.Their kit, their week, their area, their deadlines. Not as a favour to you — as practice they need anyway.

If you share this with a partner

The screen that starts the conversation.

The Everyone view shows who owns what across the whole household — including the areas nobody has claimed, which are usually the ones causing the arguments.

It is difficult to argue about a division of labour in the abstract. It is much easier to look at a list together and move four things.

How to actually hand over an area →
Doing it alone

If there is no one to share it with.

Single parents carry all of it, and no division fixes that. Two things still help.

Getting it out of your head and into a system — because the failure mode of a solo load is not unfairness, it is that one forgotten thing takes the week with it.

And moving responsibilities to the children earlier than is typical. In most single-parent households this happens anyway; doing it deliberately is considerably better than doing it in a crisis.
