Holiday and weekend routines

Holidays do not need a timetable. They need three fixed points and nothing else.

The Dayly Editors Published 24 June 2026 Updated 10 August 2026 2 min read
The short answer

Keep wake time, meals and bedtime roughly anchored during holidays and weekends, and let everything else go. Re-tighten the sleep schedule two weeks before term, not the night before.

Two failure modes at either end. Families who run the school timetable through the holidays, which is exhausting and misses the point. And families who abandon everything, which produces a September that takes a fortnight to recover from.

The middle is three anchors.

The three anchors

Wake time, roughly. Within an hour or so of the usual. This is the one that matters most, because everything else follows it, and because a four-hour drift is what makes the return so brutal.

Meals at roughly normal times. Not the same food, not at a table, not necessarily cooked. Just not at random.

A bedtime that exists. Later is fine. Undefined is not.

Everything else — jobs, homework routines, tidying, screen rules — can be relaxed deliberately, and saying so out loud is what makes it a decision rather than a collapse.

Try this

Say it explicitly at the start of the holiday: “For the next two weeks, the jobs are just X and Y.” Naming the reduction means the system is paused, not broken, and it restarts without a negotiation.

Weekends

The same, compressed. The main thing to protect is the wake time — a weekend lie-in of three hours makes Monday considerably worse, which is a real and well-documented effect.

One useful weekend structure that is not a timetable:

  • Saturday morning: the household things. Jobs, kit, laundry, the shop. Everyone, then done.
  • Saturday afternoon and Sunday: genuinely free.
  • Sunday late afternoon: the reset, thirty minutes, and then
  • the evening is calm.

The return

The Sunday-night cliff is avoidable, and the fix starts about two weeks out:

  • Move bedtime fifteen minutes earlier every two or three days
  • Restart the wake-up in the last few days, not on the morning itself
  • Rebuild the evening routine before term starts, so it is not new on the first Monday
  • Do the practical preparation in the last few days rather than the last evening

The four-week back-to-school plan.

Long holidays

Six weeks with no structure is difficult for children — some genuinely need more shape than a summer provides, and behaviour deteriorates without it.

A light frame that helps: one thing in the morning that is not a screen, one outing or activity most days, and a rough rhythm to the day. Not a timetable. A shape.

What to try this week
  1. Name the three anchors and hold only those.
  2. Say out loud which jobs are paused, so the pause is deliberate.
  3. Protect the weekend wake time within an hour of usual.
  4. Start the bedtime glide two weeks before term.
