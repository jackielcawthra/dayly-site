The two problems, and what each needs

Nightly homework needs a routine: a fixed trigger, a fixed place, food first, and a defined end. The strongest predictor of homework happening in a household is not motivation — it is whether it is decided each evening or was decided in September.

Long projects need decomposition. “Do a project on the rainforest” contains no first move and no trigger, which is why it gets started the night before. Breaking it into five dated steps on the day it is set is the whole intervention.

Dayly holds both: the routine as a recurring slot, and the project as steps that appear on specific days on the child’s own list.

What a child sees

Tonight’s work, tonight. Not the whole month — that is a wall, and a wall gets ignored.

The next step of a project, on the day it is meant to happen, sized so that it can be finished in one sitting.

Deadlines that arrive before they are due, so the panic version does not happen.

What a parent does

Less than you might expect, and the evidence supports that. Parental involvement — interest, structure, availability — is associated with better outcomes. Parental control — sitting over it, correcting it — is associated with worse ones, and the gap widens as children get older (Patall et al., 2008).

So Dayly gives you the conditions, not the content: the slot exists, the plan exists, the deadline is visible. What happens at the table is between the child and the work.

Common questions

Does it track what homework is set?

Not automatically — schools do not expose that. You or the child add it, ideally the day it is set, which is also when the plan should be made.

Will it nag them?

No. It shows tonight’s work on their list. Being nagged by an app is not much better than being nagged by a parent.
