How to keep track of school newsletters

The information is not missing. It is in a PDF you opened on a phone while walking.

The Dayly Editors Published 3 February 2026 Updated 8 August 2026 2 min read
The short answer

Read the newsletter once a week at a fixed time with the calendar open, extract only what applies to your family, act on anything that takes under two minutes immediately, and put everything else on a date. Reading it on a phone while walking is the failure mode.

Almost nobody misses the newsletter. They read it — on a phone, one-handed, walking somewhere — and then it is gone, and three weeks later the trip is tomorrow.

The problem is not attention. It is that reading and recording were separated by a gap in which the information evaporated.

The routine

Ten minutes, once a week, same slot, with the calendar open. Sunday evening works for most families because it is next to the rest of the week’s planning.

Four passes:

  1. Dates. Anything with a date goes on the calendar now, with the child’s name on it.
  2. Actions. Forms, money, replies. Anything under two minutes: do it now. Anything longer: on the
  3. calendar, on a specific day, not “this week”.
  4. Things to buy. Straight onto the shopping list.
  5. Everything else. Skim, and let go. Most of a newsletter is not about your family.
Try this

Read it on a laptop, sitting down, with the calendar visible. The single change from phone-while- walking to laptop-with-calendar catches more than any organising system.

Handling the channels

Most schools use more than one, which is the real problem. A workable approach:

  • Decide which channel is authoritative — usually the newsletter or the school app — and treat
  • the rest as gossip until confirmed.
  • The class chat is not a source of record. Useful for “does anyone know if…” and hopeless as
  • a place to keep information. Never rely on being able to find something in it again.
  • One adult per organisation. School to one, clubs to the other. Splitting each one produces
  • duplicated effort and missed items in equal measure.

The two-minute rule

If acting takes less than two minutes — signing, replying, adding a date — do it while the newsletter is open. Filing something to do later creates a second task: remembering the first one.

When you have missed something

Ask. “Sorry, did I miss something about the trip?” is a normal sentence and no school thinks less of you for it. Most parents have missed the same thing, and the class chat is full of people quietly hoping someone else will ask first.

What to try this week
  1. Book the ten-minute slot in the same place every week.
  2. Read it on a screen with the calendar open, sitting down.
  3. Do everything under two minutes on the spot.
  4. Decide which channel is authoritative and stop tracking the others.
