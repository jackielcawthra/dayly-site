Mental Load

What is the mental load?

The dentist texted three weeks ago. You saw the message, thought I must remember that, and somehow you still haven’t booked it.

The Dayly Editors Published 4 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 7 min read Ages all
The short answer

The mental load is not the washing or the cooking. It is everything around those jobs: noticing what needs doing, remembering it, thinking ahead, deciding, and checking that things happened. In many families it settles on the mother, and nobody ever decided it should.

The reading folder needs to go back on Wednesday. One child has outgrown their shoes. There is a birthday party on Saturday and nobody has bought a present. The dentist texted three weeks ago. You saw the message, thought I must remember that, and somehow you still haven’t booked it.

None of these things is particularly difficult.

But somebody has to notice them. Somebody has to remember them. Somebody has to know what needs to happen next.

In many families, that somebody is the mother.

Not because anyone decided it. She answered the first school email, so the school has her number. She joined the class group. She booked the dentist. She knows which child has swimming on Thursday and which one needs a packed lunch on Friday.

Then, because she knows, people ask her.

Where is my kit?

Did you see the email?

What time do we leave?

Do I need anything tomorrow?

Over time, one person becomes the place where the answers live.

That is the mental load.

It is not just doing the washing or making dinner. It is everything around those jobs: noticing what needs doing, remembering it, thinking ahead, making decisions and checking that things actually happened.

The work is easy to miss because, when it is done well, nothing happens.

The form gets signed. The kit appears in the bag. The birthday present is bought.

Nobody sees the things that were remembered because they were remembered.

He sees it. So why is it still hers?

There is a familiar explanation for this. Perhaps women simply notice more. Perhaps men genuinely don’t see the mess, the empty fridge or the school email waiting to be dealt with.

But research suggests something more complicated.

In one experiment, people were shown photographs of the same room and asked to judge how clean it was. The room was identical. What changed was the name attached to it: in some cases it belonged to Jennifer; in others, to John.

People judged Jennifer’s room more harshly.

The mess was the same.

He sees it. He just isn’t necessarily the person who gets judged for it.

And that distinction matters. If you know that tomorrow’s swimming trip is yours to sort out, you read the school email differently. You notice the time. You check whether the kit still fits. You remember the goggles are somewhere in the car.

Responsibility changes what you notice.

The work happens before the work

Sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed couples about the thinking that happens around family decisions. She found four parts to it: anticipating what will be needed, identifying the options, making the decision and checking afterwards that it happened.

Mothers did more of all four.

But the interesting part was where the imbalance appeared. Couples often made decisions together. The thinking before the decision and the checking afterwards were much less likely to be shared.

Two people can sit down and decide which school to choose. That looks shared.

But one person may already have spent weeks reading the information, remembering the deadline, talking to other parents and wondering what happens if they miss it.

The decision is shared.

The work that made the decision possible was not.

Half the chores. Still carrying it all.

A 2024 study of mothers of young children found that mothers reported doing 72.6% of the cognitive work in their households — the remembering, planning and organising — compared with 27.4% for their partners.

The physical work was divided 63.6% to 36.4%.

And the difference mattered. Mothers carrying more of the cognitive work also reported more stress, depressive symptoms and burnout, as well as lower relationship satisfaction.

This is why “but I do half the housework” doesn’t necessarily answer the question.

The dishwasher can be divided exactly in half.

Someone still has to notice that there are no tablets left.

Nobody chose this. It just happened.

Usually, nobody chooses it.

Someone is home more when the children are small. Someone takes the first parental leave. Someone has the more flexible job. Someone joins the school group because somebody has to.

Then that person knows more.

Because they know more, they are asked more.

Because they are asked more, it becomes easier to keep knowing.

And eventually there is a person who remembers the dentist, the swimming kit, the birthday present, the school trip and the fact that the passport expires in June.

There is no meeting at which the family agrees that this person will become its memory.

It simply happens.

How to stop being the answer

The answer is not to divide up more individual tasks.

“Can you do the shopping?” still leaves someone responsible for noticing that the food is running out, thinking about what is needed, making the list and checking that it happened.

A better handover is an area.

Food is yours.

Sport is yours.

School is yours.

The whole thing: the noticing, the planning and the doing.

It will be done differently. That is part of handing it over.

And children can have their own share too. Their swimming kit. Their school bag. Their birthday invitations. Their responsibilities at home.

Not because children should run the household, but because learning to notice what belongs to you is part of growing up.

The other important thing is to make the information visible.

Because there is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from being the person everyone asks.

You know the answer because you had to know the answer.

You remembered because someone had to remember.

You knew what was coming because, somewhere in the back of your mind, you were already preparing for it.

There is no app that can decide who should carry responsibility in a family. That part still belongs to the people in it.

But once the information is somewhere everyone can see it, one person no longer has to be the family’s memory.

That is one of the things Dayly is trying to make easier.

What to try this week
  1. Write down every area — not task — that keeps a household running. Aim for twenty lines. Do it separately from your partner, then compare.
  2. Pick the one area you would most like to stop holding. Hand over the whole thing, including the noticing.
  3. Agree out loud that it will be done differently, and that you will not correct it for a month.
  4. Give each child one area that is genuinely theirs, with the consequence attached.
  5. Put the areas somewhere everyone can see them, so nobody has to be asked.

Common questions

Is the mental load the same as multitasking?

No. Multitasking is doing several things at once. The mental load is holding responsibility for things that are not happening yet — remembering that they exist, tracking when they will matter, and deciding what to do about them. You can be sitting still and carrying an enormous mental load.

Is this only about mothers?

No, and framing it that way makes it harder to fix. The pattern is structural: whoever becomes the default point of contact accumulates the load. Research does find it lands disproportionately on mothers in different-sex couples, but single parents carry all of it, and in plenty of households it is the father. The useful question is not who is naturally better at it — it is which structure put it all in one place.

How do I explain it to a partner who does not see the problem?

Lists work better than arguments. Write down the areas separately, then compare. Most of the disagreement disappears when both people can see that one column contains twenty items and the other contains six — and the conversation moves on to who takes what.

Can an app fix this?

Not on its own. Software makes responsibility visible and stops it living in one head, which is the necessary condition — but the handover is a decision two adults make, and the standard-lowering is the hard part. Dayly is a place to keep it. It cannot want it for you.
