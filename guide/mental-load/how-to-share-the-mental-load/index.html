How to share the mental load

Delegating tasks does not share the load — it makes you a manager. Here is the version that works.

The Dayly Editors Published 11 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 6 min read
The short answer

Share the mental load by transferring whole areas of responsibility rather than individual tasks. Write down every area, split them by preference and capacity rather than fairness arithmetic, agree that the new owner sets the standard, and give it a month before you judge it. Anything you still have to remember to ask about has not actually been handed over.

Most advice about this stops at “communicate more”.

Which is true, and no help at all on a Tuesday.

What follows is a method. Five steps, one uncomfortable conversation, and a month of holding your tongue.

Step 1 — Write down the areas, separately

Not tasks. Areas.

“Food”, not “cook dinner on Tuesday”. An area is a standing responsibility, and it includes noticing when something needs doing.

Do this apart from each other. Then swap lists.

Two things usually happen. One list is longer. And each of you has written down something the other never thought of, which is the whole problem, sitting there in one observation.

A workable set of areas for a family with school-age children:

  • School communication · Term dates and childcare · Homework and reading
  • Clubs, sport and kit · Food and shopping · Meals and cooking
  • Laundry and clothes sizes · Cleaning and the state of rooms · Bins and recycling
  • Health and appointments · Birthdays, presents and cards · Social calendar and visitors
  • Holidays and travel · Money, bills and subscriptions · The house: repairs, services, admin
  • The car · Pets · Screen time and devices
Try this

Print the list. Put one name against each line — the person who currently notices when it needs doing, not the person who does it.

The lines with no name are the ones that get dropped. They usually explain the last three arguments you had.

Step 2 — Divide by appetite, then capacity, then fairness

Fifty-fifty on every area sounds equitable and works badly. It splits the noticing in two, and then nobody is quite sure whose turn it is to notice.

A better order.

Appetite first. Someone genuinely does not mind the food shop. Give it to them whole.

Then capacity. Whoever has the day that bends — the shorter commute, the quieter afternoon — takes what has to happen on weekdays.

Then fairness. Split what is left as evenly as you can.

Revisit it every term. Not every week. An area needs a long enough run to become automatic, and a fortnight is not long enough.

Step 3 — Hand over the noticing, in writing

The handover is a sentence, and it should be explicit enough to feel slightly awkward.

“Sport is yours. That means kit, times, fees, lifts, and knowing when the boots stop fitting. I will not be tracking any of it.”

That last clause is the one that matters.

Without it you have a co-manager. And two managers is worse than one.

Step 4 — Let the standard move

This is where most handovers die.

The new owner does it differently. A different shop, a different day, a lower bar, a deadline that was nearly missed.

And if every difference gets corrected, the message is that the area was lent rather than given. It comes back within a fortnight.

The rule that works: correct nothing for a month. Unless it is a safety issue, or a deadline somebody outside the family is holding.

After a month, raise two things at most. Raise them as preferences, not as errors.

Worth knowing

This is genuinely hard, and it is worth saying why.

Handing over an area means accepting a worse outcome for a while, in exchange for a permanently smaller load. People who are good at running households are often bad at that trade, because they can see the worse outcome coming and it costs them something real.

Step 5 — Put it somewhere neither of you owns

While the division lives in a conversation, one person is still the archive.

Written down — a shared list, a whiteboard, a shared app — it becomes a fact either of you can look up without asking the other.

Here is the test. Could you go away for a week without briefing anybody?

If the answer is no, the information is still in your head, and the division has not actually happened.

What about the children?

They are not staff. They are also not guests.

A child’s areas are the third column of that list, and they are the column that grows.

By age
4–6Their shoes and coat, their plate to the sink, one job at the table.
7–10Their school bag, their kit, their reading, one room-sized job in the house.
11–13Their own washing, their week, their homework deadlines, a meal they can cook.
14–17Their diary, their appointments, their revision plan, their packing, their money.

Every area they take is one you stop holding. And the practice is the point, rather than free labour.

When you are doing it alone

Single parents carry all of it, and no amount of dividing fixes that.

Two things still help.

Get it out of your head and into something you can look at. The failure mode of a solo load is not unfairness — it is that one forgotten thing takes the whole week with it.

And move areas to the children earlier than feels usual. In most single-parent households this happens anyway. Doing it deliberately beats doing it in a crisis.

How you will know it worked

Somebody else notices the thing before you mention it.

You get asked about your areas, and not about theirs.

A week goes by and you did not think about the dentist once.

And the children ask each other, or ask the list, instead of asking you.

What to try this week
  1. Both write the area list separately tonight. Twenty lines each, ten minutes, no discussion until both are finished.
  2. Compare them. Note the lines only one of you wrote — those are the invisible ones.
  3. Hand over one whole area this week, with the noticing included, out loud.
  4. Agree the no-correction month explicitly, so it is a rule rather than a mood.
  5. Write the division down somewhere neither of you has to be asked to see.

Common questions

What if my partner agrees and then nothing changes?

Usually the handover was a task, not an area — "can you do the shop this week" instead of "food is yours". Tasks come back automatically because the noticing never moved. Re-do it as an area, in writing, with the sentence about you not tracking it.

How long before it feels normal?

Most households report the wobble lasting three to six weeks. The first fortnight is the worst, because the old owner is still noticing everything and biting their tongue. If it is still chaotic after two months, the area was probably too big — split it.

Is it fair to give children responsibilities to reduce my load?

Both things can be true. Children need practice at running their own lives, and that practice happens to reduce what you carry. The line to watch is whether the responsibility is age-appropriate and theirs — looking after their own kit is theirs; being the person who remembers a younger sibling's kit is not.
