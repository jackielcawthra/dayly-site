How to create a homework routine that holds

Motivation is not the variable. The routine is.

The Dayly Editors Published 12 April 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read Ages 6–18
The short answer

A homework routine needs a fixed trigger, a fixed place, food beforehand, a defined end point, and to run on evenings when there is nothing set. Hand it over in stages: sit with them, then be nearby, then be available, then be absent.

The households where homework happens reliably are not the ones with more motivated children. They are the ones where it is not decided each evening.

The five components

1. A trigger, not a time. “After snack” beats “at half past four”. Times require someone watching a clock; that someone is you.

2. A place. The same one. A table, decent light, everything needed within reach. Phone in another room — this matters far more for teenagers than any other single factor.

3. Food first. Non-negotiable. Most 16:30 homework battles are hunger with a worksheet attached.

4. A defined end. A time or a task, agreed in advance. Open-ended homework expands to fill the evening and poisons the whole arrangement.

5. It runs even when there is nothing set. This is the component everyone drops and it is the one that makes it a routine. On empty nights: reading, or five minutes of retrieval on something from last week. Short. The point is the slot, not the work.

Try this

Write the routine as four lines on a card and stick it where the homework happens. Snack. Phone in the kitchen. Twenty-five minutes. Bag packed for tomorrow.

Sequencing the evening

For most families the order that works is:

Home → food → move about for a bit → homework → bag packed for tomorrow → free time.

Two details matter. Homework before free time, because reversing it means stopping something enjoyable, which is a fight every night. And bag packed immediately after, because the materials are already out and it takes ninety seconds.

Handing it over

The same four stages as everything else:

  1. Sit with them (5–7)
  2. Nearby, in the room, doing something else (8–10)
  3. Available in the house, come at the end (11–13)
  4. Absent (14+)

Move up when the current stage has been smooth for a month. Move down temporarily if things fall apart — going back a stage briefly is much better than the routine collapsing.

When it stops working

They stall for forty minutes before starting. The first step is too big. Break it down — the first action should take under five minutes.

It has become a nightly fight. Reduce it to something almost trivially small for a fortnight — ten minutes, no arguments — and rebuild. More on this.

Nothing ever seems to be set. Check with the school once, then run the slot regardless.

It has expanded to two hours. Cap it, tell the teacher, and have the child write the note.

What to try this week
  1. Pick the trigger and the place, and do not vary them.
  2. Feed them first, every time.
  3. Set an end point in advance and honour it.
  4. Run the slot on an empty night, with reading instead.

Common questions

Should homework happen straight after school or later?

It depends on the child, and it is worth testing rather than assuming. Some need forty minutes of decompression; others lose all momentum if they sit down. Try both for a week each and keep the one that produced fewer arguments.
