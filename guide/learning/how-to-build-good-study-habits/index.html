How to help children build good study habits

The two most popular study techniques are the two with the weakest evidence. That gap is worth closing early.

The Dayly Editors Published 18 August 2026 3 min read Ages 10–18
The short answer

The best-supported study techniques are retrieval practice and spaced practice; the most commonly used are rereading and highlighting, which have weak support. Build the habit of testing rather than reviewing, spread work over days, and establish a fixed time and place before there is anything at stake.

There is an unusually clear gap in this area between what works and what students do. In a review ranking common study techniques, practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility; rereading, highlighting and summarising were rated low (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

Rereading and highlighting are, of course, what almost everyone does — because they feel productive and are not unpleasant.

The two that work

Retrieval practice. Close the book and try to remember. Harder, feels worse, works better. How to do it at home.

Spacing. The same total time spread over days beats one long session. Three lots of twenty minutes on Monday, Wednesday and Friday beats an hour on Thursday.

Everything else is secondary to these two.

The habits worth building early

A fixed time and place. Established at eleven or twelve, before anything is at stake, so it is automatic by the time it matters.

Phone in another room. The largest single environmental factor for teenagers, and the one worth spending your authority on.

Starting badly on purpose. The hardest part of studying is beginning. A rule of “five minutes and then you can stop” gets past it, and they almost never stop.

Planning backwards from a deadline. The method.

Knowing what they do not know. The single most useful metacognitive skill, and retrieval practice teaches it as a side-effect: you find out what you cannot remember.

What to stop doing

Rereading notes. Feels like studying, produces familiarity rather than knowledge.

Highlighting. Almost no evidence of benefit, and it feels productive enough to displace something that would work.

Copying things out. Transcription is not learning.

All-nighters. Sleep consolidates memory. Trading it for revision hours is a bad exchange, and the evidence here is consistent.

Studying with friends, mostly. Occasionally excellent, usually social.

Try this

Show your teenager the ranked list of techniques from the research itself. Most find it genuinely interesting, and “the thing you are doing is the least effective one” lands much better as a finding than as a parental opinion.

Building it into a week

  • Same slot most days, even when short
  • Ten minutes of retrieval on something from last week, before starting anything new
  • One planning session a week — what is coming, when will it happen
  • A weekly look at what has not been understood, and a decision about who to ask

The honest limit

Study habits help most when a student wants to do well. If motivation is the actual problem — and at fifteen or sixteen it often is — technique will not fix it, and pushing harder tends to make it worse.

That is a different conversation: about what they want, what they are worried about, and whether anyone has asked them recently.

What to try this week
  1. Replace one rereading session with closed-book retrieval.
  2. Spread one topic across three short sessions instead of one long one.
  3. Move the phone to another room during study.
  4. Show them the research ranking, and let it make the argument.

Sources & further reading

  1. Dunlosky, J. et al. (2013). Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques
  2. The Learning Scientists — free evidence-based study materials for students and parents
