Retrieval practice: the study technique parents should know about

Rereading a page can feel reassuring. Then the book closes, and that is when you find out what they actually remember.

The Dayly Editors Published 19 March 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 7–18
The short answer

Retrieval practice means trying to recall something from memory rather than looking at it again. Close the book and ask a few questions, days apart rather than all at once, and give them time before you supply the answer. It feels harder than rereading, and that is the part that works.

Rereading a page can feel reassuring. The words look familiar, the notes look organised, and your child feels as though they are learning.

Then the book closes.

That is when you find out what they actually remember.

Retrieval practice is the simple act of trying to bring information back from memory rather than looking at it again. It is one of the better-supported study techniques we have, and it is surprisingly easy to use at home. Research comparing study methods has consistently found that practice testing and spreading study across time are much more useful for long-term learning than rereading or highlighting (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

The important part is that remembering should happen before looking at the answer.

Why remembering works better than rereading

Rereading feels easy because the information is right in front of you. That feeling of familiarity can be mistaken for knowing.

Retrieval is different. The book is closed, the answer is not there, and the child has to search their memory for it. It feels harder, and sometimes it reveals that they have forgotten much more than they thought.

That is not evidence that the technique is failing. It is the useful part.

Retrieval practice has particularly strong support for factual and conceptual learning. It is not a replacement for being taught something in the first place, and it is less straightforward for skills such as extended writing.

It does not need to look like a test

This is probably the most useful thing for parents to know.

You do not need to sit your child down with a revision paper every evening. A few minutes can be enough.

Close the book and ask, “What can you remember about the water cycle?”

Let them talk. Then open the book together and see what was missing.

You can do the same thing in the car, over breakfast or while walking home from school. Three questions about something they learned last week may be more useful than another twenty minutes of reading it again.

For younger children, it can be even simpler: “What do you remember about the story?” or “Can you tell me three things you learned today?”

The point is retrieval, not creating another piece of homework.

Give memory some time to forget

Spacing matters too.

Three short attempts spread across a week are generally more useful for long-term retention than putting the same amount of time into one long session.

That means Monday, Wednesday and Friday can be more effective than Thursday night.

It also explains why asking about something from last week can be surprisingly productive. The child may have to work to bring it back, which is exactly the point.

Don’t rush to give the answer

When a child cannot remember something, it is tempting to tell them immediately.

Give them a little time first.

If they are stuck, a hint can help them retrieve the answer themselves. “It starts with a…” or “What were the three stages?” is different from simply supplying the answer.

The struggle should be manageable, though. If the child genuinely does not know something, more struggling will not magically create the knowledge. Teach it, then try retrieving it again later.

Mix things up

Once a child is comfortable with retrieval, different topics can be mixed rather than studied in completely separate blocks.

It is harder. It can also feel less productive.

That is another recurring feature of good learning techniques: the methods that feel easiest are not necessarily the ones that produce the strongest learning.

For an older child, this might mean mixing questions from different parts of a subject. For a younger one, it could simply mean asking about yesterday’s maths, last week’s science and a book they read earlier in the month.

What this can look like in real life

A few simple formats cover most situations.

A brain dump is one of the easiest. Give the child a blank piece of paper and a few minutes to write everything they can remember about a topic, then let them check what they missed.

You can ask three questions in the car without any materials at all.

With flashcards, the important part is answering before turning the card over. Flipping immediately and thinking “yes, I knew that” is really just another form of rereading.

Older children can work through past questions with the book closed, which brings retrieval much closer to the conditions they will eventually face in an exam.

And explaining something aloud can be particularly useful. Asking a child to teach you what they know forces them to retrieve the information and organise it at the same time.

It should feel harder

One of the strangest things about retrieval practice is that a child can feel as though they are doing worse while actually learning more.

Performance during retrieval can look worse than performance during rereading, while later retention is better (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Researchers sometimes describe these as “desirable difficulties”: the difficulty is part of what makes the learning stick.

That is worth explaining to children, particularly teenagers. If they understand why the harder method is being used, they are more likely to trust it.

You do not need to turn every evening into a study experiment.

Try replacing one rereading session with a few closed-book questions. Ask about something from a few days ago rather than something they have just put down. Keep it short, and then move on.

The aim is not to make studying harder for its own sake.

It is to spend less time creating the feeling of knowing, and more time practising the thing that actually matters: remembering.

What to try this week
  1. Do one three-minute brain dump on something from last week.
  2. Replace one rereading session with closed-book questions.
  3. Space one topic across three short sessions rather than one long one.
  4. Explain to your child why the harder method is the better one.

Common questions

Does this work for younger children?

Yes, in a light form — "what do you remember about the story?" — from about six or seven. Keep it playful; it should feel like a game rather than a test, or it becomes another thing to dread.

Sources & further reading

  2. Roediger, H. & Karpicke, J. (2006). Test-Enhanced Learning. Psychological Science
