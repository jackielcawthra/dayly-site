Stop doing everything on holiday

Everyone else is enjoying themselves, and you are still keeping track of the key, the towels, the sunscreen and what time you need to leave.

The Dayly Editors Published 6 August 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 5 min read Ages 4–17
The short answer

Give each child something genuinely theirs — a bag, the water bottles, one restaurant choice, part of a day — and do not take it back because you are faster. Let small things go wrong. Change the question from “has everyone got everything?” to “have you got your things?” And do not turn the week into a project.

There is a particular kind of holiday exhaustion that has very little to do with the holiday itself.

You have arrived somewhere beautiful, everyone else is enjoying themselves, and yet you are still keeping track of the room key, the towels, the sunscreen, the bags, what time you need to leave, where everyone is and what everyone is doing next.

The work has not disappeared. It has simply moved somewhere warmer.

A family holiday is actually a good opportunity to change this. Children have more time, fewer school commitments and plenty of ordinary things they can take responsibility for. The aim is not to give them chores while everyone else relaxes. It is to stop treating the holiday as something that one adult has to organise for everyone else.

Give everyone something that is theirs

Children are more likely to take responsibility when the responsibility is real.

That might be their own bag, their swimming things, the family’s water bottles or making sure they have everything before leaving the beach. Older children can take responsibility for choosing a restaurant, checking a route or planning part of a day.

The important part is not to take it back simply because you can do it faster.

If a child is responsible for their own beach bag, they need to notice when something is missing. If they are responsible for carrying their own things, they need to remember where they put them.

The holiday gives them a safe place to practise.

Let children help decide what happens

Children do not have to plan the whole trip to become part of it.

Ask an older child to choose one restaurant. Let another decide which activity the family does one afternoon. Give a teenager responsibility for working out how to get somewhere and what it will cost.

They may make choices you would not have made.

That is not necessarily a problem.

Part of growing up is discovering that choices involve trade-offs. The restaurant might be further away than expected. The bus might take longer. The activity might not be as good as it sounded.

Children learn much more from making a real decision than from being asked what they would do in theory.

Let small things go wrong

Parents often prevent problems on holiday because there is a strong temptation to keep everything running smoothly.

But a forgotten hat, a missing water bottle or a towel left by the pool does not usually need an adult to solve it immediately.

Let the child look for the towel. Let them work out where they left the bottle. If they have forgotten something they genuinely need, help them think about what they can do.

You can be sympathetic without taking over.

These small moments are where responsibility becomes real. The child begins to connect what they do with what happens next.

Give children some money to manage

A holiday can also be a useful place to practise making choices with money.

A child can have a set amount for treats or souvenirs and decide how to use it. An older child might manage a larger amount across several days.

They may spend it too quickly.

That is not necessarily something to fix.

If the money is gone, the child has learned something about planning that would be difficult to teach through a lecture about saving.

The adult does not need to turn every mistake into a lesson. The experience usually does enough.

Make leaving places everyone’s responsibility

This is one of the smallest changes that can make a surprisingly large difference.

Before leaving the beach, restaurant, pool or hotel room, everyone checks their own things.

Younger children can check their bag and shoes. Older children can also check chargers, phones, sunglasses and anything else they brought with them.

The question changes from “Has everyone got everything?” to “Have you got your things?”

That sounds minor, but it moves the responsibility to the person who actually owns it.

Do not turn the holiday into a project

There is no need to create a holiday responsibility chart.

Sometimes you will carry things for your children. Sometimes you will choose where everyone eats, book the taxi and tell everyone where to be. If someone is tired, ill or overwhelmed, you help.

The point is not to make every part of a holiday educational.

It is to notice how much one adult normally carries without anyone else even seeing it, and to hand some of that over when there is a genuine opportunity.

That can make the holiday easier for children too. They know what belongs to them, they have some say in what happens and they are trusted with things that matter.

And perhaps most importantly, the holiday stops depending on one person remembering everything.

You are still the parent.

You just do not have to be the holiday’s entire operating system.

What to try this week
  1. Give each child the age band above and say it out loud before you travel.
  2. Hand one child the daily look-round.
  3. Give a fixed budget in cash at the start and do not top it up.
  4. Let one child plan one day, and go through with it.
