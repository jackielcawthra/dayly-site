Chores

Age-appropriate chores, from 3 to 17

Most chore charts tell you what a child can do at a given age. The more useful question is what they are learning to take responsibility for.

The Dayly Editors Published 29 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 7 min read Ages 3–17
The short answer

The progression matters more than the age: helping, then doing, then remembering, then noticing, then owning. A three-year-old takes part. A nine-year-old knows it is bin day without being told. A fifteen-year-old sees the work before anybody mentions it — and that last step is the one parents most often forget to hand over.

Most chore charts tell you what a child can do at a particular age. That is useful, but it misses the more important question: what are they learning to take responsibility for?

A three-year-old can put their toys away. A nine-year-old can make a simple meal. A fifteen-year-old can cook dinner for the family and manage a household job without being reminded. The exact age matters less than what happens between those points.

The goal is not to make children useful around the house. It is to gradually make yourself less necessary.

AgeThe stageWhat actually changes
3–5HelpingThey take part. Slowly, and badly, and that is the point.
6–8DoingSomething is genuinely theirs, not something you ask for.
9–11RememberingThey know it is bin day. You stop being the reminder.
12–14Running itA whole area, start to finish, including the bits nobody listed.
15–17NoticingThey see the work before anybody mentions it.

The progression is simple: helping, doing, remembering, noticing, owning. And it takes longer than most chore charts suggest.

3–5: participation, not perfection

At three, a child can carry things, sort things and put things away.

  • Toys into a named box
  • Plate to the counter
  • Shoes on the shelf, coat on a low hook
  • Helping lay the table
  • Feeding a pet
  • Carrying laundry to the machine

It will be slower with them than without them. That is not the arrangement failing. That is the arrangement.

At this age you are not really buying the labour. You are building the expectation that things belonging to the family are something you take part in looking after.

6–8: make something theirs

This is the age when children can begin to own small parts of their own lives.

  • Laying and clearing the table
  • Packing their own bag the night before
  • Clothes in the basket
  • Keeping their room reasonably tidy
  • Emptying part of the dishwasher
  • Taking out the recycling, watering plants
  • Making their own breakfast

More importantly, give them something that is genuinely theirs. Not something they do whenever you remember to ask — something they are responsible for.

A visible list helps here, because it begins to replace your voice. The aim is for the child to look at what needs doing rather than wait for you to tell them.

9–11: stop being the reminder

This is an important stage.

  • Sorting their own kit after training
  • Emptying the dishwasher
  • Changing their own bed
  • Keeping their room to an agreed standard
  • Taking responsibility for one area of the house
  • Cooking something simple with supervision
  • Putting the bins out on the right day

But there is a difference between doing the bins when a parent says it is Tuesday and knowing that Tuesday is bin day. That difference is the beginning of responsibility. The job is no longer just the physical thing; it is remembering that the thing exists.

This is also where much of the daily reminding can start to disappear — provided the responsibility has actually been handed over.

12–14: give them something to run

By this age, children can take considerably more responsibility for themselves and for the household.

  • Their washing to the machine
  • Resetting the kitchen after eating
  • Managing their own room
  • Cooking a meal for the family
  • A proper share of the cleaning
  • Shopping for food with a list

New at this stage: washing from beginning to end, cleaning a bathroom properly, looking after a younger sibling for a short period.

Expect competence and inconsistency in the same fortnight. That is normal. A teenager can be completely capable of something one day and somehow forget how it works the next. It does not necessarily mean the responsibility is too much. Sometimes it means they are fourteen.

15–17: teach them to notice

This is the part that matters most.

A teenager should gradually be able to manage the ordinary things that belong to them without supervision or reminders. They can run a household area to an agreed standard, plan and cook meals, and contribute to a busy week without waiting to be asked.

But the real step forward is something else: noticing what needs doing, and doing it.

That is very different from completing a list. A child who clears the kitchen because you told them to has learned to follow an instruction. A child who walks into the kitchen, sees that it needs resetting and does it without being asked has learned something much more valuable.

They have learned to see the work.

And that is usually the last part to arrive, because parents often hand over the job without ever handing over the noticing.

The test is not their age

Age gives you a useful starting point, but it should not decide what comes next. Look at the responsibility they already have:

  • Have you stopped reminding them about it?
  • Do they know what “done” looks like?
  • Have they found their own way of doing it?
  • Are they beginning to notice when it needs doing?

If the answer is yes, they may be ready for more.

The best sign is often surprisingly simple: you have stopped thinking about the job yourself. That is when it has really become theirs.

If you are starting late

Do not suddenly hand a fourteen-year-old a fourteen-year-old’s list.

If a child has never been expected to take responsibility at home, start with something they can succeed at. Give them one clear area, make the expectation consistent, and let it become ordinary before adding another. You may need to start below their age and move up quickly.

That is not treating a teenager like a small child. It is giving them the missing experience: what it feels like when something is genuinely yours to remember.

And when they forget, resist the temptation to take it straight back. If the swimming kit is missing, help them work out what happened. If they forgot something they needed, let the inconvenience teach them where the system failed. Responsibility includes recovering from mistakes.

Don’t turn family life into a transaction

There is a place for charts, points and rewards, particularly when you are trying to make a new routine enjoyable.

But children should also learn something more fundamental: this is what members of a family do. You do not need a reward every time you put your own things away. You do not need to be paid for every contribution. You notice what needs doing because you live here.

The aim is not to create children who are very good at completing chore lists. It is to raise people who can look around, work out what needs doing and take care of it.

The question worth asking

The useful question is not ”what chores should my child do at this age?”

It is: ”What could my child reasonably own that I am still carrying for them?”

Start there. Give them one thing. Let them learn it. Stop reminding them when they are ready. Then give them another.

Over time the list changes. But so does the person carrying it.

And that is really what you are teaching: not how to do more chores, but how to run more of your own life.

What to try this week
  1. Find the band your child is actually at, not the one their age suggests.
  2. Add exactly one job, owned and named.
  3. Check the previous job is genuinely automatic before adding anything.
  4. Let them choose from a shortlist rather than assigning.

Common questions

How long should chores take?

Ten to fifteen minutes a day for primary-age children, twenty to thirty for teenagers, plus something larger at the weekend. Beyond that you are into territory where school, sleep and being a child start to suffer.
