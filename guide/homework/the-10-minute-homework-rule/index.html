The 10-minute homework rule: where it came from and whether it holds

It is a rule of thumb that got promoted to a research finding somewhere along the way.

The Dayly Editors Published 2 March 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

The "10 minutes per year group" guideline is a professional convention endorsed by teaching bodies rather than a result derived from research. It is useful as a sanity check — if a task routinely takes twice as long, tell the school — but it is not a target and there is no evidence that hitting it produces gains.

You will have seen it: ten minutes per year group per night. Year 3, thirty minutes. Year 9, ninety.

It is worth knowing what it is and what it is not.

Where it came from

It is a professional guideline, associated with US teaching and parent bodies and popularised through Harris Cooper’s work on homework, which reported that the correlation between homework time and achievement is small at primary age and larger at secondary — and that at some point more time stops helping.

The ten-minute figure is a practical rule of thumb consistent with that pattern. It was not derived from an experiment showing that a Year 4 child who does forty minutes learns more than one who does twenty.

What the research says

What is supported: that homework’s association with attainment strengthens with age, and that very long homework loads show diminishing and eventually negative returns.

What is not supported: that any particular number of minutes is optimal for a particular child in a particular subject.

How to use it well

As a ceiling, not a target. If your Year 4 child is regularly doing an hour, something is wrong with the task, the child’s understanding, or the conditions — and the school should know.

As a conversation opener. “This is taking twice what I understand you intend — is that expected?” is a normal, useful thing to ask a teacher, and it is usually welcomed.

As permission to stop. Especially at primary. Working for the intended time and writing a note saying where they got to is legitimate, and better for everyone than two hours of misery.

What matters more than the minutes

Was it retrieval or copying? Ten minutes of trying to remember beats forty of transcription.

Was it spaced? Three short sessions beat one long one.

Did they do it themselves? Forty minutes of parent-assisted work tells the teacher nothing.

Did they sleep? For teenagers, the trade between an extra hour of revision and an hour of sleep generally goes to sleep.

A practical version

Set a timer for the intended time. When it goes, they stop. If the work is not finished, the child writes one line to the teacher: ”I worked for 30 minutes and got to question 6.”

This does three useful things: it protects the evening, it gives the teacher real information, and it teaches the child to communicate about their own work — which is worth more than the last four questions.

What to try this week
  1. Time one homework session honestly, without helping.
  2. If it is far over, tell the teacher this week.
  3. Use a timer and stop when it goes.
  4. Have your child write the note themselves.

Sources & further reading

  1. Cooper, H., Robinson, J. & Patall, E. (2006). Does Homework Improve Academic Achievement?
  2. Education Endowment Foundation — Homework
