How it works

Never be the only one who knows.

Right now, one person reads the letters, the newsletters, the emails and the messages, and works out what they mean for Tuesday. Everyone else finds out by asking them. This page explains how Dayly does that job instead — and what happens when it gets it wrong.

The three steps

1

Forward it

Send Dayly anything — an email, newsletter, PDF, photo or pasted text.

You can even set up a rule so school and club emails go straight to Dayly.

2

Dayly reads it

Dayly finds the dates, times, places, kit and deadlines, and works out who it belongs to.

You approve it before it goes into the calendar.

3

Everyone sees what’s theirs

One family calendar, with a colour for each person.

Everyone sees their own plans, responsibilities and what’s coming up — without having to ask you.

How it works day to day

Set it up your way.

Build your own list of jobs

Your words, your household. Write the jobs that actually exist here, group them how you think about them — the kitchen, the mornings, the pets — and set what each one is worth.

See who did what this week

Every job ticked off is logged against the person who did it, so you can see participation across the week without standing over anybody.

Earn points together

Points go to one household total, not to a scoreboard per child. You earn them by doing your own jobs and by thanking somebody else for theirs — recognising a sibling counts.

Work towards a reward

Set a target and what the household gets when it reaches it. Everyone arrives at the same time, so there is no winner and nobody is behind.

Give a room an owner

Somebody keeps the hallway, with an agreed standard and a record of when it was last reset. Rotate it termly, or never.

Let a child add to the list

The shopping list, the thing that ran out, the thing they need for Friday. Noticing stops being one person’s job.

Step one, in detail

What you can forward.

The test is simple: if a person could read it and work out what it means for your week, it is the right sort of thing.

School newsletters

The weekly PDF, the term letter, the head’s email. Usually six or seven dates buried in four hundred words.

Photographs of paper

The letter that came home in a bag. Take a picture and send it.

Club and team emails

Fixtures for a season, training times, a change of venue, subs due.

Messages and pasted text

“Swimming starts Thursday, bring a towel and 40 kr.” Paste it in.

Booking confirmations

Appointments, parents’ evenings, tickets, travel.

Anything with a date in it

If it is not for you, it is discarded and nothing is added.

Step two, in detail

A letter becomes the things it actually asks of you.

A single school newsletter usually contains a swimming start date, a kit requirement, a cost, a permission deadline and a non-uniform day — attached to two different year groups and written as continuous prose.

Dayly separates those into five things with dates, owners and, where it matters, an act-by date: the day you have to start, buy or sign in order to make the deadline. That second date is the one families miss.

Every item keeps a link back to what it came from, so you can always check it against the original.

The honest part

What happens when it gets it wrong.

It will, sometimes. Reading a badly written newsletter is hard for people too, and a system that claimed otherwise would be lying to you.

So it is built to fail visibly rather than quietly:

  • Nothing is written until you tick it. Not even the things it is certain about. A wrong entry in a child's diary is worse than a row waiting thirty seconds for a tap.
  • Every row shows the sentence it came from, so checking it is reading one line rather than opening the original.
  • Anything it was unsure of is separated out, left unticked, and says what was ambiguous — "it does not say which child".
  • A message it could not read at all still arrives, with its text, so you can paste it in by hand rather than wondering where it went.

The thing to compare it against is not perfection. It is the current arrangement, where one person reads everything on a phone in a queue and holds it in their head for three weeks.

And then

It is a full family organiser underneath.

Forwarding is the fastest way in, not the only thing it does. Once the week is in there, the rest of the household can be too.

Household jobs

Named owners, days with names, and a record of when each was last done.

Rooms and areas

One person resets each room, rotating on whatever schedule you choose.

Presence

Half-day accurate. Nothing falls due on a day its owner is elsewhere.

Routines

Morning, evening, homework, Sunday — as ordered lists a child can follow alone.

Meals and shopping

A week of meals, a list built from them, and a cook rota that works around training.

Trips

A packing list per person, documents, and a leave-by time worked back from departure.

Getting started

About an hour, and you don’t have to do it in one go.

The shortcut

Let the quiz do the thinking first.

The same intake we use to set a household up by hand — who is here when, what the rooms are called, who is in charge of what. Twenty minutes, and you arrive at the setup with the answers rather than the questions.

Start the setup quiz
01

Add everyone

Each person gets a name, a colour and — for children — a code that opens the app as them. A child sees their own things and cannot switch to a sibling.

02

Say who is here, when

If your children are not at home every day, say so. Nothing falls due on a day its owner is away, and the cooking rota skips whoever is out.

03

Forward the first thing

A newsletter, a fixture list, a letter. This is the point at which it starts being useful, and it happens in about a minute.

04

Give things owners

Every job and every area gets a name against it. That is the step that does the work, and the one worth taking time over.

Common questions

Do I have to forward things manually every time?

No. Most people start manually, then set one rule in their email so the two or three senders that matter — the school, the club — go through automatically and are never thought about again.

What happens to the emails I forward?

They are processed to extract what is relevant to your household, and the original is kept so you can check any item against its source. Nothing is used for advertising or profiling, and nothing is shared. Trust and safety.

Can it read a photograph of a letter?

Yes — that is one of the main ways school information travels. A clear photograph of the whole page works best.

How does it know which child something is for?

From the year group, class, club or name in the text, matched against the children in your household. When it cannot tell, it asks rather than guessing.

What if we never get emails about anything?

Then add things the normal way — it is a full family organiser underneath. Forwarding is the fastest route in, not the only one.

Do the children need their own phone?

No. It works on any device with a browser, including a shared tablet in the kitchen. Children open it with a code rather than an account.

Will it send us a lot of notifications?

Not by default. It is a place you look, not a thing that shouts, and there are no streaks to lose if you ignore it for a fortnight.

What if we stop paying?

The account goes read-only rather than being deleted, and you can still see and export everything.

Forward the next thing that arrives.

Thirty days free, no card. It is useful about a minute after you send the first email.
