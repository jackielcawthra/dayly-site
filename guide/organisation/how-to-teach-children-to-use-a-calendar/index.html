How to teach children to use a calendar

"Next Thursday" means nothing to a seven-year-old. A visible week does.

The Dayly Editors Published 17 June 2026 Updated 8 August 2026 3 min read Ages 5–16
The short answer

Start with a visible week rather than a month, put the child’s own events on it in their words, and let them add things themselves as soon as they can write. Move to a month view around nine or ten, and to planning backwards from a date around twelve.

Adults forget how abstract time is. “The week after next” is a genuinely difficult concept, and children asked to plan around it are being asked to manipulate something they cannot picture.

A calendar makes time into a shape. That is its whole function, and it is why the format matters more than the tool.

Start with a week, not a month

A month grid is too much for a young child: thirty boxes, most of them empty, and the important one is fourteen boxes away. A week — seven columns, today marked — is legible from about five.

What goes on it:

  • School and non-school days, clearly different
  • Their clubs, with their name on
  • Anything unusual: a trip, a dress-up day, a visitor
  • Whose day it is for the jobs they own

Their events, in their words. “Football” not “E. training 17:30 Ekhagen”.

Let them write on it

The moment a child can write, they should be adding things themselves. A calendar somebody else maintains is a notice board; a calendar you write on is a tool.

Small things count: a friend coming over, a birthday, the day the library book is due. The habit of putting something on the calendar when you hear about it is the actual skill, and it is worth more than accuracy.

Try this

Give your child a pen and the calendar every Sunday. Ask what they know about the coming week that is not on it yet. Younger children usually know at least one thing you did not.

By age

By age
5–7A visible week on the wall. Today marked. Pictures for events. An adult updates it; the child looks at it daily.
8–10The child adds their own events. Introduce the month view for things further out — holidays, birthdays, the school trip.
11–13Their own calendar, shared with the family. Deadlines go on it, not just events. They notice clashes.
14+A calendar on their phone that they actually maintain, including school deadlines, and they check it without being asked.

Teaching planning backwards

Around eleven or twelve, a calendar stops being a record and becomes a planning tool. The skill is working backwards from a date:

The project is due on the 28th. It has five steps. Today is the 14th. When does each step happen?

Do it with them the first three or four times, on paper, with the calendar in front of you. Then let them do it badly a few times, which is how it gets learned. See planning long-term school projects.

The family calendar problem

One calendar with five people on it becomes unreadable fast, and children stop looking. What helps:

  • A colour per person, consistently
  • The ability to see just your own things
  • Only the things that need to be known, rather than everything that is happening
  • One place, not a wall calendar plus an app plus a WhatsApp group

That last point is where most families come unstuck, and it is worth solving before adding anything new.

What to try this week
  1. Put a visible week where the family passes it — not in a study.
  2. Add your child’s own events in their words, with their colour.
  3. Give them the pen on Sunday and ask what is missing.
  4. For older children, plan one deadline backwards together.

Common questions

At what age can children manage their own calendar?

Most can maintain one from about eleven, with a weekly check with an adult, and run it independently from around fourteen. Before that, they can read and add to a family calendar — which is the more useful skill anyway.
