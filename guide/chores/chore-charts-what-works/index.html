Chore charts: what works and what doesn’t

Charts are excellent memory aids and mediocre motivators. Most families use them the wrong way round.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 March 2026 Updated 6 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

Chore charts work as a visibility tool — they answer "what am I supposed to do?" without an adult. They work poorly as a long-term motivator, because reward novelty fades. Keep the chart, drop the prizes, and make sure the child can update it themselves.

A chart does two different jobs. And families tend to buy it for the second one.

Job one is visibility. It tells a child what needs doing without a parent having to speak. That is genuinely valuable, and it is what makes charts worth having at all.

Job two is motivation. Stickers, points, a prize at the end.

That works for a few weeks. Then it stops, reliably, in most houses.

Why the motivation half fades

Two things happen.

The novelty of the reward wears off, which is ordinary and predictable.

And the reward quietly changes what the job is. It becomes something you do for a sticker rather than something you do because it is yours. So when the sticker stops being interesting, the reason goes with it.

The research on rewards and motivation is more nuanced than it is usually reported. The effect is strongest for things a child already found interesting, and much weaker for genuinely dull tasks (Deci et al., 1999).

Emptying a bin was never intrinsically interesting, so a small reward is unlikely to ruin anything.

It is also unlikely to still be working in March.

What makes a chart work

The child updates it. If a parent ticks the boxes, it is a monitoring tool. It is not theirs.

It is short. Five or six lines. A chart with twenty rows is wallpaper by week two.

It lives where the work happens. Kitchen jobs in the kitchen, not on a noticeboard in the hall.

It shows today, not the month. Most charts fail by displaying too much time at once.

It shows who, permanently. Names against jobs beat rotating columns.

It survives a bad week. Anything that resets on a missed day teaches a child that one miss ruins everything. That is a poor lesson and a reliable way to lose them.

What to do instead of prizes

The tick is doing more work than people assume. Finishing something visible is satisfying on its own.

Beyond that, three things.

Notice specifically. “You did the bins without being asked” is worth more than a sticker and costs nothing.

Let the effect be visible. “The kitchen looks like that because you did it.”

Save rewards for genuinely extra work. Occasionally, and unexpectedly, rather than as a standing contract. An unexpected reward does not carry the same cost.

Try this

Take the prizes off your existing chart for a month. Keep everything else exactly as it is.

Then see what changes.

In most households the answer is very little — which tells you the chart was doing the work all along.

Charts by age

Under seven: pictures, today only, and a physical tick.

Seven to eleven: a written week, names against jobs, and they do the ticking.

Twelve and up: a chart is usually the wrong shape. A shared list or an app they can carry works better, because their week no longer fits on a fridge.

What to try this week
  1. Cut your chart to six lines and today only.
  2. Move it to where the jobs happen.
  3. Let the children do the ticking, including badly.
  4. Take the prizes off for a month and watch what actually changes.

Sources & further reading

  1. Deci, E., Koestner, R. & Ryan, R. (1999). Extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin
