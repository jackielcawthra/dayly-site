Should children be paid for chores?

The research does not support the idea that paying children for work is either obviously good or obviously bad. That leaves room for common sense.

The Dayly Editors Published 25 March 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 5–17
The short answer

Do not pay for the ordinary business of being a member of the family. Do pay for work that is genuinely additional. And let pocket money exist on its own, as a child’s money to manage, unconnected to whether the dishwasher was emptied. Some work teaches membership; some work teaches money; children can learn both.

There are two ideas that tend to collide when parents talk about paying children for chores.

The first is that children should contribute because they are part of the family. You do not get paid for putting your own dishes away or picking your clothes up from the floor any more than you get paid for being considerate to the people you live with.

The second is that children should learn that work has value, and that money is one way of understanding that value.

Both make sense. The research is more interesting than the argument usually suggests, because it does not support the idea that paying children for work is either obviously good or obviously bad.

What the research actually tells us

Much of the research on this question is really research about rewards and motivation. A large meta-analysis found that expected, tangible rewards tied to completing a task can reduce people’s later interest in doing that task when the reward disappears. The effect is strongest when the task was something they already found interesting (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).

That distinction matters.

There is a difference between paying a child to do something they already enjoy and paying them to clean the car. Nobody needs to discover their “intrinsic motivation” for emptying the recycling.

The evidence also does not support the much larger claim that pocket money ruins children’s work ethic. Nor does it show that children who do unpaid chores necessarily grow into more responsible adults. Other researchers have argued the effect is smaller and more conditional than the headline suggests (Cameron & Pierce, 1994). The honest conclusion is narrower: making a reward a condition of doing something can change the way a child experiences that activity, particularly when they were already motivated to do it.

That leaves quite a lot of room for common sense.

The useful distinction is between contributing and earning

There is a simple way to think about it.

Some things belong to the ordinary business of being a member of a family. You look after your own belongings. You help clear up after dinner. You put your laundry where it belongs. You contribute to the home because you live there.

Those responsibilities do not need a price attached to them.

Then there is work that is genuinely additional. A child might wash the car, help clear out the garage, weed the garden or take on a job that would otherwise have fallen to someone else. Paying for that work can make perfect sense. It teaches something real about work, effort and money without turning everyday family life into a transaction.

There is also pocket money, which is a different thing again. Giving a child some money that is genuinely theirs to manage gives them an opportunity to make decisions, save, spend and occasionally make a bad decision. That is part of learning about money. It does not need to be connected to whether they emptied the dishwasher.

So you can have all three: contribution, paid work and money to manage. They simply do not have to be the same thing.

What happens when everything has a price?

One problem with paying for ordinary chores is that the question can slowly change.

Instead of “What needs doing?” it becomes “How much will I get?”

That can make every small contribution negotiable. It also sends a slightly strange message about family life: that you participate when the payment is worth it.

There is another problem at the other extreme. If teenagers are doing substantial work for the household and never have any opportunity to earn or manage money, they may miss an equally useful lesson. Family contribution and paid work do not have to be kept completely separate.

The distinction is what matters.

What about points and rewards?

Rewards can be useful when they recognise something genuinely extra, particularly when the reward is not the reason the child is expected to contribute in the first place.

The danger is making the ordinary business of family life into a points system. If every basic responsibility earns something, the child can start to experience the responsibility as a job rather than as something that belongs to them.

That is a fairly different lesson from the one most parents are trying to teach.

A child does not need to be paid to become responsible. They need opportunities to own things that matter, notice what needs doing and experience the consequences of following through or not following through.

Money can teach other things.

So, should you pay your children?

For everyday responsibilities, I would not.

Not because paying is morally wrong, and not because research proves that unpaid chores create better children. Simply because these are two different lessons.

Some work teaches membership. Some work teaches money.

Children can learn both.

The most useful arrangement is often the least complicated: ordinary responsibilities belong to everyone in the family; genuinely extra work can be paid; and pocket money can exist separately as a child’s own money to manage.

That leaves children with something more valuable than a price for every job. They learn that being part of a household means taking care of it — and that there is also a place in life where work, money and choice meet.

That distinction is worth keeping clear.

What to try this week
  1. Write your three tiers: membership, extra, allowance. Do it with the children in the room.
  2. Check nothing in the membership tier is currently paid — and move it if it is.
  3. Set one clear price for one genuinely extra job, and pay it the day it is done.

Common questions

How much pocket money is normal?

It varies enormously by country and household, and the amount matters far less than the arrangement: paid predictably, without conditions, and genuinely theirs to spend badly. A budget the child cannot mismanage teaches nothing.

Should I dock pocket money for undone jobs?

It is popular and it tends to convert the allowance into a wage, which brings back everything in the paid tier. If a job is not done, the consequence should attach to the job — it still needs doing, and it is still theirs — rather than to the money.

Sources & further reading

  1. Deci, E., Koestner, R. & Ryan, R. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin
  2. Cameron, J. & Pierce, W. (1994). Reinforcement, Reward, and Intrinsic Motivation: A Meta-Analysis. Review of Educational Research — the main counter-argument
  3. Money and Pensions Service — Children and young people’s financial capability — UK evidence on how money habits form in childhood
