Independence

The teen independence checklist

Work backwards from the day they leave. It is closer than it looks.

The Dayly Editors Published 29 July 2026 Updated 14 August 2026 3 min read Ages 13–18
The short answer

By eighteen, a young person should be able to cook several meals, run their own laundry, manage a budget, book and attend their own appointments, travel independently, handle their own admin and ask for help when they need it. Most of it is learned between thirteen and sixteen, not in the final year.

A useful exercise: work backwards from the day they move out. If that is at eighteen, and they are thirteen now, there are about sixty months — and a lot of them are taken up by exams.

Here is what to aim for, and roughly when to start.

Domestic

  • Cook five different meals from scratch, including one for the whole family (start at 12)
  • Do a food shop from a list, to a budget (13)
  • Run their own washing, start to finish, including putting it away (12)
  • Change their own bed (9)
  • Clean a bathroom and a kitchen properly (13)
  • Iron the things that need ironing (15)
  • Sew a button, unblock a sink, reset a trip switch, bleed a radiator (15)
  • Know what to do when something breaks (16)

Money

  • Manage a monthly budget with real trade-offs (14)
  • Use a bank account, understand a statement (13)
  • Know what things actually cost — rent, food, a phone, transport (15)
  • Understand interest, debt and how credit works (16)
  • Save for something specific over months (12)
  • Deal with money going wrong without hiding it (any age)

Admin and health

  • Book and attend their own appointments (15)
  • Register with a doctor, order a prescription (16)
  • Fill in a form, upload a document, sign up for something (15)
  • Know their own medical history and any allergies (14)
  • Handle basic illness — when to rest, when to ask, when it matters (15)
  • Keep their own documents somewhere they can find them (16)

Getting about

  • Travel across their own town independently (13)
  • Plan and take a longer journey, including changes (14)
  • Book their own travel (16)
  • Know what to do when a journey goes wrong (14)
  • Manage the airport, if you travel (16)

School and work

  • Manage their own deadlines across several subjects (14)
  • Email a teacher appropriately about their own work (14)
  • Prepare for an interview (16)
  • Hold a part-time job or volunteering commitment (16)
  • Ask for help before it is a crisis (all of it)

Social and personal

  • Sort out a disagreement without an adult (13)
  • Say no, and mean it (any age)
  • Look after a friend who is not alright (15)
  • Recognise when they are not alright, and tell someone (all of it)
  • Be a good guest and a decent host (14)
Try this

Print this and go through it with your fifteen- or sixteen-year-old. Most take it seriously — particularly if it is framed as a list of things they will need rather than a list of failures. Ask them to pick the three they most want to be able to do.

The important one

The last item in the social section is the one that matters most, and it is not a skill so much as a relationship. A young person who tells someone when things are going badly is protected in a way that no checklist provides.

That is built over years, mostly by how the small failures were received. A calm response to a forgotten kit at nine is part of the same project as an honest phone call at nineteen.

What to try this week
  1. Print the list and go through it with your teenager.
  2. Pick three things to work on this term, chosen by them.
  3. Hand over one piece of their own admin completely.
  4. Notice how you respond to the next small failure.
