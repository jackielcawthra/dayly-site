Family travel organisation: how to make the holiday belong to everyone

A family holiday can be something everyone looks forward to, and something one person has been organising for weeks.

The Dayly Editors Published 14 May 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 5 min read Ages all
The short answer

The tiring part of family travel is not the logistics but having to keep all of them in your head. Deal early with the things that cannot be fixed later, work backwards from the moment you have to arrive rather than from the departure time, and give every person their own list rather than one an adult holds and checks.

A family holiday can be something everyone looks forward to and something one person has been organising for weeks.

There are passports to check, bookings to find, medication to remember, clothes that no longer fit, equipment that needs replacing and a departure time that somehow becomes everyone’s problem only when it is already too late. Then there is the journey itself, the waiting, the unpacking and the small things nobody thought to bring.

None of this is particularly difficult. What makes it tiring is having to keep all of it in your head.

The useful question is not how to become better at holiday logistics. It is how to make the logistics belong to the family rather than to one person.

Start before the packing

Packing is usually where people begin, but it is rarely where the real problems are.

Passports, visas, bookings, medication and transfers need attention while there is still time to do something about them. So do the less obvious things: checking whether the children’s ski boots still fit, whether someone needs a prescription, whether the accommodation actually has the cot you assumed it did.

A few weeks before the trip, deal with anything that depends on another person or has a deadline. Then check documents, equipment and the things you will need to take. Leave the final few days for laundry and packing.

The purpose is not to think about the holiday constantly. It is to stop the things that need time from becoming last-minute problems.

Work backwards from the departure

The departure time on a ticket is not the time you need to leave the house.

Work backwards from when you need to arrive, allowing for parking, security, walking and getting everyone out of the house. Then leave some room for the thing that always happens: someone cannot find their shoes, needs the toilet or suddenly remembers something essential.

It is much easier when everyone knows the real leave-by time before the morning starts. There is no need to keep negotiating it while trying to get everyone out of the door.

Give everyone their own list

This is one of the simplest changes you can make.

Instead of one enormous packing list that an adult holds and checks, give each person their own. The shared list can contain passports, medication, travel documents, chargers and other things that belong to everyone. Each person can have responsibility for their own clothes, shoes, toiletries, entertainment and equipment.

Children will need different amounts of help at different ages. A younger child might work through the list with you. An older child can pack independently and eventually be trusted not to have their bag checked.

The important change is not that the child has packed a suitcase. It is that the suitcase has become their responsibility.

“Have you packed everything?” keeps the job with the parent. “Have you checked your list?” gives it back to the child.

Let the journey belong to them too

A three-hour flight, a long drive or an hour waiting at the airport is still part of the day. Children do not experience it as empty space between home and the holiday.

Give each child a small journey bag they are responsible for: a book, headphones, water, a snack and something to do. A change of clothes for younger children is worth keeping somewhere accessible rather than at the bottom of a suitcase.

It is also easier to decide things such as screen use before everyone is tired and bored than to negotiate them in the middle of a journey.

But not every minute needs filling. Some waiting is simply waiting. Children can learn to manage that too.

The first twenty minutes matter

Arriving somewhere new can create its own small chaos. Everyone is tired, bags are everywhere and suddenly someone needs a swimming costume.

Give things a home as soon as you arrive. Put the documents somewhere safe, decide where dirty clothes go and let everyone unpack their own things. Children who know where their things live are much more likely to find them again.

The same applies when you return. Empty the bags properly, get the washing started and put equipment back where it belongs. Make a note of anything that was missing, broken or outgrown while you still remember it.

It is not glamorous, but it means the next holiday starts from a known place rather than from a pile of half-unpacked bags.

The goal is not a perfect holiday

There will still be delayed flights, forgotten chargers and somebody who suddenly needs something that is sitting in the bag beside them.

The difference is whether those things are being managed by one person who has been thinking about them for three weeks, or by a family in which everyone gradually knows what belongs to them.

That is really what organisation is for. Not a perfect trip, and not a perfectly controlled family. Just a little less that one person has to remember, and a little more that everyone can carry for themselves.

100:100, rather than 50:50. The adults hold what genuinely belongs to everyone. Children gradually hold more of what belongs to them. Everyone contributes fully to the part that is theirs.

A family holiday should not require one person to take the whole family with them in their head.

What to try this week
  1. Check every passport expiry date today. It is the failure with the highest cost.
  2. Make a list per person rather than one master list.
  3. Give the children their lists a week before packing day.
  4. Work the leave time backwards and write it where everyone can see.

Common questions

How far in advance should we start?

Passports and anything that sells out: eight weeks. Everything else fits comfortably in two. The item that ruins holidays is almost always a passport or a visa requirement, and both need lead time you cannot buy back.

How do I stop packing everything for everyone?

Give each person a written list and a packing date, and do not check bags for anyone over about eleven. The first trip where someone has no socks is the last trip where they do not read the list.
