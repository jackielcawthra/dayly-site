Dayly vs a chore chart on the fridge

A chart on a wall has one enormous advantage over any app: it is always visible and needs no decision to open.

Written by the people who make Dayly. We have an obvious interest, so we have tried to say clearly where the alternative wins and who should not use ours.

What a paper chart does better

It is always in view. No unlocking, no login, no decision to open an app. For younger children this is decisive, and it is why we suggest printing routines for under-sevens even if you use Dayly for everything else.

It costs nothing and needs no device.

A child can draw it, which makes it theirs in a way a screen does not.

Everyone sees it, including visitors, grandparents and whoever is looking after the children on Thursday.

Where paper charts fail

They cannot handle presence. If your children are not at home every day, a fixed weekly grid is wrong most weeks — and correcting it by hand every week is a job somebody is now doing.

They do not keep a record. “When was the bathroom last done?” has no answer, which matters in the specific argument where two people remember it differently.

They do not handle anything beyond jobs. Kit days, deadlines, forms, appointments, packing.

They go stale. Almost every chart in almost every house is a few weeks out of date, and once it is wrong, people stop reading it.

They become wallpaper. A grid with twenty rows is not read after week two.

What we would actually suggest

For a family with young children only: use a paper chart, keep it to five or six lines, put it where the work happens and redraw it each term. It is genuinely the right tool.

For a family with school-age children, more than one child, or children who are not at home every day: the chart stops being able to represent reality, and that is the point at which something else earns its place.

And the combination works well: Dayly holding the system, with the morning routine printed and stuck at child height by the door.

The thing neither solves

A chart or an app makes work visible. Neither makes it happen. The four conditions that decide whether a child does a job — it is genuinely theirs, they can see when it is due, the consequence lands on them, and nobody rescues it — are the same either way. More on that.
