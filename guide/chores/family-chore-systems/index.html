Chores

Family chore systems that survive real life

A rota can look wonderfully fair. But somebody has to remember whose turn it is — and that somebody is usually still the adult.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 April 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 5 min read Ages 4–17
The short answer

A rota distributes jobs; ownership distributes responsibility, and only one of them stops the adult managing the system. Give younger children something concrete, let the area grow with them, keep rewards for genuinely extra work, and decide in advance what still happens in a bad week. Then leave it alone long enough to become ordinary.

A family chore system is easy to design when everyone is well, the week is quiet and nobody has forgotten that there is a system.

The harder question is what happens in November, when school is busy, somebody has a birthday, football has moved to a different evening and everyone is tired. That is when most systems reveal what they are actually made of.

The best system is not the one that looks fairest on paper. It is the one that still works when nobody has much energy left to think about it.

The system should remove work, not create more of it

A rota can look wonderfully fair. Everyone gets a turn at everything, the unpleasant jobs move around and nobody can complain that somebody else always gets the good ones.

But somebody has to remember whose turn it is.

Someone has to change the rota. Someone has to explain the changes. Someone has to notice when a child is away. And when a job moves from one person to another every week, nobody quite gets to the point where it becomes theirs.

That matters because the purpose of a family system is not simply to distribute jobs. It is to distribute responsibility.

If the adult is still managing the system, the adult is still carrying part of the work.

Ownership is usually simpler

There is something different about saying, “This is yours.”

A child who always empties the dishwasher does not need to discover what today’s job is. A child who looks after the hallway knows that the shoes, bags and things left there are part of what they are responsible for, even if nobody has written each one down.

That is where ownership becomes more useful than a long list of tasks. It asks the child to notice, not simply to obey an instruction.

It also means that things will not always be divided into identical pieces. One child may have more jobs because they are older. Another may have a responsibility that takes longer but happens less often. Fair does not have to mean identical.

The aim is not 50:50. It is 100:100: everyone genuinely responsible for their part.

Some jobs are too abstract at first

The idea of owning an area works particularly well as children get older, but “the kitchen is yours” is a very different instruction to a six-year-old than it is to a sixteen-year-old.

Younger children usually need something they can see and understand. Put your dishes away. Feed the dog. Put dirty clothes in the basket. Clear the table.

As children grow, the responsibility can grow with them. They can move from doing individual jobs to noticing what needs doing within an area.

That progression matters. The long-term goal is not a child who can follow a chore list perfectly. It is a person who can look around a shared home and recognise that something is their responsibility.

Be careful with rewards

Points and pocket money can make a new system exciting, and there is nothing inherently wrong with rewarding genuinely extra work.

The difficulty comes when every ordinary contribution becomes a transaction.

If putting your plate in the dishwasher earns something, the child can quite reasonably start wondering what they will get for doing the next thing. The job has stopped being part of living together and become a negotiation.

There is a useful distinction between contributing because you are part of the family and doing something extra because you have chosen to take on more.

The first does not need a sticker.

A system needs a bad-week version

This is probably the most important test.

What happens when the family has a week in which almost everything goes wrong?

If the answer is that the entire system needs to be reorganised, simplified, explained and relaunched, it was probably asking too much in the first place.

A better system has a minimum. There are a few things that still happen when everyone is exhausted, and other things can wait.

That might mean the kitchen gets reset, school things are ready for the morning and laundry gets where it needs to go. The rest can wait until there is more capacity.

The point is not to keep the house perfect. It is to keep the responsibility alive.

Do not redesign it every time it goes wrong

Families often respond to a failed system by creating a new one.

The chart changes. The jobs change. The children get a new list. There is a family meeting. Everyone is enthusiastic for several days, and then ordinary life returns.

A simple system that keeps going is usually more valuable than a perfect one that has to be restarted every few months.

Changes are useful when something genuinely needs changing: a child has grown, school has changed, a job no longer makes sense or the balance has become unreasonable. Otherwise, give the system enough time to become ordinary.

That is when it starts doing its real work.

What a good system should make possible

A good family chore system should eventually become almost boring.

Nobody needs to ask whose turn it is. Nobody needs to stand over the children. Nobody needs to keep the whole arrangement in their head.

The children know what belongs to them, the adults know what belongs to them, and everyone understands that the details can change without the underlying principle changing: we all live here, so we all take care of it.

The system is only the structure around that idea.

And when the structure is simple enough to survive a difficult week, responsibility has somewhere to grow.

What to try this week
  1. Write your system on one sheet of A4. If it does not fit, simplify it.
  2. Convert any weekly rota into named ownership.
  3. Name the minimum that happens even in a terrible week.
  4. Put the next review in the calendar at the start of next term.
