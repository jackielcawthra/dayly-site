How to stop reminding your child about everything

Your child may not know what they have to do today. But they know that you know. That is how the reminder loop gets established.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 8 min read Ages 5–17
The short answer

You cannot simply remind less — something has to replace the reminder. Put the information somewhere the child can reach without you, point at it rather than supplying the answer, and allow the small things to be forgotten. The shift to aim for is from “why didn’t you remind me?” to “where can I check?”

There is a particular kind of morning that many parents know well. Get dressed. Brush your teeth. Eat something. Where is your bag? Have you got your shoes? The reading folder. Your coat. We are leaving.

None of the reminders is unreasonable. The problem is what happens when there are twenty of them.

Your child gets through the morning, which makes the system look as though it is working. But the person doing the remembering, noticing and prompting has become part of the system too. The child does not have to know what needs to happen next because somebody else already does.

And once you become that person, the reminders are surprisingly difficult to stop.

The short version

If you want your child to become more independent, the answer is not simply to remind them less. Something has to replace the reminder.

  1. Notice how often you are prompting. Count the reminders on one ordinary morning.
  2. Put the information somewhere your child can see it. A routine, list, calendar or family system.
  3. Point to the system instead of supplying the answer. “What does your list say?” is different from “Have you packed your bag?”
  4. Let some things be forgotten. Children need to experience small, manageable consequences.
  5. Keep helping where it matters. Safety and serious consequences are different.
  6. Give responsibility, not just tasks. The aim is for the child to know what needs doing without waiting for someone to tell them.

Why reminders keep needing to happen

A reminder does two things. It gets the thing done, which is why you keep giving it, and it removes the need for your child to have noticed it in the first place.

There is nothing particularly wrong with their memory. There is simply a more reliable memory in the room, running for free. If you are always the person who knows that the library book is due tomorrow, that swimming is on Tuesday and that the school trip needs a packed lunch, your child has little reason to build a system for knowing those things themselves.

The tell is that your child may not know what they have to do today, but they know that you know.

That is how the reminder loop gets established. You notice. You remind. They do it. Tomorrow, you notice again.

Something has to take your place

You cannot simply remove the reminders and expect a child to become organised overnight. The information has to live somewhere they can reach without you.

That might be a list by the door, a routine in the bedroom, a calendar they can read, or a shared family system where each child has their own view of what is coming. The particular system matters less than the principle: when your child needs to know something, the first place they look should not be you.

At first, keep reminding as normal while the system becomes familiar. Then start changing the way you prompt.

Instead of:

“Have you got your reading folder?”

try:

“Have you checked what you need today?”

Instead of:

“It’s swimming today.”

try:

“What do you have after school?”

Instead of:

“Put your shoes on.”

try:

“What comes next?”

You are still helping, but you are no longer supplying the answer. You are teaching your child where the answer lives.

Then you have to let it work

This is usually the uncomfortable part.

At some point, something will be forgotten. The library book stays at home. The football shirt is still in the washing machine. The snack never made it into the bag.

It is tempting to step in because you could have prevented it with one sentence. Sometimes you should. Safety, medication and consequences that are wildly out of proportion to the mistake are not useful opportunities for learning.

But there are plenty of things that can simply be allowed to land.

“That’s annoying” is often enough.

A child who forgets their kit once and has to deal with the result has learned something that another reminder could never teach: they are responsible for remembering it.

The aim is not to let children fail. It is to stop preventing every small failure on their behalf.

The handover takes time

Children do not become independent because a parent announces that they are now independent. They become independent through a gradual transfer of responsibility.

At first, you may still remind them. Then you point them towards the system. Then you stop asking. Your child will forget things during this process, and some of those things will be inconvenient.

That does not necessarily mean the system is failing. It may mean the handover is actually happening.

The temptation is to take responsibility back the moment things become messy. If you do that every time, the child learns something else: that responsibility ultimately belongs to you, because you will eventually take over.

Give them room to become competent.

Teenagers need to know what you are doing

With older children, it helps to say this explicitly. If the reminders suddenly stop, they may experience it as a withdrawal of interest rather than a transfer of responsibility.

You can tell them what is changing:

“You are old enough to run more of your own week now. I am still here if you need help, but I am going to stop being the person who reminds you about everything.”

Then hold the boundary.

A teenager who has never had to remember an appointment, organise schoolwork or check what they need for tomorrow is unlikely to become completely self-managing simply because they turned seventeen. The work needs to start earlier, while the consequences are still small enough to learn from.

The part parents often miss

There is another reason reminders are difficult to give up: sometimes the reminder is not really for the child.

It is for you.

You remind them to pack the bag because then you know the bag is packed. You ask whether homework is done because you can stop thinking about the homework. You check the coat because you do not want to spend the morning wondering whether they remembered it.

The reminder relieves your anxiety.

That means stopping can feel uncomfortable even when you know it is the right thing to do. For a while, you may know that something has probably been forgotten and have to resist the urge to fix it.

That is part of the handover too. Your child is learning to carry more of the responsibility, while you are learning not to carry it for them.

Independence needs somewhere to live

The goal is not a child who never needs help or never forgets anything. Everyone forgets things.

The goal is for the answer to gradually move somewhere other than the parent’s head: a calendar they can check, a routine they understand, a place for their things, a list that belongs to them.

That is why a shared family system can be useful. It gives children somewhere to look instead of somewhere to ask. Each child can see what belongs to them, what is coming up and what needs doing, while the parent remains available without being the family’s reminder service.

The real shift is from ”Why didn’t you remind me?” to ”Where can I check?”

Once the answer is somewhere the child can reach for themselves, responsibility has somewhere to go.

What to try this week
  1. Count your prompts on one ordinary morning. Just count — do not change anything.
  2. Put everything you prompted about onto one visible list.
  3. Spend two weeks converting reminders into questions.
  4. Then go quiet, and decide in advance which forgotten things you will let land.

Common questions

Will my child just fail at everything for a while?

Some things, briefly. Most families see a rough fortnight followed by a noticeably better month, because the child starts building their own prompts — a bag by the door, a note, a check after dinner. If it is still chaotic after a month, the external system probably is not visible or specific enough.

What about children who genuinely struggle with memory?

They need more external structure, not more reminders from you — because a person-prompt cannot be checked at will and a list can. For children with ADHD in particular, visible, consistent, in-the-environment cues generally work better than verbal ones, and it is worth getting specific advice for your child rather than relying on general guidance.

Is not reminding them the same as not caring?

Say the opposite out loud, because it is not obvious from the outside. You are still available, still interested, still willing to help build a system. What you are withdrawing is the running commentary, not the attention.
