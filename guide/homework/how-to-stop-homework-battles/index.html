How to stop homework becoming a nightly battle

If every evening ends in a row, the homework is no longer the most important thing at stake.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 May 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

Reduce the conflict by shrinking the task, fixing the trigger, removing yourself from the content, and telling the school what is actually happening. At primary age in particular, the evidence does not justify paying for homework completion with the relationship.

First, the thing worth saying before any technique: a child’s relationship with you, and with school, is a bigger asset than any given piece of homework. If those are being spent nightly, the trade is a bad one — particularly at primary, where the average attainment effect is small.

That is not permission to give up. It is permission to stop pushing harder as the first move.

Find out what the fight is actually about

Five common ones, and they need different responses:

”I can’t do it.” Genuine difficulty, often hidden by anger. Test it by asking them to explain the first question rather than answer it. Response: stop, and tell the teacher.

”It’s boring and pointless.” Sometimes accurate. Response: acknowledge it honestly, keep it short, and do not defend a worksheet you also think is poor.

”I’m exhausted.” School is a long day and some children are genuinely spent by four. Response: move the slot later, or shorten it.

”You’re on top of me.” Response: leave the room. This one resolves fast.

”This is the one thing I can refuse.” A control issue rather than a homework issue. Response: offer real choices — when, where, which subject first — and stop making completion the battlefield.

Make it smaller

The most reliable de-escalation is to reduce the task until it is beneath argument. Ten minutes. One question. A timer.

Once ten minutes happens reliably for a fortnight, extend it. Rebuilding from something small and successful works; insisting on the full amount while it is failing does not.

Take yourself out of the content

Much of the heat comes from a parent being simultaneously the teacher, the enforcer and the person whose approval is on the line. Reduce your role to conditions only — time, place, food, availability — and the temperature drops within days.

Tell the school

Teachers generally do not know that their twenty-minute task takes an hour and a half in your kitchen, and most will adjust. A short, factual note works:

“Amelia worked for 30 minutes and completed questions 1–6. She found 7 onwards difficult and we stopped there.”

That is not a complaint or a failure. It is the information the school needs.

Worth knowing

If homework problems are severe, persistent and accompanied by real difficulty with reading, writing or attention, it is worth asking the school for a proper conversation. Persistent homework misery is sometimes the first visible sign of something that is easier to help with once it is identified.

For older students

The dynamic shifts at fourteen or fifteen: the work matters more, and your leverage is lower. What works better than pressure:

  • Ask about the subject, not the task
  • Help with planning, which they usually accept, rather than content, which they usually do not
  • Protect sleep rather than adding hours
  • Be explicit that you are on their side about the workload, even where you are not going to help
  • them avoid it
What to try this week
  1. Work out which of the five fights you are actually having.
  2. Cut the task to something too small to argue about, for a fortnight.
  3. Leave the room during homework.
  4. Send the teacher one factual note about what is really happening.
