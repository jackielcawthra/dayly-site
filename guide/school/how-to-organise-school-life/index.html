How to organise school life

The information arrives through ten channels and has to end up in one place. Usually the only thing capable of doing that conversion is a person.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 6 min read Ages 4–18
The short answer

School generates more family logistics than anything else, and none of it arrives in a usable form. What changes things is not capturing it faster but stopping one person having to convert it — forwarding what arrives rather than transcribing it, putting both adults on the school’s list, and giving each child their own school life to hold.

School is the largest single generator of family logistics, and almost none of it arrives in a usable form. It comes as a newsletter attachment, a photograph of a letter, a line in a class group, an app that emails you about a different app, and something a teacher mentioned at pick-up which nobody wrote down. The problem is rarely that a family is disorganised. It is that the information arrives through ten channels and has to end up in one place, and the only thing capable of doing that conversion is usually a person.

Which is why school is where most families first notice the mental load. Every one of those messages has to be read, understood, translated into a date or a kit day or a form, attached to the right child, and then remembered until it matters.

Stop transcribing. Start forwarding.

The traditional advice is to capture everything immediately: read the newsletter, find the dates, type them into a calendar, add the kit to a list, put the form somewhere sensible. It works, and it puts one person in the middle of every piece of school communication for eleven years.

The more useful change is to stop being the thing that converts information into a calendar. A newsletter forwarded to a shared family address can become the dates, the deadlines and the kit without anybody typing them out. A photograph of a letter that came home in a bag can do the same. The work that used to happen in somebody’s head — reading it, working out what it means for which child, remembering it — happens somewhere the whole family can see.

That does not eliminate the thirty seconds at the moment of arrival. It changes what the thirty seconds are for. Instead of extracting five things from a newsletter and distributing them, you forward it and glance at what comes back.

Some of it is still paper, and that is fine

A shared system does not solve everything school produces, and it is worth being clear about which parts it does not touch.

Forms that need signing are still forms that need signing. Money in an envelope is still money in an envelope. A costume for World Book Day still has to exist by Thursday. One tray by the door for things that physically have to go back is a good idea and always will be, because the constraint there is not information, it is an object.

What changes is that the tray no longer has to double as a memory. The deadline lives in the same place as everything else, so the form in the tray is not the only thing reminding anybody it exists.

The parts worth setting up once

Three things are worth doing deliberately at the start of a school year, because they prevent a whole category of problem rather than solving one instance of it.

Term dates, inset days and holidays, the moment they are published — usually before the previous year has ended. Fifteen minutes, and it is the difference between booking a holiday in an inset week and not.

The weekly pattern: which days need which kit, for each child. PE, swimming, library, music. Once that exists in a form everybody can see, it stops being something one parent holds.

And who receives what. This is the one families get wrong most often, and it is the origin of a great deal of resentment. Whichever adult is on the school system receives everything, and so becomes the person who knows. The fix is not to forward each message to the other parent, which keeps one person in the middle. It is either to put both adults on the school’s list, or to give the family a single address that both of them can see.

Give each child their own school life

The part most families skip is the one that pays off longest.

A child’s kit day, their reading, their homework deadlines and their club do not need to live in an adult’s head. What they need is somewhere the child can look, and an adult who has agreed to stop being the reminder.

By age
5–7They know which day is PE. An adult still holds everything else.
8–10Their bag, their kit, their reading, packed the night before against their own list.
11–13Their timetable, their kit, their homework deadlines. They tell you about parents’ evening.
14+Everything about their own school life, including the things that come up twice a term.

This takes a term or two, and it involves forgetting things. A child who has been reminded about PE every week for three years has had three years of practice at being reminded, which is a different skill from remembering.

Where it still goes wrong

Three failure points survive any system, and they are worth naming because they are predictable.

The first is the non-routine item: cake sales, dress-up days, trips, money in an envelope. There is no weekly habit to attach them to, which is exactly why they need to reach the shared system on the day they appear rather than the night before they are due.

The second is the gap between two adults. One parent hears something at pick-up and the other does not. Relaying it is not a solution — it makes one of them the channel. A shared place is.

The third is Sunday. Everything that was true on Friday has been forgotten by Sunday evening, and the week starts in deficit. A short look at the week ahead — the Sunday reset — is the cheapest intervention available to a family, whatever else they use.

What it looks like when it is working

You find out about the trip when the letter arrives rather than the night before. Both adults know about parents’ evening without either of them having told the other. Each child knows their own kit days. Term dates were in the calendar before anybody booked a holiday. And nobody is scrolling back through a group chat looking for a date that somebody mentioned in passing three weeks ago.

What to try this week
  1. Put next year’s term dates and inset days in the calendar today.
  2. Write the weekly kit pattern for each child, once, where they can see it.
  3. Set up one tray for school paper and empty it every evening.
  4. Split the organisations between adults — school to one, clubs to the other.

Common questions

We use the school’s app. Isn’t that enough?

School apps are good at delivering information and poor at turning it into action — they tell you about a trip but they do not put the money, the form and the packed lunch on the right days. Treat the app as an inbox and the family system as the place things actually live.

How do I stop missing things in the newsletter?

Read it once, on a fixed day, with the calendar open, and extract only what applies to your family. The routine that works.

Want to make this easier?

One place for everything school sends you.

Forward the newsletter and the letters. Term dates, kit days, deadlines and forms come back as a week, split so each child sees only their own.
