Independence

The teen independence checklist

At eighteen, a young person does not suddenly become independent. Most of it has to be learned earlier, not in the final few months.

The Dayly Editors Published 29 July 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 13–18
The short answer

Ask what you would want them able to handle without you, then look at what still belongs entirely to you and start moving it across. The lists cover the house, money, health and admin, travel, deadlines and personal life. Start with one thing, hand it over properly, and resist quietly taking it back.

At eighteen, a young person does not suddenly become independent.

Most of the things they will need to manage when they leave home have to be learned earlier: cooking, money, laundry, appointments, travel, deadlines, knowing when to ask for help. They are not things that can usefully be handed over in the final few months before a child leaves home.

A better approach is to work backwards.

If your teenager might leave home at eighteen, what would you want them to be able to handle without you? Then look at where they are now and decide what can gradually become theirs.

This is not a test, and there is no correct age for every skill. Some teenagers will be ready earlier, some later. The point is to notice what still belongs entirely to the parent and start moving some of it across.

Around the house

By the time they leave home, a young person should be able to:

  • Cook several simple meals from scratch
  • Cook a meal for other people
  • Plan a simple food shop and stay within a budget
  • Do their own washing from start to finish, including putting it away
  • Change their bed
  • Clean a bathroom and kitchen properly
  • Iron the things that need ironing
  • Sew on a button and deal with simple household problems
  • Work out what to do when something breaks

They do not need to be experts at DIY. They do need enough confidence to notice a problem, work out whether they can solve it and know where to find help if they cannot.

Money

Money becomes much easier to learn when it is real.

A teenager can gradually take responsibility for:

  • Using their own bank account
  • Understanding what is coming in and going out
  • Managing a monthly budget
  • Saving towards something over several months
  • Understanding the real cost of food, housing, transport and a phone
  • Understanding interest, borrowing and debt
  • Making trade-offs rather than simply asking for more money
  • Telling someone when money has gone wrong rather than hiding it

A small mistake with money while they are still at home is usually a much easier problem to deal with than the same mistake for the first time when they are living independently.

Health and administration

These are easy for parents to keep doing because they are often quicker.

At some point, though, the young person needs to know how to:

  • Book their own appointments
  • Attend an appointment without a parent
  • Know their own medical history and allergies
  • Know what medication they take and why
  • Order a prescription when appropriate
  • Fill in a form and upload a document
  • Keep important documents somewhere they can find them
  • Recognise when they need medical advice
  • Ask for help when something is wrong

Knowing when to ask for help is part of independence, not the opposite of it.

Getting around

Independence often grows through small journeys.

A teenager can gradually learn to:

  • Travel around their own town independently
  • Read a timetable and plan a route
  • Make a longer journey involving changes
  • Buy or book their own ticket
  • Work out what to do when a train or bus is cancelled
  • Navigate an airport when appropriate
  • Get home safely if the original plan changes

The first few times, an adult may travel with them or talk through the plan beforehand. Eventually, the child should be the one making the decisions.

School, work and deadlines

By the later teenage years, school administration should increasingly belong to the teenager.

They can learn to:

  • Keep track of deadlines across several subjects
  • Plan backwards from a deadline
  • Email a teacher appropriately about their own work
  • Prepare for an interview
  • Manage a part-time job or volunteering commitment
  • Turn up on time without a parent organising every step
  • Ask for help before a problem becomes a crisis

The important shift is from being reminded to knowing what needs to happen.

Social and personal life

Independence is not only practical.

A young person also needs to learn how to:

  • Sort out an ordinary disagreement without an adult taking over
  • Say no when they do not want to do something
  • Be a considerate guest and host
  • Look after a friend who is struggling
  • Recognise when they themselves are struggling
  • Tell someone when something is going badly
  • Know that asking for help is still an option

The last one may be the most important.

A teenager who can cook, budget and catch a train but feels they have to hide a serious problem from everyone is not fully prepared for adult life.

What matters more than the checklist

It is tempting to work through a list like this and start worrying about everything your teenager cannot yet do.

That misses the point.

The question is not whether a sixteen-year-old can already do everything on the list. It is whether responsibility is gradually moving in their direction.

Perhaps they can do their own laundry but still rely on you to book every appointment. Perhaps they manage money well but cannot yet cook. Perhaps they travel independently but still need reminding about deadlines.

Start with one thing.

Hand it over properly, give them enough support to learn it, and then resist quietly taking it back when they do it differently from you.

The goal is not a perfectly capable eighteen-year-old who never needs anyone.

It is a young person who knows how to look after themselves, how to deal with ordinary problems, how to make decisions, and perhaps most importantly, how to ask for help when they need it.

That is independence worth working towards.

What to try this week
  1. Print the list and go through it with your teenager.
  2. Pick three things to work on this term, chosen by them.
  3. Hand over one piece of their own admin completely.
  4. Notice how you respond to the next small failure.
