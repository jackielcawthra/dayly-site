Mental Load

The mental load checklist

Sixty lines. Go through it separately, then compare. The gaps are the conversation.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 April 2026 Updated 5 August 2026 4 min read
The short answer

Work through this list separately from your partner and put a name against every line — the person who notices it needs doing, not the person who does it. The lines only one of you wrote, and the lines nobody owns, are where the household is actually unbalanced.

Print it, or work through it on a phone.

One rule. Mark the person who notices that the thing needs doing — not the person who does it.

Those are very often different people. And that difference is the entire subject.

School and childcare

  • Term dates, inset days and holiday cover
  • The newsletter, and what was in it
  • Permission slips, forms and deadlines
  • Parents’ evenings and the booking system
  • Reading records and home learning
  • Which child needs which kit, on which day
  • World Book Day, own-clothes day, cake sales, dress-up days
  • School shoes, uniform sizes and what no longer fits
  • The class WhatsApp group and what was decided in it
  • Friendship problems, and which ones need an adult

Clubs, sport and activities

  • Sign-up windows and fees
  • Session times, venues and who is driving
  • Kit, boots, shin pads, water bottles, name labels
  • Matches, fixtures, tournaments, festivals
  • Telling the coach when they cannot come
  • Whether they still want to do it

Food

  • What everyone will actually eat
  • What is in the house and what runs out on Thursday
  • The list, the order, the delivery slot
  • Packed lunches and who has school dinners
  • Allergies, and what the friend coming to tea cannot eat
  • Something for the weekend when nobody has planned

Health

  • Dentist and optician recall dates
  • Vaccinations and the follow-up that was never booked
  • Repeat prescriptions and what is running low
  • Which child is coming down with something
  • Sun cream, insect bites, plasters, the thermometer
  • Growth: shoes, coats, the swimming costume that no longer fits

Home

  • The state of each room and when it was last properly done
  • Laundry: whose, when, and what is needed for tomorrow
  • Bedding and towels, and when they were last changed
  • Bins and recycling, and the collection day
  • Batteries, bulbs, loo roll, washing tablets
  • Repairs, services, the boiler, the leak nobody mentioned
  • Bills, renewals, subscriptions, insurance

Social and family

  • Birthdays: theirs, their friends’, family
  • Presents, cards, wrapping, and getting them there
  • Party invitations and replying to them
  • Play dates, sleepovers, and who is collecting
  • Grandparents: visits, calls, how everyone is doing
  • Christmas, Easter, and everything they involve

Travel

  • Dates that work with school, work and clubs
  • Booking, and the thing that gets booked too late
  • Passports and their expiry dates
  • Packing, per person
  • Cover, currency, adapters, parking, the airport run
  • Somebody to feed the cat

The children themselves

  • Whether each one is basically alright at the moment
  • Who needs more attention this week
  • Screen time and what they are watching
  • Homework, deadlines and how school is actually going
  • Friendships, fallings-out, who they sat with
  • What they are worried about but have not said
Worth knowing

The last section is the one people forget to count, and it is the heaviest. Emotional monitoring — holding a running assessment of how everyone is — does not appear on any rota and does not stop when you are at work.

What to do with the result

Compare lists. You are looking for three things:

  1. Lines only one of you wrote. Those are invisible to the other person. Not ignored — invisible.
  2. Lines with no name. Unowned areas are where things get dropped, and they generate most of
  3. the arguments.
  4. A whole section in one column. That is not a fair split, however even the totals look.

Then hand over whole areas rather than individual lines. The method is here, and the awkward part — letting the standard move — is step four.

What to try this week
  1. Both fill in the list separately. Twenty minutes, no discussion until you are done.
  2. Circle every line only one of you wrote.
  3. Give a name to every unowned line, or agree out loud that nobody is doing it.
  4. Move one whole section — not one line — to the other parent.
  5. Move one section, sized down, to a child.

Common questions

Should the children see this list?

The parts that concern them, yes — older children are frequently surprised by how much is being tracked on their behalf, and it is a better argument for taking on their own kit than any amount of nagging.
