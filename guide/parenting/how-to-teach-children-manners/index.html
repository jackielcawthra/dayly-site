How to teach children manners

Children learn manners the way they learn language: by hearing them constantly and using them badly for years.

The Dayly Editors Published 22 July 2026 Updated 14 August 2026 3 min read Ages 2–17
The short answer

Model the behaviour constantly, expect it consistently at home, prompt privately rather than correcting publicly, and set expectations that match the age. Manners are a long habit rather than a lesson, and public correction reliably backfires.

Manners are a set of habits about noticing other people. Like most habits, they are acquired by repetition in a setting where they are normal — not by being told off in a restaurant.

What actually teaches them

Modelling, relentlessly. Children copy what adults do far more than what adults ask for. Say please and thank you to your children, to each other, and to the person at the till. A household where adults are polite to each other produces polite children with very little instruction.

Expecting it at home. Manners practised only in public are performance. Please and thank you at your own table, doors held for family, greeting people who come in.

Prompting privately. “What do you say?” quietly beats a correction in front of the person. Public correction produces embarrassment, and embarrassment produces resistance rather than learning.

Explaining the point, once. Not repeatedly. “It tells someone you noticed them” is a reason a seven-year-old can hold. The reason matters more than the rule for anything to generalise.

Practising the awkward ones. Greeting an adult, saying thank you for a present they did not want, introducing themselves. Practise at home, playfully, before it is required.

By age

By age
2–4Please, thank you, hello, goodbye. Prompted every time, without frustration. That is the whole curriculum.
5–7Greeting people by name. Waiting to speak. Basic table manners. Thanking a host. Saying sorry and meaning something by it.
8–11Answering when spoken to properly. Phone and message manners. Writing a thank-you. Behaviour at other people’s houses. Noticing when someone needs help.
12–14Introducing themselves. Making conversation with an adult. Being a decent guest. Manners online, which are the ones that will matter most.
15+Hosting. Difficult conversations. Apologising properly. Recognising when the polite thing costs something and doing it anyway.

What does not work

Public shaming. It teaches that manners are about avoiding embarrassment, and it damages the relationship you need for the actual teaching.

Inconsistency. Insisted on when visitors come, ignored otherwise, and children learn that it is a performance for outsiders.

Adults with different rules. Children notice immediately when politeness is required of them and not of the adults, and it undermines the whole thing.

Forcing physical affection. Making a child hug or kiss someone they do not want to teaches something about bodily autonomy that is worth not teaching. “Would you like to say hello, wave, or shake hands?” gets the manners without the cost.

Try this

Notice how often you say please and thank you to your own children over one day. Most parents are surprised, in both directions, and it is the highest-leverage thing to change.

The thank-you note

Worth keeping, even in its modern forms. A message, a voice note, a drawn card — the format matters much less than the habit of acknowledging that somebody thought of you.

Make it small enough to be feasible: one sentence, sent the same week. A long letter that never gets written teaches nothing.

What to try this week
  1. Count how often you say please and thank you to your children.
  2. Prompt privately for a week rather than correcting publicly.
  3. Practise one awkward social situation at home, playfully.
  4. Set a one-sentence thank-you rule that is small enough to happen.
