How to teach children to think ahead

Most morning problems are not really morning problems. They became problems the evening before, when there was still time to do something about them.

The Dayly Editors Published 16 April 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 5–16
The short answer

Look at tomorrow together at the end of the day, and ask what your child needs rather than checking whether the bag is packed. Put the timetable and the calendar somewhere they can actually see. Let small forgettings happen. Then move the noticing across gradually, so the child is not organised but still dependent on you to say what needs organising.

Most morning problems are not really morning problems.

The missing water bottle, the football kit that is still wet, the form that needed signing, the shoes that cannot be found when everyone is already late — most of these things became problems the evening before. By the time morning arrives, there is very little time left to do anything about them.

For a young child, this is not particularly surprising. Children tend to live close to the present. Tomorrow is an idea, not yet a place they need to prepare for. The ability to look ahead, work out what will be needed and do something about it now develops gradually, and it is something parents can help them practise.

The useful shift is from preparing for a child to helping them learn how to prepare.

Tomorrow starts the night before

The easiest place to begin is at the end of the day, when there is still time to do something about what is coming.

Look at tomorrow together. What is happening? Is anything different from a normal day? What will your child need? Sometimes the answer will be obvious. Sometimes checking the school timetable will reveal that it is PE, or that there is a trip, or that something needs to be brought in.

At first, you may need to do much of the thinking. That is part of teaching.

But there is an important difference between asking, “Have you packed your bag?” and asking, “What do you need tomorrow?” The first checks whether a task has been completed. The second asks the child to look ahead and work it out.

Over time, the questions should become less necessary.

Children need access to the information

It is difficult to expect a child to prepare for something they cannot see coming.

If the timetable, calendar, club information and school messages all live in a parent’s phone, then the parent remains the person who knows what tomorrow contains. The child can only prepare when somebody tells them what to prepare for.

This is one reason visible family information matters. A calendar on the wall, a school timetable beside the child’s desk, a shared family system or a simple list can all serve the same purpose: they move some of the information out of the parent’s head and into the child’s world.

The system does not need to be sophisticated. It needs to be somewhere the child can actually look.

Let preparation have a purpose

Children learn this more easily when the consequence is real.

If there is swimming tomorrow, they can work out that they need their swimming things. If there is football, they can check their kit. If they have a presentation, they can think about what needs to be ready before they leave the house.

They will forget things sometimes.

That is not necessarily a failure of the system. Forgetting is part of learning to remember. When the consequence is small and safe, allowing a child to experience it can teach more than another reminder from a parent.

This does not mean standing back while a six-year-old misses an important appointment because they forgot their shoes. Parents still judge which consequences are appropriate. The point is simply that children need some space in which their own preparation, or lack of it, can matter.

The support should gradually move

There is a natural progression here, although it will look different for every child.

At first, you might notice what is coming and tell your child what they need. Then you might ask the questions and let them find the answers. Later, they can check the timetable themselves and make their own preparations.

The important thing is not how quickly this happens. It is whether the responsibility is gradually moving in the right direction.

Otherwise, it is easy to create a very organised child who is still completely dependent on a parent to tell them what needs organising.

That is why “Have you got everything?” can become such a familiar question. It sounds like independence, but the parent is still doing the noticing.

A list can help, but it is not the skill

For some children, a short checklist makes the transition much easier. It can hold the things that would otherwise be forgotten and reduce the amount they have to keep in their head.

But the list is a tool, not the goal.

A child who follows a list that someone else has written is practising a useful skill. A child who eventually realises that they need a list, decides what belongs on it and changes it when their circumstances change is doing something more sophisticated.

The same is true of calendars, reminders and family organising systems. Good systems do not replace thinking. They give thinking somewhere to live.

Some children need more help than others

Children vary enormously in how easily they anticipate what is coming. Some seem to live three days ahead. Others need much more help noticing what tomorrow will require.

That is not necessarily a character difference, and it is not useful to turn preparation into a test of whether a child is “responsible”.

The support can simply match the child.

A younger child may need someone beside them. An older child may need a visible routine or a reminder. Eventually, the aim is for the child to have enough of their own system that the adult no longer needs to hold the whole process.

That is what makes the difference between helping a child prepare and preparing everything for them.

The skill is bigger than tomorrow

Learning to think ahead is one of those ordinary childhood skills that turns out to matter everywhere.

It is noticing that the weather will be cold before leaving the house. Charging something before it runs out. Checking what is needed for tomorrow’s activity. Realising that homework will take longer than the time available after dinner. Seeing that something needs doing now because waiting will make it harder later.

None of these things is particularly remarkable on its own. Together, they are part of being able to manage a life.

The aim is not to make children perfectly organised, or to make every evening run efficiently. It is to give them enough information, structure and room to practise so that, gradually, tomorrow stops being something an adult has to prepare for them.

It becomes something they can see coming too.

What to try this week
  1. Pick the trigger — the specific thing that happens right before the check — and use it every night.
  2. Write the four questions on a card and keep it where the bag lives.
  3. Run it aloud for a week, then hand it over and stand nearby saying nothing.
  4. Do it on the easy nights too.
