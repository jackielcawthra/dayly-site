Why homework doesn’t work — and how it could

Somebody has been sitting at the kitchen table for forty minutes and has written two lines. The problem may not be homework. It may be the homework.

The Dayly Editors Published 15 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 8 min read Ages 5–18
The short answer

Homework does little for primary pupils, but the reason is probably what gets set rather than homework itself. The technique with the strongest evidence — trying to recall something without looking at it, a little while after learning it — is almost never what arrives in the bag. And removing homework may widen the gap it was meant to close.

Most families argue about homework at some point. It is usually a Tuesday, it is usually about something small, and it usually ends with everyone feeling worse. The reading record has not been filled in. The worksheet was in the bag all along. Somebody has been sitting at the kitchen table for forty minutes and has written two lines.

Schools have begun to notice. A second-grade teacher in Texas announced she would stop setting homework altogether, explaining that research had been unable to prove it improved performance. A Florida district serving 42,000 children removed homework for all primary pupils and replaced it with twenty minutes of reading. Many schools have quietly done something similar.

The research they are citing is real. But there is something odd about the conclusion, and it is worth sitting with before the next Tuesday evening.

If practice works, why doesn’t homework?

Nobody argues that a child learning the violin should stop practising between lessons. Nobody suggests that the hour in the lesson is enough. The idea that you get better at something by returning to it is not controversial anywhere else.

Psychologists have also identified a set of study techniques that reliably work, several of which look ideally suited to being done at home. So the finding that homework produces very little seems to sit awkwardly with almost everything else we know about how people learn.

The education writer Natalie Wexler has argued that the explanation is simpler and more uncomfortable than it first appears. The problem may not be homework. It may be the homework.

The kind that works is not the kind that gets set

The technique with the strongest evidence behind it is retrieval practice: trying to recall something without looking at it. Not re-reading the page, not highlighting it, not copying the definition out neatly — closing the book and attempting to remember.

The timing matters too, and it is counterintuitive. The best moment to practise recalling something is not immediately after learning it, while it is still fresh, but a little later, once you have begun to forget. Which is to say, after school.

A homework task built around this would be short and would look almost too easy. Answer these four questions about what we did in class today, without opening your book. Then, next week, four more about the same material.

That is not what usually arrives in the bag. What usually arrives is a worksheet to be completed, a page to be re-read, or a project that mainly measures how much time a parent has. A large review of learning techniques rated practice testing and spaced practice as having high utility across subjects and ages, and rated re-reading, highlighting and summarising as having low utility. Those three are what most homework consists of, and they feel productive, which is part of why they persist.

Teachers were not taught this either

It would be easy to read that as a complaint about schools. It is not, and Wexler is careful about it: most teachers have had very little training in how or why to set homework, because teacher-training programmes largely do not cover it.

So a teacher setting a worksheet at the end of a long day is not making a lazy choice. They are doing the thing the profession has always done, without having been given the research that would suggest anything else. The result is a national habit that almost nobody has deliberately chosen and that everybody, including the children, quietly resents.

There is also a measurement problem worth knowing about. Much of the research uses standardised reading tests, which are designed to assess general comprehension rather than what a child has learned in a particular class. A homework task that taught a child a great deal about Ancient Egypt will not show up on a test whose passages happen to be about the Arctic. Some effective homework may be invisible to the studies used to judge it.

Taking homework away helps the children who need it least

The most common objection to homework is that it is unfair. Better-off parents can help more, provide a quiet room, supply a laptop. Homework, the argument goes, widens the gap.

The uncomfortable possibility is that removing it widens the gap further.

Children from less educated families are the ones most likely to need what good homework provides, because they are less likely to be picking up academic knowledge and vocabulary at home in the ordinary course of things. And in practice, homework is not distributed the way the objection assumes. Pupils at lower-performing schools frequently get very little of it. One study found ninth-graders describing perhaps a worksheet or two, an occasional project and half an hour of reading — with few consequences for not doing it.

There is a striking example of what the alternative can do. A professor at the University of North Carolina, having noticed that Black and Hispanic students were failing her course in disproportionate numbers, changed the homework so that students had to quiz themselves without consulting their notes. Results improved for everyone. The gap between Black and white students halved, and the gaps for Hispanic students and first-generation students closed entirely.

That is a homework intervention. It happened at university, and there is no obvious reason the same principle could not start much earlier.

The deeper problem is what there is to remember

There is one more layer, and it is the one most likely to change how you read a school newsletter.

In many primary schools the curriculum has narrowed to reading and mathematics, with science and the humanities squeezed to the edges — particularly where test results are under pressure. A great deal of classroom time goes on practising comprehension skills in the abstract: making inferences, identifying the author’s purpose, comparing and contrasting.

But the single largest factor in whether a child understands a piece of writing is how much they already know about the subject of it. Comprehension is not a transferable technique that can be drilled in isolation. It rests on knowledge.

Which means that even a teacher who knows about retrieval practice runs into a limit. Asking a child to recall what they learned today about “making comparisons” achieves very little, because there is nothing underneath it to recall. Retrieval practice needs content, and the content is what has been removed.

What this means on a Tuesday evening

None of this makes the argument at your kitchen table any shorter, but it should change what it is about.

The version of homework worth defending is short, tied directly to what the class is actually studying, and built around remembering rather than copying. It returns to the same material after a gap. The ten-minute rule that homework researchers recommend — ten minutes in the first year of school, twenty in the second, and so on — leaves most of the evening alone, and even brief work of the right kind can matter.

If what arrives in the bag is not that, then the honest position is that the evidence for it is thin, and a conversation with the teacher about what a particular task is meant to achieve will do more than another forty minutes at the table.

What it is almost certainly not about is whether your child is trying hard enough.

What to try this week
  1. Pick a fixed time and place, and use it every school night, even when there is nothing set.
  2. Swap one rereading session for retrieval — book closed, tell me what you remember.
  3. On the next long project, write the steps the day it is set.
  4. Move from sitting with them to being available in the next room.
  5. If it is taking far longer than school intends, tell the teacher this week.

Common questions

How much homework should a child have?

The commonly quoted "10 minutes per year group" rule is a convention rather than a research finding — see where it came from. It is a reasonable sanity check, not a target.

Should I check my child’s homework?

Look at whether it is done rather than whether it is right, especially past age nine. A teacher needs to see the child's actual understanding, including the errors — corrected homework hides exactly the information the teacher needs.

My child says they have no homework, every night. What do I do?

Check with the school once so you know what is actually being set, then use the fixed slot regardless — reading, or reviewing something from the week. A slot that only exists when there is homework never becomes a routine.

Is it worth arguing about homework at primary age?

Rarely. The average effect on attainment at primary is small, and the cost of a nightly conflict to a child's attitude to school is real. Build the routine, keep it short, and tell the teacher when it is not working.
