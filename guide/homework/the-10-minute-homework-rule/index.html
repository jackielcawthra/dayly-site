The 10-minute homework rule: what it actually means

It sounds more scientific than it is. There is no magic number, and a child should not spend an entire evening proving that they have tried.

The Dayly Editors Published 2 March 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 4 min read Ages 5–18
The short answer

Ten minutes per school year is a practical convention, not a result from an experiment. Treat it as a ceiling: when homework regularly runs well over, something is wrong with the task or the conditions, and the teacher needs to know. Set the time, let the child work, and stop when it is up.

The “10-minute rule” sounds more scientific than it is.

You may have heard the guideline: ten minutes of homework per school year. Year 3 means 30 minutes. Year 6 means an hour. Year 9 means 90 minutes.

It is a useful rule of thumb. It is not a finding that tells us how long any particular child should spend doing homework.

That distinction matters, particularly when a child is regularly spending twice as long as the guideline suggests.

Where the rule came from

The idea is associated with US education organisations and was popularised through the work of researcher Harris Cooper. His research found that the relationship between homework and academic achievement becomes stronger as children get older, while very large amounts of homework eventually bring diminishing returns (Cooper, Robinson & Patall, 2006).

The ten-minute figure is a practical convention based on that broader evidence. It was not produced by an experiment showing that a child who does exactly 40 minutes of homework learns more than one who does 20.

There is no magic number.

Use it as a ceiling, not a target

This is probably the most useful way for parents to think about it.

If a Year 4 child is regularly spending an hour on homework that should take around 40 minutes, the answer is not necessarily to become better at homework.

Something may be wrong with the task, the child’s understanding, the instructions or the conditions in which they are working.

And sometimes the teacher simply needs to know.

A parent can say, quite reasonably, “This is regularly taking much longer than we expected. Is that what you intended?”

That gives the teacher information they cannot get from a completed worksheet.

The minutes are not the most important thing

Two children can spend the same 40 minutes on homework and get very different value from it.

Is the child actively remembering something, or copying information from a book?

Are they practising something they have already been taught, or spending most of the time trying to understand the question?

Are they doing the work themselves, or is a parent gradually taking over?

And what is the cost of those extra minutes?

For a teenager, an additional hour of revision that comes directly out of sleep may not be a particularly good trade. For a younger child, an evening spent struggling through unnecessary homework can take time away from reading, playing, moving and simply being with the family.

More homework is not automatically more learning.

When the timer goes, stop

For families caught in the nightly homework cycle, the simplest approach can be surprisingly effective.

Set a reasonable time based on the school’s expectations and the child’s age. Let the child work independently for that period. When the time is up, stop.

If the work is unfinished, the child can write a short note explaining how long they worked and where they got to.

That does something useful for everyone. The evening has an end, the teacher gets accurate information about how long the work actually takes, and the child learns to communicate about their own work rather than relying on a parent to negotiate on their behalf.

It also changes the question from “How do we get this finished?” to “What can realistically be done in the time available?”

Those are not the same question.

What if it really does take longer?

First, find out why.

If the child does not understand the material, more time may not solve the problem. If they are distracted, exhausted or hungry, another hour at the desk may not help either. If the assignment is unusually large, the school may simply need to know.

And if the child is spending far longer because they are working very slowly, that is useful information too. It may point to something that needs attention rather than something that can be solved by repeatedly telling them to work faster.

The point of the ten-minute rule is not to give parents permission to abandon homework.

It is to give families a sensible reference point.

A child should not have to spend an entire evening proving that they have tried.

Homework has a place in learning, but it also sits inside a life. Once the work has taken a reasonable amount of time, there is value in letting the evening be an evening.

What to try this week
  1. Time one homework session honestly, without helping.
  2. If it is far over, tell the teacher this week.
  3. Use a timer and stop when it goes.
  4. Have your child write the note themselves.

Sources & further reading

  1. Cooper, H., Robinson, J. & Patall, E. (2006). Does Homework Improve Academic Achievement?
  2. Education Endowment Foundation — Homework
