Retrieval practice, explained for parents

Rereading feels productive. Remembering feels hard. The hard one is the one that works.

The Dayly Editors Published 19 March 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read Ages 7–18
The short answer

Retrieval practice means trying to recall information from memory rather than reviewing it. It reliably outperforms rereading and highlighting, and it works best when spread out over days. Practically: close the book and ask questions, a few minutes at a time, several days apart.

If you take one thing from the learning-science literature into your kitchen, make it this.

The finding

Trying to retrieve something from memory strengthens the memory far more than reviewing it does. Testing is not just measurement; it is one of the most effective ways to learn.

In a large review ranking study techniques by evidence, practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice came out as the two with high utility. Rereading and highlighting — the two techniques students actually use most — came out low (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

What the research says

Why children avoid it: retrieval feels worse. Rereading is fluent and creates a sense of familiarity that is easily mistaken for knowing. Retrieval is effortful and surfaces what you do not know, which is unpleasant and precisely the point.

The catch: this is well supported for factual and conceptual material. It is less clear-cut for skills like extended writing, and it is not a substitute for being taught something in the first place.

How to do it at home

The basic move. Book closed. “Tell me everything you can remember about the water cycle.” Then open the book and see what was missed. Two or three minutes.

Spread it out. The same three minutes on Monday, Wednesday and Friday beats nine minutes on Thursday. Spacing is doing as much work here as the retrieval.

Ask, don’t tell. When they cannot remember, resist supplying it immediately. A few seconds of struggle is where the benefit is. Then give a hint rather than the answer.

Mix it up. Interleaving different topics within a session is harder and generally better than blocking one topic at a time — though it feels less productive, which is a recurring theme.

Concrete formats

  • Brain dump. Blank paper, three minutes, write everything you remember. Then check.
  • Three questions in the car. No materials needed and it fits in a school run.
  • Flashcards, used correctly. Say the answer out loud before turning it over. Turning it over
  • and nodding is rereading with extra steps.
  • Teach it to me. Explaining out loud is retrieval plus organisation. Younger siblings and pets
  • are acceptable audiences.
  • Past questions, closed book. For older students, the highest-value version.
Try this

Tonight: ask three questions about something they learned last week, not today. The gap is what makes it work, and last week is what they are about to forget.

What to expect

It will feel like they know less than you thought. That is the technique working — you are seeing the real state of the memory rather than the flattering version rereading produces.

Performance during retrieval practice is often worse than during rereading, while long-term retention is better. Researchers call these desirable difficulties, and they are hard to sell to a thirteen-year-old who wants to feel like they are getting somewhere.

Which is a reason to explain the mechanism rather than just imposing the method. Most teenagers find this genuinely interesting, and it is one of the few pieces of study advice that comes with a real evidence base attached.

What to try this week
  1. Do one three-minute brain dump on something from last week.
  2. Replace one rereading session with closed-book questions.
  3. Space one topic across three short sessions rather than one long one.
  4. Explain to your child why the harder method is the better one.

Common questions

Does this work for younger children?

Yes, in a light form — "what do you remember about the story?" — from about six or seven. Keep it playful; it should feel like a game rather than a test, or it becomes another thing to dread.

Sources & further reading

  1. Dunlosky, J. et al. (2013). Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques
  2. Roediger, H. & Karpicke, J. (2006). Test-Enhanced Learning. Psychological Science
