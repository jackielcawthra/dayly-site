How to make chores less of a battle

They may not understand what “tidy your room” means, or may have learned that nothing really happens until a parent asks for the third time.

The Dayly Editors Published 14 February 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 4–17
The short answer

Before changing the child, look at the job. Make it concrete enough to finish, stop being the cue that makes it happen, fit it to what the child can actually manage, agree what good enough looks like, and watch what your own escalation is teaching. Some resistance is normal and does not need to become a battle about character.

There is a moment that happens in many families.

You have asked your child to put their clothes away. You have asked again. You have asked what feels like ten times. Eventually you are standing in the doorway, looking at the clothes still on the floor, wondering why something so simple has become such a battle.

It is easy to conclude that the child is lazy, careless or simply refusing to cooperate.

Sometimes they are refusing. But quite often, something else is going on.

A child may not be avoiding the work itself. They may not understand what “tidy your room” means, may not know when they are supposed to do it, may not have a workable place to put things, or may have learned that nothing really happens until a parent asks for the third time.

Before changing the child, it is worth looking at the job.

A chore needs to be clear

“Clean the kitchen” is an obvious instruction to an adult who has spent years maintaining a home. It is not necessarily obvious to a child.

Does it mean put the dishes away? Wipe the table? Load the dishwasher? Sweep the floor? All of it?

The more vague the job, the easier it is for a child to stand there doing nothing or to do one small part and genuinely believe they have finished.

Children become more capable when the responsibility becomes more concrete. “Put your dishes in the dishwasher after dinner” is something they can understand and eventually remember. “Help more around the house” is not.

This is not about lowering expectations. It is about making the expectation possible to meet.

Repeated reminders change who owns the job

There is another problem that is easy to create without meaning to.

If a child only does the chore after being reminded, the reminder becomes part of the system. The child does not need to remember when to do it because the parent will eventually provide the cue.

The parent may not even notice this happening. It starts as a helpful reminder and becomes part of the routine.

Then the parent gets frustrated because the child “never remembers”, while the child has learned that remembering is not actually necessary.

If the aim is responsibility, the child eventually has to own the noticing as well as the doing.

That does not mean abandoning younger children and expecting them to manage everything themselves. It means gradually removing the part of the job that the adult has been carrying.

The job has to fit the child

A five-year-old and a fifteen-year-old should not have the same expectations.

But age is only part of it. Children differ in their ability to organise, remember sequences, estimate time and cope with several demands at once.

A child may be quite capable of putting away ten toys and completely overwhelmed by “sort your bedroom”.

Sometimes the answer is to make the job smaller. Sometimes it is to change when it happens. Sometimes it is to change the environment so that the child can actually succeed.

A laundry basket where clothes are taken off is more useful than a rule that clothes must never be left on the floor. Open storage is often easier for a young child than drawers that need sorting. A rubbish bin in the room removes one small barrier.

Good systems do some of the work.

The standard matters too

Children are not always refusing. Sometimes they have done the job and the adult does not consider it finished.

The bed is made, but not properly. The dishwasher is emptied, but the cupboards are not arranged the right way. The room is tidy, but there are still books on the desk.

If every attempt is followed by correction, children learn something unintended: there is no point taking responsibility because the adult will come back and do it again anyway.

This does not mean accepting everything. Some things genuinely matter. But a child needs to know what “good enough” looks like, and there needs to be some room for them to reach that standard differently from you.

Some resistance is normal

Children do not exist to keep the household running smoothly.

They have other things they would rather be doing. They get tired. They forget. They sometimes object simply because being told what to do is irritating.

That is not a parenting failure, and it does not need to become a major negotiation every time.

The useful question is whether the expectation is clear and reasonable, and whether the child is being given a genuine opportunity to take responsibility for it.

You can be firm about the expectation without turning every missed chore into a battle about character.

Look at what happens after the reminder

One of the most useful things a parent can do is watch the sequence.

You ask. The child says they will do it. You remind them. They still do not move. You ask again. Eventually you become angry, and the child does the job.

If that happens repeatedly, the family has developed a system in which escalation is what makes the chore happen.

Changing that system takes time. The child may initially resist because the old pattern was predictable. The adult may also find it uncomfortable to stop prompting.

That does not mean the new approach is failing.

The aim is to get to a point where the child knows what belongs to them, knows when it needs doing and has enough structure to manage it without an adult standing nearby.

Chores are not really about chores

The reason we ask children to contribute at home is not simply to get the dishwasher emptied or the rubbish taken out.

Those things need doing, but they are also ordinary opportunities to learn that living with other people means noticing what needs doing and taking care of your share of it.

That is why responsibility is different from obedience.

A child who waits to be told can complete a chore. A child who notices, remembers and follows through is learning to take care of something that belongs to them.

So when a child is not doing their chores, it is worth resisting the quickest explanation.

They may need firmer boundaries. They may need less help. They may need more help. They may need a clearer job, a better system or simply more time to grow into the responsibility.

The point is not to find the perfect way to make children do chores.

It is to build a household in which contributing is normal, responsibilities are clear, and children gradually become people who notice what needs doing without waiting for somebody else to notice it first.

What to try this week
  1. Run the six-question check on your worst-contested job.
  2. Fix one thing only, and give it a fortnight.
  3. Check nobody is quietly rescuing it — including you, sighing.
