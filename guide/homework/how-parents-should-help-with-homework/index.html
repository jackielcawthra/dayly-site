How parents should help with homework

The evidence points to a clear line: set the conditions, do not do the work.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 February 2026 Updated 12 August 2026 3 min read Ages 5–18
The short answer

Help with the conditions — time, place, food, availability, breaking big tasks into steps — and not with the content. Research consistently finds that parental involvement helps while parental control is associated with worse outcomes, particularly as children get older.

There is a distinction running through the research that most homework advice skips, and it is the useful one.

Involvement — interest, structure, availability, encouragement — is generally associated with better outcomes.

Control — sitting over it, correcting it, taking over when it is going badly — is generally associated with worse ones, and the gap widens as children get older (Patall et al., 2008).

The practical translation: own the conditions, not the content.

The conditions worth owning

  • A time. Same slot every school night, whether or not there is anything set.
  • A place. A table, adequate light, not a bedroom for younger children, phone elsewhere.
  • Food first. Almost every homework battle at 16:30 is a blood sugar problem.
  • Availability. In the next room, findable, not hovering.
  • Decomposition help. Big tasks broken into steps is a genuine parental contribution.
  • A finish. A time when it stops, especially for younger children.

The four questions

When they are stuck, questions transfer the skill; answers finish the worksheet.

  1. ”What is the question actually asking?” Half of all being-stuck is comprehension of the task.
  2. ”What do you already know that is like this?” Points at retrieval rather than at you.
  3. ”What would you do if I wasn’t here?” Startlingly effective. Most children have a plan.
  4. ”Where could you look?” Book, exercise book, an example from earlier in the week.

If they are still stuck after these, the right answer is often to stop and write a note to the teacher. Being stuck is information the school needs.

Try this

Try answering nothing for one week. Only ask the four questions. Notice how often they get there anyway — most parents are surprised, and children find it is a compliment.

What not to do

Do not correct it. The teacher needs to see what the child can do. A page of corrected work tells them nothing, and it actively removes the information they use to plan.

Do not do the presentation. The poster that is obviously an adult’s helps nobody, and children know exactly what it means about your confidence in them.

Do not teach a different method. Especially in maths. Your method may well be better; it is not the one being taught this term, and the collision costs more than it gains. Ask them to show you how they were shown.

Do not turn it into the evening’s main event. Homework that dominates the household gets its importance from the volume of attention rather than the content.

By age

By age
5–7Sit with them. It is short. Reading together is the important part.
8–10Nearby, not next to. Start, then leave. Come back at the end.
11–13Available in the house. Help with planning, not content. Check it happened, not whether it is right.
14+Theirs. Provide food, quiet, and no additional pressure. Ask about the subject rather than the task.

When you cannot help with the content

Very common past year seven, and it is not a problem. What a child needs from you is not subject expertise — it is someone who takes their work seriously, expects it to happen, and does not panic.

“I don’t know either — what does the book say?” is a completely legitimate contribution and models something useful.

What to try this week
  1. Answer nothing for a week. Ask the four questions instead.
  2. Move from sitting next to them to being in the next room.
  3. Feed them before homework, every time.
  4. Stop correcting. Let the teacher see the real work.

Common questions

What if my child cannot do it at all?

Stop, and write a note to the teacher saying how long they worked and where they got stuck. That is more useful to the school than a completed page, and it protects your evening.

Sources & further reading

  1. Patall, E., Cooper, H. & Robinson, J. (2008). Parent Involvement in Homework: A Research Synthesis
