Mental Load

What is the mental load?

The dentist texted three weeks ago. You saw the message, thought I must remember that, and somehow you still haven’t booked it.

The Dayly Editors Published 4 March 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 9 min read Ages all
The short answer

The mental load is not the washing or the cooking. It is everything around those jobs: noticing what needs doing, remembering it, thinking ahead, deciding, and checking that things happened. In many families it settles on the mother, and nobody ever decided it should.

The reading folder needs to go back on Wednesday. One child has outgrown their shoes. There is a birthday party on Saturday and nobody has bought a present. The dentist texted three weeks ago and the message is still somewhere in the back of your mind. None of these things is particularly difficult, but somebody has to notice them, remember them, work out what needs to happen next and, usually, make sure that it does.

In many families, that person is the mother. Not necessarily because anyone decided it should be that way, but because she answered the first school email, joined the class group, booked the dentist and happened to be the person who knew what was going on. Once one person knows more, it becomes easier to ask them. And once everyone is asking the same person, that person becomes the place where the answers live.

That is the mental load. It is the work that happens around the jobs themselves: anticipating what will be needed, remembering what has already happened, planning what comes next, making decisions and checking that things have actually been done. It is difficult to see because, when it works, there is nothing to see. The form is signed, the kit is in the bag, the birthday present is bought. The thing that made all of that happen disappears.

He sees it. So why is it still hers?

There is a familiar explanation for this imbalance: perhaps women simply notice more. Perhaps men genuinely do not see the mess, the empty fridge or the school email waiting to be dealt with. But research suggests that the difference is not quite as simple as that.

In one experiment, people were shown photographs of the same room and asked to judge how clean it was. The photograph did not change. What changed was the name attached to it. When people were told the room belonged to Jennifer, they judged it more harshly than when they were told it belonged to John. The mess was identical, but the judgement was not.

That matters because responsibility changes what we notice. If tomorrow’s swimming trip is yours to organise, you read the school email differently. You notice the time, check whether the kit still fits and remember that the goggles are probably still in the car. You are not simply doing the job; you have become responsible for knowing about the job.

That distinction helps explain why “he doesn’t notice” can be such an unsatisfying explanation. He may see the same things. He may simply not experience them as things that are his to hold in mind.

The work happens before the work

Sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed couples about the thinking involved in running a household and broke it into four parts: anticipating what will be needed, identifying the possible options, making the decision and checking afterwards that it happened. Mothers did more of all four.

The interesting part was not necessarily the decision itself. Couples often made important decisions together. The imbalance appeared around the decision: one person was more likely to have anticipated the problem in the first place and more likely to remember afterwards that something still needed doing.

This is why a conversation that looks completely equal from the outside can still rest on an unequal amount of work. Two people may sit down together and decide which school their child should attend, while one of them has spent the previous three weeks reading the information, remembering the deadline, asking other parents and thinking through the alternatives.

The visible decision is shared. The work that made the decision possible may not be.

50:50 isn’t the goal

A 2024 study of mothers of young children found that mothers reported doing 72.6% of the cognitive work in their households — the remembering, planning and organising — compared with 27.4% for their partners. The physical work was divided 63.6% to 36.4%.

The difference was important. Mothers who carried more of the cognitive work also reported more stress, depressive symptoms and burnout, as well as lower relationship satisfaction.

That suggests that the answer is not necessarily to divide every household task exactly in half. Family life rarely works that neatly anyway. One person may cook more while another does more of the driving; one may enjoy organising birthdays while the other is happier dealing with school administration.

What matters is that responsibility is actually shared.

The better model is 100:100. Each person is fully responsible for the things they have taken on. If food is yours, you notice when it is running out, think about the meals, make the list and order it. If sport is yours, the kit, the fees, the messages, the times and the lifts belong to you too.

That means accepting that the other person may do things differently. Otherwise the responsibility has not really moved; you have simply acquired a new way of supervising it.

Nobody chose this. It just happened.

Most families do not sit down and decide that one person will become responsible for remembering everything. Someone is home more when the children are small. Someone takes the first parental leave. Someone has the more flexible job. Someone joins the school group because somebody has to.

Then that person knows more. Because they know more, they are asked more. Because they are asked more, it becomes easier to keep asking them than to build the same knowledge somewhere else. What began as convenience gradually becomes a division of responsibility.

By then, one person knows about the dentist, the swimming kit, the birthday present, the school trip and the passport that expires in June. The other person may be perfectly willing to help, but the family has already developed a system in which one person is the memory.

No one had to choose it for it to become real.

How to stop being the answer

Changing that arrangement starts with moving responsibility, rather than simply moving tasks. “Can you take the children to football?” is still an errand handed out by the person who knows about football. “Football is yours” is different. It includes the messages, the kit, the fees, the transport and knowing what is coming up.

The same principle can apply to children. A child who is old enough to lose a water bottle is old enough to begin becoming responsible for one. At first they will need reminders. Eventually, the aim is that they remember. The forgotten bag or missing kit is not necessarily evidence that the system has failed; sometimes it is how responsibility is learned.

It also helps to make the information itself visible. A family can agree that school belongs to one person, sport to another and certain responsibilities to the children, but if all the information still arrives in one person’s head, the underlying problem remains. School sends an email, the football club changes Saturday’s time, a birthday invitation appears, a form needs signing. Someone still has to hold all of it.

A shared system can give that information somewhere else to live. Dayly is built around that simple idea: family information should be available to the people it concerns, rather than living by default in the head of the person who happens to have become the family’s memory.

The app cannot decide who should take responsibility. That is a conversation for the family. But once the information is visible to everyone, it becomes much easier for people to own their part of family life.

The goal was never two people doing exactly the same things.

It is two people who are both fully responsible for the life they share.

100:100.

What to try this week
  1. Write down every area — not task — that keeps a household running. Aim for twenty lines. Do it separately from your partner, then compare.
  2. Pick the one area you would most like to stop holding. Hand over the whole thing, including the noticing.
  3. Agree out loud that it will be done differently, and that you will not correct it for a month.
  4. Give each child one area that is genuinely theirs, with the consequence attached.
  5. Put the areas somewhere everyone can see them, so nobody has to be asked.

Common questions

Is the mental load the same as multitasking?

No. Multitasking is doing several things at once. The mental load is holding responsibility for things that are not happening yet — remembering that they exist, tracking when they will matter, and deciding what to do about them. You can be sitting still and carrying an enormous mental load.

Is this only about mothers?

The research is about mothers, and the article says so: in one 2024 study mothers reported 72.6% of the cognitive work. But the mechanism is expectation rather than biology. Same-gender couples divide this work far more evenly, which is the clearest evidence that it is not a fact about women — and it is why the pattern can move at all. Research does find it lands disproportionately on mothers in different-sex couples, but single parents carry all of it, and in plenty of households it is the father. The useful question is not who is naturally better at it — it is which structure put it all in one place.

How do I explain it to a partner who does not see the problem?

Lists work better than arguments. Write down the areas separately, then compare. Most of the disagreement disappears when both people can see that one column contains twenty items and the other contains six — and the conversation moves on to who takes what.

Can an app fix this?

Not on its own. It can give the information somewhere to live other than one person’s head, which is the part that makes everything else possible. Deciding who takes responsibility is a conversation for the family, and no app has anything useful to say about that.
