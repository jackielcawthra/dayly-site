What actually motivates children?

Three conditions, and none of them is a sticker.

The Dayly Editors Published 27 May 2026 Updated 14 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

The best-supported account of motivation identifies three needs: autonomy (a real choice), competence (it is achievable and progress is visible) and relatedness (it matters to people they care about). Practically: offer genuine choices, size tasks so success is likely, and connect the task to the household rather than to a prize.

The most useful framework here is self-determination theory, which has held up across several decades of research in education, sport and work (Ryan & Deci, 2000). It identifies three psychological needs that, when met, produce durable motivation — and when frustrated, produce exactly the resistance most family arguments are made of.

1. Autonomy — having a real say

Not doing whatever they like. Having genuine input into how things happen.

What it looks like:

  • Choosing which jobs to take from a list of five
  • Choosing when homework happens within an agreed window
  • Choosing the order of a routine they helped write
  • Being asked their opinion about how something in the house works, and it changing

What kills it: instructions with no room in them, systems imposed without consultation, “because I said so” as a standing answer.

The effect is disproportionate to the size of the choice. A child who picked the bins does the bins more reliably than one who was given the bins, and the work is identical.

2. Competence — being able to do it, and seeing that

Children persist at things they are getting better at and abandon things they cannot succeed at.

What it looks like:

  • Tasks sized so success is likely — start smaller than seems necessary
  • Visible progress: a list ticked, a skill that is obviously improving
  • Being taught properly rather than told repeatedly
  • Specific feedback: “you remembered the shin pads without being asked”

What kills it: vague standards, work redone by an adult, comparison to a sibling, tasks that are too big, and praise that is obviously inflated.

3. Relatedness — mattering to people who matter

The one most often left out, and probably the strongest in families.

What it looks like:

  • The job visibly helps someone: laying the table means everyone eats
  • Doing it together, at least sometimes
  • Being needed rather than being managed
  • Contribution named as contribution: “the kitchen looks like that because you did it”

What kills it: work that seems pointless, being the only one doing it, and being paid for things that were about belonging.

Try this

Take one job your child resists and check it against all three. Do they have any say in it? Are they confident they can do it well? Is it obvious that it matters to anyone? Most contested jobs fail at least two.

Where rewards fit

Narrowly. They can start a habit and they can acknowledge genuinely extra effort. They cannot manufacture any of the three needs, and offered routinely for things a child already valued, they can displace the original reason. The evidence.

Motivation at different ages

By age
3–7Relatedness dominates. Doing it with you, and being told it helped, is most of the motivation available.
8–12Competence becomes central. They want to be good at things, and they notice when they are not. Size tasks carefully.
13–17Autonomy dominates. A young person with little control over their day will resist whatever they can, and the resistance is about control rather than the task.

The uncomfortable implication

If a teenager is resisting everything, the most likely explanation is that they have very little genuine autonomy anywhere and are taking it where they can.

The counter-intuitive move — giving ground somewhere real — usually works better than tightening, and it is worth trying before concluding it is a discipline problem.

What to try this week
  1. Offer a genuine choice on one thing that is currently an instruction.
  2. Make one task smaller so that success is likely.
  3. Name one contribution as a contribution, out loud.
  4. For a resistant teenager, give ground somewhere real.

Sources & further reading

  1. Ryan, R. & Deci, E. (2000). Self-Determination Theory. American Psychologist
