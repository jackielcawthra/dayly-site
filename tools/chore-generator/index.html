Family Chore Generator

Name your rooms, say what needs doing in each, and put a person against them. Ownership rather than rotating turns — which is the version that survives past November.

Free. No signup. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

Step 1 — who lives here

Adults included. Everyone who could own something.

Step 2 — your rooms

Use your names for them, not estate-agent ones. If the living room is called the church, call it the church — the app works the same way.

Step 3 — the jobs that aren’t a room
Step 4 — how owners are decided

Why it asks for rooms rather than handing you a list

Because nobody can name your rooms for you and be right. A generic chore chart gives every household the same eight jobs, and every household then quietly ignores the four that do not apply and misses the three that do.

Rooms also change what a job means. “Tidy up” is a category, and categories do not get started. Wipe the surfaces in the kitchen is a task with an end, and a nine-year-old can tell when it is finished. That is the difference between a job that happens and a job that gets argued about.

Who should own the bathroom

Rooms that involve cleaning a loo, a kitchen or a machine go to people aged eleven and over in this generator. That is a default, not a rule — plenty of nine-year-olds manage a downstairs toilet perfectly well. Change it to suit your house.

Common questions

Should rooms rotate?

If they do, rotate at a term boundary or every couple of months rather than weekly. Weekly rotation means nobody builds a habit and everybody has to check whose turn it is — which is a job in itself, held by a parent.

What if a child shares a room?

Give the room one owner and rotate it between the two who sleep in it, rather than splitting the jobs. Split jobs in a shared room reliably become nobody’s.
