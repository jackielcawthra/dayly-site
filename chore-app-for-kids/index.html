Why chore apps get abandoned

The novelty was the motivation. Points and prizes engage quickly and fade predictably. The evidence on rewards.

An adult had to run it. If a parent assigns jobs each week, the app has digitised the mental load.

Children could not use it alone. An app a seven-year-old cannot navigate is an app a parent operates on their behalf.

A missed day broke something. Streaks and resets punish exactly the weeks a family most needs a system.

How Dayly is built instead

Named ownership. “The dishwasher is Noah’s” produces a habit. “We take turns” produces a negotiation every day.

Days with names. Every recurring job says “every Monday” or “every other Saturday”. A child cannot act on “every 7 days” because they have no way of knowing when that is.

Triggers, not times. After dinner. When you come down. Before screens.

A child’s own screen. What is permanently theirs, what is due today, what is waiting on them.

Nothing punishing. No streaks, no resets, no sibling leaderboard. Nobody is losing every day.

Points, narrowly. They exist — for taking on someone else’s job, or doing something nobody asked for. Not for being a member of your own household, which is a different thing and should stay one.

Presence. Nothing falls due on a day its owner is elsewhere.

The system matters more than the app

A family that has not agreed who owns what, what “done” means, and what happens when it does not happen will have the same problem with better graphics. Do the system first, on paper — then pick something to hold it.

Common questions

Is there a reward system?

There are points, deliberately scoped to genuinely extra effort rather than to the basics. You can ignore them entirely and most households do.

What age can children use it?

From about five with an adult nearby, and independently from about seven. Younger children do better with a picture routine, which Dayly can print.
