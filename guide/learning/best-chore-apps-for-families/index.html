The best chore apps for families

Most chore apps are abandoned within two months. The reasons are predictable and worth knowing before you subscribe.

The Dayly Editors Published 16 August 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

Chore apps last when children can see their own jobs without a parent, when jobs have owners rather than rotating, when nothing punishes a missed day, and when they do not require an adult to assign work each week. Reward-heavy apps engage quickly and fade fastest.

Where we stand

We make Dayly, which includes household jobs among other things. That is a conflict of interest, so this article is about what to look for rather than a ranking with ours at the top. Some links may be affiliate links — see our affiliate policy.

Why most get abandoned

Six weeks is the common lifespan, and the causes repeat:

The novelty was the motivation. Points and prizes engage quickly and fade predictably. Why.

It required an adult to run it. If a parent has to assign jobs each week, the app has digitised the mental load rather than reduced it.

Children could not use it alone. An app a seven-year-old cannot navigate is an app a parent operates on their behalf.

A missed day broke something. Streaks and resets punish exactly the weeks when a family most needs the system.

It was another app. Jobs in one place, calendar in another, school in a third. Consolidation is usually more valuable than features.

What to look for

A genuine child view. The test: can your seven-year-old open it and find out what they are meant to do, without asking? This is the single most predictive feature.

Ownership rather than rotation. Named jobs build habits; rotas generate admin.

Recurrence that names a day. “Every Monday” is usable by a child. “Every 7 days” is not — nobody can work out when that is.

Nothing that punishes a gap. No streaks to lose, no resets, no leaderboard between siblings.

A record of what was done and when. Useful for the honest disagreement about whether the bathroom was cleaned this month.

Optional rewards, narrowly scoped. If points exist, they should be for extra effort rather than for the basics of being a household member.

What to avoid

  • Sibling leaderboards. Somebody is losing every day, and it is usually the same child.
  • Advertising in a child’s view. Non-negotiable.
  • Per-child pricing. It penalises exactly the families with the most to organise.
  • Anything requiring every family member to have a smartphone, if your seven-year-old does not.
  • No export. If you cannot get your setup out, you cannot leave.

The uncomfortable truth

The app is not the intervention. A family that has not agreed who owns what, what “done” means, and what happens when it does not happen, will have the same problem with better graphics.

Do the system first, on paper. Then pick a tool to hold it — and by that point you will know exactly what you need from one.

What to try this week
  1. Write the system on paper before you choose any app.
  2. Test any candidate by having a child use it unaided.
  3. Reject anything with sibling leaderboards or streak punishments.
  4. Check what it costs per family and what happens to your data if you leave.
