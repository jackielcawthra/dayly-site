The failure mode of most family apps

They are built around a parent entering tasks and children receiving them. That is a rota with notifications, and a rota needs a manager for ever.

Six weeks in, the pattern is familiar: the parent is still the only one entering anything, the children have stopped opening it, and the app has become one more thing to keep up to date.

What we did differently

Ownership rather than assignment. Jobs and areas belong to people permanently. Nobody is allocating work each morning.

A genuine child view. Not a read-only list of what a parent typed — their own screen, in their own words: what is permanently theirs, what is due tonight, what tomorrow needs.

Days with names. “Every other Saturday” rather than “every 14 days”, because an interval is not something a child can act on.

Nothing that punishes a gap. No streaks, no resets, no leaderboard between siblings. A missed fortnight costs nothing, which matters because the weeks you most need a system are the weeks you cannot maintain one.

Presence built in. If your children are not at home every day, nothing falls due on a day its owner is away.

The one-question test

Before choosing any family app — ours included — ask: can a seven-year-old open it and find out what they are supposed to be doing, without asking an adult?

Most fail that, and it is the question that decides whether an app reduces the mental load or just relocates it.

Common questions

How is this different from a shared to-do app?

To-do apps assume a user who voluntarily opens an app and manages their own list. That describes some adults and very few children. Dayly is built around recurring ownership, presence and a child-appropriate view.

What if we stop using it for a while?

Nothing happens. There is nothing to lose and nothing resets. You open it again and it is where you left it.
