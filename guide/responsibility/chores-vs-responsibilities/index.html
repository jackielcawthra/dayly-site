Chores are not the same as responsibility

A child can empty the dishwasher every day and still not be responsible for the dishwasher. The difference is not the work. It is the noticing.

The Dayly Editors Published 1 March 2026 Updated 22 July 2026 6 min read
The short answer

A chore is something a child is told to do. A responsibility is something they know is theirs. The physical work can be identical; what differs is who notices it needs doing. The test: what would stop happening if you said nothing for two weeks?

A child can empty the dishwasher every day and still not be responsible for the dishwasher.

That sounds like a distinction without much difference. The dishwasher gets emptied either way. But there is a difference, and it becomes more important as children get older.

A chore is something a child is told to do. A responsibility is something they know is theirs.

The difference is not the work. It is the noticing.

If you have to remember that the dishwasher needs emptying, remember that it is Noah’s job, find Noah, ask Noah to do it and then notice later whether it actually happened, the responsibility is still partly yours. Noah is doing the task. You are carrying the system.

That may work perfectly well when he is four. It becomes less useful when he is fourteen.

Chores are a good place to start

There is nothing wrong with chores.

Young children need adults to tell them what to do. They are still learning how the household works, what things need doing and how to do them. A four-year-old is not going to notice that the dishwasher needs emptying because nobody has mentioned it for two days.

So you might say, “Can you put your plate in the dishwasher?” Then you say it again tomorrow. That is a chore.

Over time, though, something can change. The child learns that plates go in the dishwasher, that the dishwasher gets emptied when it is clean, and that this is something they can take care of.

The instruction can disappear.

That is the point at which a chore starts becoming a responsibility.

Responsibility includes the noticing

This is the part that is easy to miss.

If you tell a child to take the rubbish out every Tuesday, they are completing a task. If they know that rubbish is their responsibility, they have to notice when it needs doing, remember when collection is, work out what needs to happen and do it without waiting for somebody to tell them.

The physical work may be exactly the same.

What the child is practising is completely different.

A choreA responsibility
Someone tells youYou notice
You complete the taskYou manage the whole thing
You wait for the reminderYou know what needs to happen
The adult keeps trackYou keep track
You practise following instructionsYou practise judgement and follow-through

This is why a household can have children who “do loads” and still have one adult who feels responsible for everything.

The children are helping. The adult is still holding the plan.

The question that tells you which one it is

There is a simple test.

What would happen if you said nothing for two weeks?

If the dishwasher stopped being emptied, the football kit never made it into the wash and the rubbish stayed by the door, those things may be chores rather than responsibilities.

That is not a criticism. It simply tells you where the responsibility still sits.

The aim is not to turn every household job into a responsibility immediately. Some things genuinely need prompting, particularly with younger children. The useful question is whether the list of things that collapse without you gets smaller as the child gets older.

How to hand something over

The easiest way to turn a chore into a responsibility is not to give a better instruction. It is to hand over the whole area.

Start with something small enough for the child to manage.

Make the trigger clear. “Empty the dishwasher when you come down in the morning” gives them something in the world to respond to. “Empty the dishwasher when I tell you” makes your voice the trigger.

Make the information visible if they need it. A list, a routine or a calendar can do something important here: it means the child does not have to ask you what happens next.

Then agree what “done” means. If the dishwasher is their responsibility, does that mean emptying it, putting everything away and shutting the cupboards? Decide once.

And then stop reminding.

That last part is what makes the handover real. If you continue to prompt, the child has no reason to start noticing for themselves.

There will be a period when it looks worse

This is where many parents take the responsibility back.

The child forgets. The job is done later than you would have done it. They miss something you would have noticed immediately.

It is tempting to conclude that they are not ready.

Sometimes they are not. But sometimes they are simply learning.

A responsibility cannot be learned without having something to be responsible for. That means allowing children to experience manageable consequences rather than quietly preventing every mistake.

The standard may also change.

A child doing something themselves will often do it differently from you. The bed may not look the way you make it. The dishwasher may be loaded in a completely different order. Their school bag may be less organised than yours would be.

If the outcome is good enough, let it be theirs.

Responsibility grows when the child grows

What a child can own changes over time.

A young child might be responsible for putting their shoes in the same place every day. Later, they can manage their school bag, remember their swimming things or put their own laundry away. Eventually, responsibility can extend to an entire area: their sports equipment, their room, their homework or their own schedule. There is a fuller picture of that in age-appropriate responsibilities.

The important thing is not the age at which a particular job appears on a list.

It is that responsibility keeps moving towards the child.

You are not trying to create children who never need help. You are trying to make sure that help is actually help, rather than a permanent system in which an adult has to notice everything first.

The real goal

A chore gets something done.

A responsibility changes who has to think about it.

That is why the distinction matters. If your child empties the dishwasher because you ask, you have a child who can empty a dishwasher. If they know it is theirs, notice when it needs doing and take care of it without you, you have taught them something that will be useful long after the dishwasher has stopped being your problem.

The goal is not for children to do more chores.

It is for them to gradually own more of their lives.

What to try this week
  1. List everything your children do around the house. Mark each C or R using the “if I said nothing” test.
  2. Convert one C into an R this week using the four moves.
  3. Notice how many of your daily sentences are reminders. That number is your real workload.

Common questions

Should young children have responsibilities or just chores?

Both, sized down. A four-year-old can genuinely own "shoes on the shelf" — one object, one place, visible, with a natural consequence. Anything with multiple steps at that age is realistically a chore, and that is fine.
