Laundry: what children can do, and when

Handing over the washing is the single biggest transfer most families make. Here is the order to do it in.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 July 2026 Updated 6 August 2026 3 min read Ages 3–16
The short answer

Children can put clothes in a basket from three, carry it from six, sort from eight, run a machine from ten and manage the whole cycle including putting it away from twelve. The two rules that matter: a named laundry day per person, and nothing gets washed unless it is in the basket.

Laundry is the highest-volume recurring job in most family homes, and the one that comes back soonest.

It is also the easiest to hand over, because the machine does the hard part.

The stages

By age
3–5Clothes into the basket. Nothing else. That is the whole job, and it takes three years to become automatic.
6–7Carrying their own basket to the machine. Putting their own folded clothes into drawers.
8–9Sorting into darks and lights. Loading the machine. Hanging things up.
10–11Running a wash start to finish with supervision: powder, programme, on.
12–13The whole cycle on a named day — wash, dry, fold, put away. On their own.
14+Their own washing entirely, plus knowing what needs washing before it becomes a crisis.

The two rules

A named day per person.

“Every seven days” means nothing to a child. They have no way of working out when that is.

“Tuesday is your washing day” is something they can check. Give everybody a different day and the machine is never contested.

If it is not in the basket, it does not get washed.

Not as a punishment. It is simply what is true.

The alternative is an adult combing bedroom floors, which is the largest hidden part of household laundry and appears on no chore list anywhere.

Say it once. Then let it be true.

A teenager with no clean football kit on Saturday morning has learned something that no amount of asking was ever going to teach.

Try this

Add “or any time your basket is full” to each person’s day.

Some weeks generate far more washing than others. A rule with no flexibility in it gets broken once, and then abandoned.

Putting it away is the job

Washing, drying and folding are the easy parts.

The pile on the chair is where family laundry systems actually fail.

So make “in the drawer” the definition of done, rather than “clean”.

A basket of clean clothes at the end of a bed is an unfinished job that everybody in the house has quietly agreed to stop seeing.

Making it possible

One basket per person, in their room. Not one communal heap.

Fewer clothes. A child with forty tops generates forty tops of washing and has nowhere to put them. This is the single most effective change available, and the least popular.

Drawers, not hangers, for anyone under about twelve. Hangers need two hands, a decision, and height.

Label the drawers for the younger ones, with pictures. It halves the “where does this go” question.

The bed linen question

Children can strip their own bed from about six, and remake it from about eight.

Badly, at first. Badly is fine.

Give it a fixed day — most families use a weekend morning — and it stops being an event and becomes a routine.

It is one of the easiest wins in the whole house.

What to try this week
  1. Give every person a named washing day, and say it out loud.
  2. Put a basket in each bedroom.
  3. State the basket rule once, then hold it.
  4. Redefine “done” as in the drawer, not clean.

Common questions

At what age can a child use the washing machine alone?

Most children can run a machine with supervision from about ten and alone from twelve, depending on the machine and the child. Do the first few together, write the programme on a card stuck to the machine, and accept that something pink will happen eventually.
