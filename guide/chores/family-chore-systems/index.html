Family chore systems that survive a busy term

Every system works in week one. The question is which one is still running in November.

The Dayly Editors Published 5 April 2026 Updated 6 August 2026 3 min read
The short answer

Ownership-based systems — a named person for each job or area, rotated only at term boundaries — survive longest, because they build habits and require no weekly negotiation. Rotas fail on the admin they generate; points systems fail when the novelty goes.

Most chore systems collapse in the same place.

The week when everything is busy and nobody has the energy to run the system.

What survives is whatever needed no administration in the first place.

The four systems

1. The rota

Jobs rotate weekly or daily between the children.

The argument for it: it feels fair, and it spreads the unpleasant jobs around.

Why it fails: somebody has to run it. Everybody has to check it. No habits form. And every handover is a fresh opportunity for a dispute.

The admin lands on a parent — which is the thing you were trying to reduce.

2. Ownership

Each child owns specific jobs, indefinitely. Rotate at the end of a term, if at all.

The argument for it: habits form. Nobody has to check whose turn it is. Standards improve, because the same person keeps doing it.

Why it fails: it can look unfair if the jobs are badly matched. Fix that when you allocate them, not every week.

This is the one that survives.

3. Areas

Each person owns a room or a zone — everything in it — rather than a list of individual tasks.

The argument for it: it teaches noticing, which individual tasks never do. “The hallway is yours” includes things nobody wrote down.

Why it fails: it is too abstract for under-nines. And it needs an agreed standard, or it turns into a disagreement about what “done” means.

Best used alongside ownership. Two named daily jobs, plus one area.

4. Points

Jobs earn points. Points buy something.

The argument for it: excellent for a few weeks, and genuinely useful for optional extra work.

Why it fails: the novelty fades. Everything becomes negotiable. And children start asking what a job pays before agreeing to do it.

Keep points for genuinely extra work — taking on somebody else’s job, doing something nobody asked for. Not for the basics.

The combination that works

For most families with children over six.

  1. Two daily jobs per child, owned, attached to a trigger. After dinner. When you come down.
  2. One area each, with a written standard, reset weekly on a named day.
  3. One family reset. Fifteen minutes, everybody, same time each week.
  4. Points only for extra, if you use them at all.
  5. Reallocate once a term. Not weekly.

How to make it survive a bad week

Have a minimum. Name the two or three things that happen no matter what. Everything else can lapse in an exam week without the whole thing collapsing.

Do not restart from scratch. The urge after a bad fortnight is to redesign it.

Resist that. A mediocre system running continuously beats a perfect one relaunched three times a year.

Review at term boundaries. They are natural, they are infrequent, and everybody expects things to change then anyway.

What to try this week
  1. Write your system on one sheet of A4. If it does not fit, simplify it.
  2. Convert any weekly rota into named ownership.
  3. Name the minimum that happens even in a terrible week.
  4. Put the next review in the calendar at the start of next term.
