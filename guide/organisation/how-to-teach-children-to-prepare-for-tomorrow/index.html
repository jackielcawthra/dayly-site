How to teach children to prepare for tomorrow

Five minutes in the evening buys twenty in the morning, and it moves the noticing to the child.

The Dayly Editors Published 16 April 2026 Updated 6 August 2026 3 min read Ages 5–16
The short answer

Build a fixed five-minute check into the evening: look at tomorrow, pack the bag, put out the clothes, deal with anything that needs an adult. Do it at the same trigger point every night, keep it short, and let the child run it as soon as they can.

Morning problems are almost always evening problems that were not noticed in time. The kit was in the wash. The form needed signing. The costume was at the bottom of a bag. All of it was solvable at seven o’clock the night before and none of it is solvable at ten to eight.

The check

Five minutes, same trigger every night — after dinner, or straight after teeth. Four questions:

  1. What’s on tomorrow? School, clubs, anything different.
  2. What do I need for it? Kit, instrument, book, money, form.
  3. Is it here, clean, and in the bag? Not “do I own it”. Is it in the bag.
  4. Does anyone need to know anything? A form to sign, a lift to arrange, something to buy.

That fourth question is the one that saves parents most, and children skip it unless it is written down. It is the moment a problem still has twelve hours to be solved in.

Try this

Do the check standing next to the bag, not sitting on a sofa. Proximity to the object matters more than you would expect — the bag prompts the memory.

What “prepared” looks like by age

By age
5–7Clothes out, bag by the door, an adult runs the check aloud.
8–10The child runs the check with a written list; an adult is present but says nothing.
11–13The child runs it alone. The adult finds out about the form because the child mentions it.
14+The check is invisible and internal, and includes things a week away.

Making it stick

Attach it to something that already happens. After dinner. After the plates are cleared. Never “before bed”, which is a moving target in every household.

Keep it to five minutes. A twenty-minute evening routine gets abandoned in the first busy week. Short survives.

Do it even on easy days. Habits form from consistency, not necessity. A Friday check that takes ninety seconds is what makes the Wednesday check automatic.

Let them find the problem. The value is not the packed bag; it is that the child discovers the gap themselves and has time to solve it. If you spot it first and announce it, you have done the check for them.

The one-week test

After a fortnight, try this: say nothing at all on a Sunday evening and see whether Monday morning happens. Whatever they miss tells you exactly what still needs to be on the list — and whatever they get right, they now own.

What to try this week
  1. Pick the trigger — the specific thing that happens right before the check — and use it every night.
  2. Write the four questions on a card and keep it where the bag lives.
  3. Run it aloud for a week, then hand it over and stand nearby saying nothing.
  4. Do it on the easy nights too.

Common questions

What if evenings are already chaotic?

Then start with the bag alone — one question, not four — and add the rest once that is automatic. A five-minute routine that never happens is worth less than a thirty-second one that always does.
