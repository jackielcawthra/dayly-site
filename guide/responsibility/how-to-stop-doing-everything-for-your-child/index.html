How to stop doing everything for your child

Doing it yourself seems easier than letting a child struggle with it. The trouble is that the child gets very good at being helped.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 April 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 5 min read Ages 4–17
The short answer

Hand things over in stages — together, then nearby, then checked afterwards, then not checked at all. Let the small consequences land without adding a lecture. Make succeeding easy, because good systems do not replace responsibility, they make it possible. And on the days when you simply do it yourself, take it back tomorrow.

There is a particular kind of parenting that looks like help but slowly becomes a habit.

You find the missing shoe because you know exactly where it probably is. You pack the swimming bag because you can do it in two minutes. You fill in the form because it needs to go back today. You remind them about homework because you know what happens if they forget.

Most of the time, there is a perfectly good reason. You are in a hurry. They are tired. You know how to do it. Teaching them would take longer.

And that is how it happens. Not through one big decision, but through hundreds of small ones in which doing something yourself seems easier than letting a child struggle with it.

The trouble is that the child gets very good at being helped.

The quicker way is not always the easier way

A child who never has to remember their swimming kit does not get much opportunity to learn how to remember it.

A child whose bag is always packed for them does not discover what needs to go in it. A child whose clothes are always found for them does not have to develop a system for keeping track of them.

This is not because children are incapable. It is because capability needs somewhere to develop.

Parents often know this in theory and find it much harder in practice. The ten minutes it takes to teach a child how to do something can feel impossible when you are already late for school. So you do it yourself, and the morning goes smoothly.

Then the same thing happens tomorrow.

Responsibility has to move gradually

There is no need to suddenly stop helping and announce that a child is now responsible for everything.

A better handover usually happens in stages. You might do something together first, then let the child do it while you are nearby. Later, you can let them do it without you and check afterwards. Eventually, the checking can disappear too.

The pace will depend on the child and the responsibility. A six-year-old may need much more support than a twelve-year-old, and a child who is learning something new may temporarily need more help again.

The important thing is that the responsibility is moving.

If you are still noticing, reminding, checking and fixing everything, the child may be doing the physical task but you are still carrying the responsibility for it.

Let them experience some small failures

This is usually the hardest part.

A child forgets their kit. They are disappointed. Your instinct is to drive back home and get it.

Sometimes you should. Safety matters, as do situations where someone else would be seriously affected. But there are also plenty of small consequences that are uncomfortable without being harmful.

The forgotten kit means sitting out that activity. The homework is late and the teacher deals with it. The child leaves their water bottle at school and has to ask lost property.

You do not need to add a lecture. The experience is enough.

A calm “That’s frustrating. What could help you remember next time?” gives the child something to think about without turning the mistake into a judgement about them.

The aim is not to let children fail for the sake of teaching them a lesson. It is to stop removing every opportunity they have to learn one.

Make it easier to succeed

Letting go does not mean making things unnecessarily difficult.

If the child needs to remember five things every morning, give them somewhere visible to keep the list. If school equipment has a particular place, make that place easy to use. If tomorrow is different from an ordinary day, make sure the child can see that before the morning arrives.

Good systems are not a substitute for responsibility. They make responsibility possible.

They also help parents step back without feeling that they have simply abandoned the child to remember everything.

You are allowed to do it for them sometimes

There will be days when you are working late, the baby is awake, someone is ill and the bus leaves in six minutes.

Do it yourself.

Independence is not a performance, and a child does not need to practise responsibility at every possible moment. Family life involves helping one another, sometimes because somebody genuinely needs help and sometimes because today is simply not the day to practise.

The important thing is what happens over time.

If “I’ll do it this time” becomes the permanent arrangement, the child never gets the responsibility back. If it really is just this time, you can return it tomorrow.

The goal is not to need less help from you tomorrow

The deeper purpose is something slightly different.

Children eventually need to be able to run parts of their own lives without a parent standing behind them. They need to know what belongs to them, notice when something needs doing, work out what to do and deal with the ordinary consequences when they forget.

That does not happen because we tell children to be independent.

It happens because, little by little, we give them something real to own.

And sometimes that means accepting that the slower way today is the way that makes tomorrow easier.

What to try this week
  1. Name the stage you are actually at for one task, honestly.
  2. Move up exactly one stage.
  3. Shift one morning check to the night before.
  4. Choose one small consequence you will let land this week, and decide in advance not to rescue it.

Common questions

My child gets very upset when they forget things. Is this cruel?

Distress is not the same as harm, but it is a signal worth reading. If a child is genuinely anxious rather than annoyed, go back a stage and build more visible structure — a list, a night-before check — rather than removing the responsibility. The aim is a child who feels capable, and capability comes from succeeding at slightly hard things, not from failing at very hard ones.

What if my partner keeps rescuing?

Extremely common, and it undoes the whole thing quietly. Agree the specific task and the specific consequence in advance — not the general principle, which everybody agrees with and nobody applies at 07:48.
