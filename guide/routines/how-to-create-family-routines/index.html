Routines

How to create family routines that hold

A routine is not about time. It is about knowing what happens next — which removes a set of decisions that would otherwise be made again every day.

The Dayly Editors Published 12 January 2026 Updated 18 August 2026 9 min read Ages all
The short answer

A routine works when it starts with the part of the day that keeps going wrong, follows something that already happens rather than a clock, stays short enough to live with, and is visible to the child rather than held by a parent. It also needs a version for difficult days, and reviewing when it keeps failing — because a routine cannot make an overloaded life manageable.

A family routine is not really about time. It is about knowing what happens next. A schedule might tell you that homework starts at 17:00, while a routine gives the evening some shape: after dinner, the table is cleared, tomorrow is checked, bags are packed and everyone knows what comes after that. The difference is small, but it matters. A routine removes a series of decisions that would otherwise have to be made again every day, and that makes family life easier for both children and adults.

This is also why routines can reduce mental load. Anything that happens in a predictable way is one less thing for somebody to remember, initiate and supervise. The aim is not to make family life run like a timetable. It is to make some parts of it familiar enough that nobody has to keep working out what happens next.

The short version

A family routine works best when it:

  • Starts with a real problem — choose the part of family life that repeatedly needs reminders
  • Follows something that already happens — after dinner, after school, before bed
  • Stays simple — enough structure to help, not so much that the routine becomes work
  • Can be seen by everyone — children should not need to ask a parent what comes next
  • Is built with the children — they are more likely to use something they understand and have helped shape
  • Moves responsibility gradually — remind less, point to the system more, and let children experience small consequences
  • Has a difficult-day version — a routine needs to survive real family life
  • Gets reviewed when it keeps failing — sometimes the problem is not the routine; there is simply too much going on

Start with the part of the day that keeps going wrong

You do not need a routine for everything, and trying to create one for the whole household at once usually produces something far too complicated to last. Instead, look at the part of the day that repeatedly requires the same reminders or creates the same arguments. Perhaps mornings are chaotic because nobody knows where their things are, homework becomes a negotiation every afternoon, or bedtime keeps drifting later because nobody knows when the evening has actually started.

Listen to the things you find yourself saying over and over: Have you packed your bag? Where are your shoes? Did you check what you need tomorrow? Are you ready yet? Those repeated instructions are useful clues. They tell you where a routine could take over some of the work that currently depends on a parent remembering to say it.

Use what already happens

Clock times can make a routine look organised, but they still require somebody to watch the clock. “Start getting ready at 19:00” means someone has to notice that it is 19:00 and then tell everyone what to do. A trigger that is already part of the day is often easier to follow: after dinner, check tomorrow; when you come home, unpack your bag; before screens, homework is finished; after brushing your teeth, clothes go away and the school bag goes by the door.

The existing activity becomes the reminder for what comes next. This is particularly helpful for children because they are not being asked to remember a disconnected instruction. They are learning to recognise a familiar point in the day and the sequence that follows it. With enough repetition, the routine begins to require less conscious effort.

Keep it short enough to live with

A routine does not need to contain everything that happens during the day. Five or six meaningful steps are often more useful than a long list that accounts for every possible detail.

A morning routine might be getting dressed, eating breakfast, brushing teeth, checking the bag and leaving. An evening routine might include checking tomorrow, packing the bag, putting out clothes and getting ready for bed. The exact steps will depend on the family, but the principle is the same: include the things that genuinely make the next part of the day easier and leave the rest alone.

There is always a temptation to add more. When you are making a routine, everything feels important. But once the list becomes long enough to feel like another task, it stops reducing the work. A useful routine should carry some of the family’s organisation, not become another thing the family has to organise.

Put it somewhere the child can use it

A routine that exists only in a parent’s head is still a parent’s responsibility. Putting it somewhere visible changes that. The morning routine might belong by the door, the homework routine where homework happens and the bedtime routine in the bedroom. Younger children may find pictures easier to follow than words, while older children will often prefer something simple and written.

The important thing is that the child can look at it without having to ask you. That is where the gradual handover begins. At first, you may need to remind them. Then you can point them towards the routine instead of explaining what needs to happen. Later, you might ask, “What comes next?” and let them find the answer themselves.

That small change is more important than it looks. If the child always comes to you for the answer, you remain the organisational system even if the routine is written down.

Make it with the children

Children are more likely to use a routine that makes sense to them, so there is value in building it together. Work out what actually needs to happen, let them choose the order where it does not matter, and use language they recognise. “Bag by the door” may be far more useful to a child than “prepare belongings for tomorrow”.

It may not look exactly the way you would have designed it. That is part of the point. The purpose is not to create the most efficient system possible; it is to create something the people using it can eventually manage themselves.

Once you have made it, give it some time before changing it. Children need repetition before a sequence becomes familiar, and adults can be surprisingly quick to decide that a routine is not working when it has simply not had enough time to settle.

Let responsibility move gradually

There is a difference between a child completing a routine and a child owning it. If you are still checking every step, reminding them when they forget and correcting the order in which they do things, the routine may have changed but the responsibility has not.

The handover usually happens in stages. You remind them less, point them back to the system more, and gradually allow them to notice what they have missed. The child will sometimes forget something, take much longer than you would have done, or do it in an order that seems completely unnecessary to you. That is part of learning.

The goal is not to make the child dependent on a perfectly managed routine. It is to give them enough structure that they can increasingly manage without you.

Give the routine a version for difficult days

Every family has days when the normal routine is too much. Someone is ill, work runs late, there is an unexpected appointment, three children have activities at the same time, or everybody is simply exhausted.

It helps to decide in advance what the minimum version looks like. Perhaps the full evening routine involves checking tomorrow, packing bags, putting out clothes, tidying the kitchen and getting ready for bed, while the difficult-day version is simply checking tomorrow and packing the bags. That does not mean the routine has failed. It means the family has a way of getting through a difficult evening without abandoning the whole system.

The important thing is to return to the normal routine afterwards rather than treating one disrupted day as evidence that the routine does not work.

Sometimes the routine is not the problem

A routine can make a manageable life easier, but it cannot make an overloaded life manageable. If bedtime is consistently an hour too late, there may simply be too much happening in the evening. If the morning routine needs twelve steps, perhaps there are too many things to get through before school. If nobody ever manages the weekly family check-in, Sunday may already be too full.

This is worth paying attention to because otherwise every failed routine becomes another reason to think that someone in the family needs to try harder. Sometimes the answer is not a better system. Sometimes something needs to come out.

The point is not to run the family more efficiently

The best routines eventually become almost invisible. The children know what happens after dinner, where their things belong and what they need to check before tomorrow. You no longer have to announce every transition because the routine itself provides enough information for everyone to move through it.

That is the real value of a family routine. It does not make the family perfectly organised, and it certainly does not remove everything that needs thinking about. What it can do is move some of that thinking out of one person’s head and into something the whole family can see, understand and use.

Over time, the children carry more of it themselves. The adults have less to remember on their behalf. And the family spends a little less time negotiating what happens next and a little more time simply getting on with the life they are trying to organise.

What to try this week
  1. Pick the worst part of the day and build one routine for it.
  2. Write it with the children, in their words, six steps maximum.
  3. Put it where it happens, and point at it instead of speaking.
  4. Name the two-item minimum version for bad days.

Common questions

How long does a routine take to stick?

Longer than the "21 days" figure suggests — habit formation research on everyday behaviours found a wide range, with a median of about two months and considerable variation. Expect six to eight weeks before it feels automatic, and do not judge it at three.

Do routines work for children who resist structure?

Often better than expected, provided they helped write it and it is genuinely short. What children resist is usually being told what to do in the moment — a written routine is impersonal, and that is exactly why it lowers the conflict.
