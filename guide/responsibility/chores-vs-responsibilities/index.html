Chores vs responsibilities: why the difference matters

Both are useful. Only one of them ends with you not having to be there.

The Dayly Editors Published 1 March 2026 Updated 22 July 2026 3 min read
The short answer

A chore is assigned and prompted; a responsibility is owned, including the noticing. Children need both, but a household built entirely on chores keeps a parent permanently in the role of manager, because every task requires an instruction to start.

Two households, same amount of work done, completely different outcomes five years later.

In the first, a parent says “can you empty the dishwasher” and it gets emptied. In the second, the dishwasher is Noah’s, on the days he is home, and he does it without being asked because it is his.

The labour is identical. The difference is who is holding the noticing.

ChoreResponsibility
Who notices it is dueThe parentThe child
How it startsAn instructionA routine, a list, or the thing itself
What happens if it is forgottenReminderConsequence
What the child practisesCompliancePlanning, memory, follow-through
What the parent ends up doingManagingNothing

Chores are not the enemy

They are a reasonable starting point, especially for young children, and there is nothing wrong with a household that runs on some of both. Younger children genuinely cannot hold much and need the prompt. The problem is only when everything stays a chore into a child’s teens — because then the parent is still the operating system, and the child has years of practice at waiting to be told.

How to convert a chore into a responsibility

Four moves, in this order.

1. Attach it to a trigger, not a person. “Empty the dishwasher when you come down” beats “empty the dishwasher when I ask”. The trigger has to be something in the world, not your voice.

2. Make it visible. Where can they check without asking? A list, a routine card, a shared calendar. Anything that means the information does not live in your head.

3. Name the standard once. “Done” means the machine is empty and the cupboards are shut. Say it once, at the start. Do not re-specify it weekly — that is management.

4. Stop reminding, and mean it. This is the whole thing. Until reminders stop, it is still a chore with better branding.

Try this

Pick your most-nagged chore this week. Convert it: attach a trigger, write it where they can see it, agree what done means, and say out loud that you will not be mentioning it again. Then do not mention it, including with a sigh.

The tell

Ask yourself: if I said nothing at all for a fortnight, what would happen?

Whatever survives is a responsibility. Whatever collapses is a chore — which is fine, as long as you know which is which, and as long as the list of things that would collapse is getting shorter every year.

What to try this week
  1. List everything your children do around the house. Mark each C or R using the “if I said nothing” test.
  2. Convert one C into an R this week using the four moves.
  3. Notice how many of your daily sentences are reminders. That number is your real workload.

Common questions

Should young children have responsibilities or just chores?

Both, sized down. A four-year-old can genuinely own "shoes on the shelf" — one object, one place, visible, with a natural consequence. Anything with multiple steps at that age is realistically a chore, and that is fine.
