How to help children build good study habits

Rereading a chapter can make it feel familiar. That feeling is easy to mistake for knowing it.

The Dayly Editors Published 18 August 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 10–18
The short answer

The two techniques with the best evidence are closed-book retrieval and spreading work across days; the two most used are rereading and highlighting. Build a fixed time, make the first step small, teach the child to find their own gaps, and move the phone. And know the limit — a teenager who is exhausted or anxious needs a different conversation, not a better technique.

Good study habits are much easier to build before a child desperately needs them.

By the time exams become important, it can be difficult to introduce a completely new way of studying. A child has already found the methods that feel familiar, even if those methods are not particularly effective.

The good news is that the most useful study habits are not especially complicated. The strongest evidence is for retrieval practice — trying to remember something without looking at the answer — and spaced practice, where learning is spread over time rather than concentrated into one long session. Rereading and highlighting are much less effective, even though they often feel like productive studying (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

The aim, then, is not to make children study more.

It is to help them learn how to study.

Start with how learning actually works

Rereading a chapter can make it feel familiar. That feeling is easy to mistake for knowing it.

A better test is to close the book and try to remember what was there.

What were the main ideas? Can you explain them without looking? Can you answer a few questions? Can you work through a problem from memory?

This is retrieval practice. It is harder than rereading, which is partly why children may not choose it themselves. But the effort involved is part of what makes it useful.

Spreading practice out matters too. Three short sessions across a week are generally more useful for long-term learning than doing the same amount of work in one sitting.

These two ideas are worth understanding before adding much else.

Make studying a normal part of the week

A fixed time and place can remove some of the negotiation around studying.

This does not need to mean an hour at a desk every evening. A younger child might have a short reading or practice period after school. An older child might have a regular study slot on certain days.

The useful part is that studying becomes something that happens, rather than something that has to be decided from scratch every time.

Starting early also means the habit is established before the stakes become high.

Make starting easier

For many children, the hardest part is beginning.

A large assignment can feel vague and unpleasant. “Study science” is not a very useful instruction. “Spend five minutes answering questions about the last lesson” is much easier to start.

A deliberately small first step can help. Five minutes of retrieval is enough to get started, and often the work continues once the initial resistance has passed.

This is also where breaking a larger task into smaller steps becomes useful. A child does not need to feel motivated to complete an entire project before they begin. They need to know what the next step is.

Teach children to find what they do not know

One of the most useful things a child can learn is that studying is not simply about looking at information again.

It is about finding the gaps.

If they close the book and cannot explain something, that is useful information. If they get a question wrong, they have found something that needs more work.

This is one reason retrieval practice is so valuable. It does not just strengthen memory; it shows the student where their memory is weak.

A child who can say, “I understand this part, but I don’t know how to do this one” is already developing a much more useful study skill than a child who simply spends another hour rereading everything.

Put the phone somewhere else

For teenagers especially, the environment matters.

A phone within reach makes it very easy for studying to become interrupted by messages, videos or the simple habit of checking what is happening.

The answer does not have to be an elaborate family rule. During a defined study period, the phone can simply be somewhere else.

The point is to make the environment support the behaviour you are asking for rather than expecting a teenager to exercise perfect self-control every few minutes.

Plan backwards from deadlines

Children often struggle with long-term work because the deadline feels distant until suddenly it is not.

Planning backwards is a skill worth teaching.

If a project is due on Friday, what needs to happen by Thursday? What needs to happen before that? How much time does each part actually require?

This becomes increasingly important as children move into secondary school, when several subjects can have deadlines at the same time.

A simple weekly planning session can help: look at what is coming up, decide when it will happen and identify anything that needs to be started early.

Over time, the child should increasingly do this themselves.

What parents can stop doing

It is easy to become the manager of a child’s studying.

You remind them when to start, tell them what to revise, sit beside them, check whether they have finished and ask whether they have remembered everything.

Some of that may be necessary when children are young.

But the long-term goal is different.

You want the child to know when they need to study, what they need to learn, how to practise it and when they need help.

That takes time. It also means allowing some responsibility to move across even when the child’s system is not exactly the one you would have chosen.

There is a limit to what study habits can fix

Study techniques are useful when the problem is how a child studies.

They cannot solve every reason a child might be struggling.

A teenager who is exhausted, anxious, completely disengaged from school or worried about something outside their schoolwork may not need a better revision technique. They may need a different conversation.

That distinction matters because pushing harder is not always the answer.

Sometimes the most useful question is simply: ”What is making this difficult at the moment?”

A good place to start

You do not need to change everything at once.

Try replacing one session of rereading with a few minutes of closed-book retrieval. Spread one topic across several shorter sessions rather than leaving it until the night before. Move the phone out of the room for a defined period.

Then leave some space for the child to notice the difference themselves.

The goal is not a child who studies exactly the way a parent thinks they should.

It is a child who gradually understands how their own learning works, can tell what they know and do not know, and has a few reliable ways of doing something about it.

Those habits become much more valuable as the child gets older and the parent becomes less involved.

What to try this week
  1. Replace one rereading session with closed-book retrieval.
  2. Spread one topic across three short sessions instead of one long one.
  3. Move the phone to another room during study.
  4. Show them the research ranking, and let it make the argument.

Sources & further reading

  1. Dunlosky, J. et al. (2013). Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques
  2. The Learning Scientists — free evidence-based study materials for students and parents
