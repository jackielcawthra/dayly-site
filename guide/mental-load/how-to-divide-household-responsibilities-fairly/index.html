How to divide household responsibilities fairly

Fifty-fifty on every line sounds equitable and works badly. Here is a division that survives a bad week.

The Dayly Editors Published 15 April 2026 Updated 1 August 2026 4 min read
The short answer

Divide by whole areas rather than tasks, allocate first by appetite, then by capacity, then by fairness, and review once a term rather than weekly. A fair split is one where each person can name their areas without checking, and neither has to be asked.

Most attempts at fairness fail on arithmetic.

The couple counts hours. The numbers come out close. And neither of them can work out why one of them still feels swamped.

The hours were never the problem.

Fair means unambiguous, not identical

Three things matter more than an even split.

Ownership is unambiguous. Every area has exactly one name against it. Shared areas are where things get dropped, because “we both do the bins” means nobody is actually noticing the bins.

The noticing is included. If one person still has to raise it, they still own it.

The load is felt, not counted. Ten minutes of admin with a deadline attached costs more than half an hour of cooking with a podcast on. Weight it by how much of your attention it takes, not by the clock.

The order to allocate in

Appetite first. Genuine preferences are free efficiency. If somebody likes cooking and does not mind the shop, give them the whole food area.

Capacity second. Anything that has to happen inside school hours goes to whoever’s day bends. That is not a judgement about whose job matters. It is a timetable.

Fairness third. Split what is left evenly.

Then look at the residue — bins, admin, appointments, forms — and check it is not all sitting in one column.

It usually is.

Worth knowing

Watch that residue.

Allocating by appetite tends to leave one person with everything nobody wanted.

Deal with that column on its own. Split it, alternate it by term, or agree it is worth paying somebody else to do.

The column that matters is the third one.

If you cannot fill it in for an area, that area is not really owned.

How to review it without a row

Set a date. The start of each school term is a natural one.

Then ask two questions.

Which area did you most resent this term?

Not which was hardest. Resentment is the reliable signal, and it usually points at a bad fit rather than a bad person.

What did we both forget?

Anything neither of you noticed is an area with no name against it. Give it one.

And do not do this weekly. It sounds diligent and it undermines the whole thing — areas need long enough to become automatic, and renegotiating every Sunday keeps everybody in management.

The children’s column

A fair division of a household includes the people who live in it.

Here is a useful test. If a visitor watched your family for a week, could they tell which jobs belonged to the children?

If every task quietly reverts to an adult the moment a child is slow, that child does not have a responsibility.

They have a suggestion.

What to try this week
  1. Write the area table, with the third column — what the owner notices — filled in for every line.
  2. Find the residue column and split it deliberately rather than by default.
  3. Put one name against every area. Delete the word “shared” wherever you can.
  4. Book the review for the start of next term, and nothing before it.

Common questions

What if one of us works far longer hours?

Allocate weekday-bound areas by capacity and rebalance with areas that can be done at any hour — money, holidays, admin, planning. What does not work is the person with fewer hours also carrying all the noticing, because noticing runs at all hours regardless of the job.

Should children’s responsibilities count in the split?

Yes, as their own column. They should not be counted as one parent's contribution — that is how "getting the children to do their jobs" becomes a job of its own, held by whoever assigned it.
