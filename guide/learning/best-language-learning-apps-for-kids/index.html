The best language learning apps for children

Apps are excellent at vocabulary and daily habit. They are poor at conversation, which is the point of a language.

The Dayly Editors Published 6 July 2026 Updated 15 August 2026 3 min read Ages 5–17
The short answer

Language apps build vocabulary and a daily habit effectively, and are weak at speaking and listening to real people. Use one for ten minutes a day alongside something involving actual conversation — a class, a relative, a tutor — rather than as the whole method.

What apps do well

Vocabulary, through spaced repetition. This is the strongest case: showing a word again just before it is forgotten is exactly what software is good at, and spacing is one of the better-supported findings in learning research.

A daily habit. Five to ten minutes a day is genuinely valuable for a language, and an app makes that easy to sustain.

Removing embarrassment. Some children will speak to a phone long before they will speak in front of a class.

What apps do badly

Real conversation. Understanding a person speaking at normal speed, with an accent, who says something you were not expecting, is the actual skill — and it is the one apps most struggle with.

Production under pressure. Choosing from four options is not the same as producing a sentence.

Motivation past the beginner stage. Most app users plateau somewhere in early-intermediate, which is where languages become genuinely useful and also genuinely hard.

Worth knowing

It is worth being honest with a child about this: an app will not make them able to speak a language. It will give them vocabulary and a habit, which makes everything else easier. Framed that way, the plateau is expected rather than a failure.

What to look for

Spaced repetition, ideally explicitly. It is the mechanism that makes vocabulary stick.

Audio by actual speakers, and plenty of listening.

Speaking practice, even if the recognition is imperfect. Producing sound matters.

Short daily sessions rather than long ones.

Content that suits the age. An app designed for adult holidaymakers will bore an eight-year-old inside a week.

What to avoid

Streaks that punish. The single most common reason children quit — a lost streak after a holiday frequently ends the habit entirely.

Translation-only exercises. Matching English to another language teaches translation, not the language.

Gamification that outweighs the learning. If they can win without learning, they will.

How to actually get a child speaking

The app is one part of three:

  1. Daily input. Ten minutes on an app, or a book, or a programme in the language.
  2. Real listening. Cartoons dubbed into the language, songs, radio. Comprehensible input matters
  3. enormously and it is free.
  4. Someone to speak to. A class, a relative, a conversation exchange, a tutor. This is the part
  5. that cannot be replaced, and it is the part most families skip.
Try this

Switch a programme your child already watches into the target language, with subtitles in that language rather than in English. Twenty minutes a day of comprehensible input does more than most app sessions.

What to try this week
  1. Cap app time at ten minutes daily and add one source of real listening.
  2. Find one person the child can actually speak to, however briefly.
  3. Turn off streak notifications if a lost streak would end the habit.
  4. Switch one familiar programme into the target language.
