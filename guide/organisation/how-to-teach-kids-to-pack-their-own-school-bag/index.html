How to teach your child to pack their own school bag

The change that matters is not the list. It is the time of day you do it.

The Dayly Editors Published 26 February 2026 Updated 6 August 2026 4 min read Ages 5–13
The short answer

Pack the bag the night before, against a short written list your child helped write, and hand it over in four stages over about a month: together, then watched, then checked afterwards, then not checked. Kit days should live on the list, not in a parent’s memory.

The morning is the wrong time.

That is the whole article, really. But the details matter.

Why the night before changes everything

A bag packed at 07:40 is packed by whoever is fastest. Which is you.

There is no time to find the missing thing. No time for the child to think. And a real deadline attached to the outcome.

A bag packed at 18:30 has slack in it.

The missing item can be found. The child can be slow. Nobody is standing there holding a car key.

And this is the part that matters: the child is the one who discovers the gap.

The list

Two parts. Children get this immediately.

Every day: water bottle, reading folder, pencil case, lunch or lunch card, homework diary.

Depends on the day: PE kit on Tuesday and Thursday, swimming on Wednesday, library book on Monday, instrument on Friday. Plus forms and money.

Write it together. In their words.

Then stick it where the bag lives. Not in a drawer. Not on a phone they have to unlock.

Where the bag is.

Try this

Ask your child to write the list from memory first. Then correct it together.

What they leave out tells you exactly which items need to be on a list rather than in their head.

The four-week handover

Week 1 — Together. You pack alongside them, thinking out loud. “Wednesday tomorrow. That’s swimming. Costume, towel, goggles.”

Week 2 — They pack, you ask. They do it. You ask questions and do not supply answers. “What’s tomorrow? What does the list say?”

Week 3 — They pack, you check afterwards. No comment unless something is missing. And then a question rather than a fix: “Have another look at Wednesday.”

Week 4 — They pack. You do not check.

This is the week something gets forgotten. It should.

When it goes wrong

Let it.

A session sat out is a very good teacher, and there is nothing you need to add.

“Ah, annoying. What could you do differently on Wednesday?”

Then let them answer.

The workaround they come up with — kit by the door, a check after dinner, a note on the bag — is the actual skill.

It is worth considerably more than the kit.

What trips children up

Things that live somewhere else. The kit in the wash. The boots in the car. The recorder in the kitchen.

Fix the storage, not the child. Everything that goes in the bag should live in one place.

Things that only happen occasionally. Library books, letters, money, forms.

These are the real failures, because there is no routine to attach them to. Put them on a “tomorrow” pile by the bag the moment they appear.

Coming back after the weekend. Sunday evening is a repack, not a continuation. Build it into the Sunday reset.

Ages

By age
5–7You pack together, every time, with a picture list. They put in two or three named items themselves.
8–10They pack, you check afterwards. The list is written and it lives by the bag.
11–13They pack alone. You do not check. Kit days are theirs to know.
14+Bag, kit, deadlines and equipment are entirely theirs — including the ones that only come up twice a term.
What to try this week
  1. Move packing to the night before, every night, without exception.
  2. Write the two-part list with your child and stick it where the bag lives.
  3. Identify which stage you are actually at, and move up one.
  4. Put a “tomorrow” pile by the bag for letters, money and library books.

Common questions

My child says they will do it and then does not.

Attach it to a trigger rather than an intention — straight after dinner, before any screen. "I'll do it later" is not a plan, and it is not defiance either; it is a nine-year-old's honest estimate of their future self.

Should I secretly check?

Occasionally, and without acting on it, is a reasonable transition. Checking every night and fixing what is missing means you are still packing the bag, and the child knows it even if you never mention it.
