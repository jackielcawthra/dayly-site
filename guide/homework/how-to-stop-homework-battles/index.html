How to stop homework becoming a nightly battle

If every evening ends in a row, the homework is no longer the most important thing at stake.

The Dayly Editors Published 8 May 2026 Updated 22 August 2026 6 min read Ages 5–18
The short answer

Find what the fight is really about — not understanding, exhaustion, boredom, being crowded, or control — and change that rather than pushing harder. Shrink the task, stop being the teacher as well as the parent, and tell the school what actually happened. Protect sleep and the relationship.

If every evening ends in a row, the homework is no longer the most important thing at stake.

Homework can be difficult for all sorts of reasons. A child may not understand the work, be exhausted after school, find the task pointless, or simply feel that homework has become the one part of the day where a parent is constantly telling them what to do.

The answer is not always to push harder.

Sometimes the most useful thing is to work out what the argument is actually about, then change the part that is causing it.

The short version

If homework has become a nightly battle:

  1. Work out what is actually causing the conflict.
  2. Make the task smaller for a while.
  3. Stop trying to be both teacher and parent.
  4. Tell the school what is really happening.
  5. Protect sleep and the relationship, particularly when the child is already exhausted.
  6. Give older children more responsibility for solving the problem themselves.

The goal is not to make every piece of homework get done at any cost. It is to find a way of dealing with schoolwork that does not make every evening miserable.

Find out what the fight is actually about

The same argument can look very different from the child’s side.

”I can’t do it.” They may genuinely not understand the work. Ask them to explain the first question rather than immediately giving them the answer. If they cannot get started, that is useful information to take back to the teacher.

”It’s boring.” Sometimes it is. You do not need to convince a child that every worksheet is fascinating. Keep the task contained and move on.

”I’m exhausted.” Some children have very little left by the end of a long school day. They may need a break, a different time or a shorter session.

”You’re sitting on top of me.” Some children work better when a parent is nearby; others work much better when the parent leaves them alone.

”I’m not doing it.” Sometimes the argument is no longer about the homework. It has become a battle over control. Offering a genuine choice about when or where to do the work can give some of that control back without making the parent responsible for the decision.

You do not need to diagnose which one it is perfectly. You just need to stop treating every problem as though the answer is “try harder”.

Make the task smaller

When homework has become a source of conflict, reducing the amount can be more useful than demanding the full task.

Ten minutes. One question. One paragraph.

The point is to get back to a situation where starting does not automatically lead to an argument.

Once the child is managing that consistently, the amount can gradually increase.

This is particularly useful when the problem is avoidance. A task that feels enormous can become much less threatening when the first step is genuinely small.

And if even a small amount regularly ends in distress or conflict, that is information worth sharing with the school.

Stop being the teacher

Parents can accidentally end up playing three roles at once: teacher, enforcer and person whose approval matters most.

That is a difficult combination.

You can provide the conditions for homework without taking responsibility for the work itself. Make sure there is somewhere reasonably quiet to work, help establish a suitable time, provide food and be available if the child genuinely needs help.

But you do not necessarily need to sit beside them correcting every answer or explaining every concept.

If they are stuck on the content, the teacher is often the person who needs to know.

Your job is to support the child without becoming the person who has to make the homework happen.

Tell the school what is really happening

A teacher may see a twenty-minute assignment being returned incomplete. They may not know that it took an hour and a half, ended in tears and involved a parent sitting beside the child throughout.

That information matters.

A short, factual message is usually enough:

“We worked for 30 minutes. They completed questions 1–6 but found the remaining questions difficult, so we stopped.”

That is not making excuses. It gives the teacher information about the gap between what was set and what the child can realistically manage at home.

If the problem is persistent, ask for a proper conversation rather than continuing to fight about it every evening.

Protect sleep

Homework should not routinely turn into a choice between finishing an assignment and getting enough sleep.

This becomes particularly important as children get older and schoolwork increases.

If a teenager is staying up late because they have spent hours avoiding the work, arguing about it or trying to finish everything at once, the problem is probably bigger than that night’s assignment.

Look at the pattern rather than just the deadline in front of you.

Would starting earlier help? Is the workload unrealistic? Is the child struggling with one particular subject? Are they trying to do everything perfectly? Are they simply exhausted?

The answer may have very little to do with motivation.

For older children, change your role

As children move into secondary school, the parent has less ability to manage the work anyway.

That can be useful.

Instead of asking, “Have you done your maths?” ask, “How is maths going?”

Help them plan when they will work, rather than deciding exactly what they should do.

If they are struggling, help them think about who could help and what they could ask.

And make it clear that you are on their side, even when you are not going to do the work for them.

The long-term goal is not a teenager who completes homework because a parent is standing nearby.

It is a teenager who can recognise what needs doing, work out when to do it and ask for help when something is not working.

When homework problems keep happening

If homework is difficult once in a while, that is ordinary.

If there is persistent, intense difficulty with reading, writing, attention, understanding instructions or starting even very small tasks, it is worth talking to the school.

Homework can sometimes be where a difficulty becomes visible first.

That does not mean there is necessarily a problem with the child. It means that repeated struggles are worth investigating rather than treating them as a motivation problem.

The relationship matters too

There will be nights when homework does not get finished.

There will be worksheets that are boring, assignments that are badly timed and evenings when everyone has simply had enough.

That does not mean you have failed as a parent.

Schoolwork matters. But so does a child’s relationship with learning, and so does their relationship with you.

If getting through one piece of homework requires an hour of conflict every night, it is worth stepping back and asking whether the current approach is actually helping.

Sometimes the best way out of a homework battle is not to push harder.

It is to make the problem smaller, give the responsibility back to the right person, and stop making the relationship pay for the homework.

What to try this week
  1. Work out which of the five fights you are actually having.
  2. Cut the task to something too small to argue about, for a fortnight.
  3. Leave the room during homework.
  4. Send the teacher one factual note about what is really happening.
