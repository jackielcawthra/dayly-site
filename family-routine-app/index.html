Why routines fail

They are too long. Anything past five or six steps gets skipped in the first busy week.

They run on clock times. A time requires someone watching a clock, and that someone is you. A trigger — after dinner, when you come down, before screens — happens on its own.

They are all-or-nothing. A routine with no minimum version breaks the first week it cannot run, and a broken routine gets redesigned rather than resumed.

They live in a parent’s voice. If the only way to know what is next is being told, the routine has not been handed over.

How Dayly holds them

Ordered lists, per person. The morning, the evening, homework, Sunday. Each child follows their own, in their own words, at their own height on a shared screen or their own phone.

Triggers rather than times, so nothing depends on someone noticing the clock.

A day that is planned rather than listed. Jobs have a duration and either a time or a window, laid out around the fixed points — waking, the school run, dinner, bedtime. When it does not fit, it says so rather than pretending.

Nothing that breaks. Miss a week and there is nothing to lose. You resume the same routine rather than designing a better one, which is the actual difference between routines that last and routines that get relaunched three times a year.

Common questions

Can we print the routines?

Yes — a picture routine for younger children is often better on a wall at their height than on a screen. Print it and use the app for everything else.

How long does a routine take to stick?

Longer than "21 days" suggests. Research on everyday habits found a median of around two months, with a wide range. Expect six to eight weeks, and do not judge it at three.
